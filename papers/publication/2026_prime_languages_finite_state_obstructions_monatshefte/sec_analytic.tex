\section{Classical Background: Analytic Comparators}
\label{sec:analytic}

This appendix is included as classical background for comparison with
the finite-state obstruction results proved above. The
statements are classical consequences of the P\'olya--Carlson--Estermann
circle of ideas \cite{Polya1923,Carlson1921,Estermann1928} and of the
Artin--Mazur--Bowen--Lanford determinant formula
\cite{ArtinMazur1965,BowenLanford1970}. They do not enter the proof of
the fixed-DFA prime-slice bounds. Their role is to record, in the same
notation, two standard analytic mechanisms by which prime-supported
objects fail to be represented by finite rational or determinant data.

\begin{theorem}[Prime-supported Euler products have a natural boundary]
  \label{thm:euler-product-natural-boundary}
  Let \((b_n)_{n\ge 1}\subseteq\ZZ_{\ge 0}\) and assume that
  \(b_p>0\) for infinitely many primes \(p\). Suppose that
  \[
    Z_b(z):=\prod_{n\ge 1}(1-z^n)^{-b_n}
  \]
  converges to a holomorphic function on \(\abs{z}<1\). Then the unit
  circle is a natural boundary for \(Z_b\).
\leanverified{paper_prime_languages_euler_product_natural_boundary}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  We interpret convergence of the Euler product in the standard sense of
  compact-open convergence: its finite partial products converge locally
  uniformly on \(\abs{z}<1\) to the stated holomorphic function.
  The finite partial products are zero-free on \(\abs{z}<1\), and
  \(Z_b(0)=1\). Hurwitz's theorem therefore implies at once that
  \(Z_b\) is zero-free on \(\abs{z}<1\).
  Since every factor \((1-r^n)^{-b_n}\) is \(>1\) for \(0<r<1\), and
  since \(b_p>0\) for at least one prime \(p\), one has
  \(Z_b(r)\ge(1-r^p)^{-b_p}\to\infty\) as \(r\uparrow 1\). Thus the
  power series of \(Z_b\) has radius of convergence exactly \(1\), and
  all of its Taylor coefficients are non-negative integers.

  For each \(m\), the coefficient of \(z^m\) in \(Z_b(z)\) depends only
  on the finite product \(\prod_{n\le m}(1-z^n)^{-b_n}\). Each such
  finite product has Taylor coefficients in \(\ZZ_{\ge 0}\) (the
  coefficient of \(z^m\) counts partitions of \(m\) into parts of size
  \(\le m\) with multiplicity weights \(b_n\)). Hence all Taylor
  coefficients of \(Z_b\) lie in \(\ZZ_{\ge 0}\).

  Fix \(0<r<1\). Since the Euler product converges at \(r^{1/2}\),
  \[
    \sum_{n\ge 1}b_n\bigl(-\log(1-r^{n/2})\bigr)<\infty.
  \]
  Because \(-\log(1-x)\ge x\) for \(0<x<1\), this implies
  \(\sum_{n\ge 1}b_n r^{n/2}<\infty\). Moreover,
  \(\sup_{n\ge 1}nr^{n/2}<\infty\), so
  \[
    \sum_{n\ge 1}nb_n r^n
    \le\Bigl(\sup_{n\ge 1}nr^{n/2}\Bigr)\sum_{n\ge 1}b_n r^{n/2}
    <\infty.
  \]
  Therefore
  \[
    L(z):=\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{n b_n z^n}{1-z^n}
  \]
  converges absolutely and uniformly on \(\abs{z}\le r\). Let
  \(Z_{b,N}(z):=\prod_{n\le N}(1-z^n)^{-b_n}\). The finite products
  satisfy \(zZ_{b,N}'(z)/Z_{b,N}(z)=\sum_{n\le N}n b_nz^n/(1-z^n)\).
  Since \(Z_{b,N}\to Z_b\) and these logarithmic derivatives converge
  locally uniformly to \(L\), the identities
  \(zZ_{b,N}'=Z_{b,N}\sum_{n\le N}n b_nz^n/(1-z^n)\) pass to the limit
  uniformly on compact subdisks. Hence
  \[
    \frac{zZ_b'(z)}{Z_b(z)}
    =\sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{n b_n z^n}{1-z^n}
    =\sum_{m\ge 1} c_m z^m,
  \]
  where
  \[
    c_m:=\sum_{n\mid m} n b_n\in\ZZ_{\ge 0}.
  \]
  In particular, for every prime \(p\),
  \[
    c_p=c_1+p b_p.
  \]

  Suppose that \(Z_b\) admitted a holomorphic continuation across some
  non-empty open arc \(I\) of \(\abs{z}=1\). We use the
  P\'olya--Carlson theorem in the following standard form
  \cite{Polya1923,Carlson1921}: if \(f(z)=\sum_{m\ge0}a_mz^m\) has
  integer coefficients and radius of convergence exactly \(1\), then
  either \(f\) is rational or the unit circle is a natural boundary for
  \(f\). In particular, the existence of holomorphic continuation
  across even one non-empty boundary arc rules out the natural-boundary
  alternative and forces rationality. The preceding paragraphs show
  that \(Z_b\) has integer Taylor coefficients and radius \(1\); hence
  \(Z_b\) is rational. Since \(Z_b\) is
  zero-free on \(\abs{z}<1\), the logarithmic derivative
  \[
    R(z):=\frac{zZ_b'(z)}{Z_b(z)}=\sum_{m\ge 1} c_m z^m
  \]
  is rational and holomorphic on \(\abs{z}<1\).

  Write the partial-fraction decomposition of \(R\) as
  \[
    R(z)=H(z)+\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{\alpha_j}{1-z/\rho_j},
  \]
  where \(H\) is a polynomial and \(\abs{\rho_j}\ge 1\), since \(R\)
  has no poles in \(\abs{z}<1\). The poles are simple. Indeed, if
  \(Z_b(z)=P(z)/Q(z)\) in lowest terms, then
  \(R(z)=zP'(z)/P(z)-zQ'(z)/Q(z)\); each zero or pole of the rational
  function \(Z_b\), counted with multiplicity, contributes a simple
  logarithmic-derivative pole and no higher-order pole. Hence all
  non-polynomial terms in the partial-fraction expansion have the
  displayed form. Extracting coefficients gives
  \[
    c_m
    =\sum_{\abs{\rho_j}=1}\alpha_j\rho_j^{-m}+O(\theta^m)
  \]
  for some \(\theta\in(0,1)\). In particular, \((c_m)_{m\ge 1}\) is
  bounded, because finitely many unit-modulus exponential terms are
  bounded and the remaining poles have modulus \(>1\).

  Since \(R\) is rational, the sequence \((c_m)\) satisfies a linear
  recurrence after clearing the denominator of \(R\). Because the Taylor
  coefficients \(c_m\) are integers and bounded, the state vectors
  \[
    (c_m,c_{m+1},\dots,c_{m+d-1})
  \]
  of any order-\(d\) recurrence take values in a finite set and evolve
  deterministically. Hence \((c_m)\) is eventually periodic.

  For the infinitely many primes \(p\) with \(b_p>0\),
  \(c_p=c_1+p b_p\ge p\), so the prime-indexed subsequence
  \((c_p)\) is unbounded. This contradicts boundedness, and a fortiori
  eventual periodicity. This contradiction shows
  that \(Z_b\) cannot extend holomorphically across any non-empty arc of
  \(\abs{z}=1\). The unit circle is therefore a natural boundary for
  \(Z_b\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  Theorem \ref{thm:euler-product-natural-boundary} is a
  prime-supported instance of the classical rational-versus-natural
  boundary mechanism for Euler products in the work of P\'olya, Carlson,
  and Estermann \cite{Polya1923,Carlson1921,Estermann1928}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Periodic scalar determinant models after \(z=e^{-s}\)]
  \label{prop:finite-zeta-imaginary-periodicity}
  Let \(M\) be a finite square complex matrix. The scalar determinant
  model
  \[
    F_M(s):=\det(I-(e^{-s})M)^{-1}
  \]
  is \(2\pi i\)-periodic wherever it is holomorphic. Here \(e^{-s}\) is
  a scalar multiplying \(M\), not an exponential of the matrix. Indeed,
  \[
    F_M(s+2\pi i)
    =\det(I-(e^{-(s+2\pi i)})M)^{-1}
    =\det(I-(e^{-s})M)^{-1}
    =F_M(s).
  \]
  In particular, if \(A\) is the adjacency matrix of a shift of finite
  type \(X_A\), then its Artin--Mazur zeta function is
  \[
    \zeta_{X_A}(z)=\det(I-zA)^{-1}.
  \]
  Consequently \(\zeta_{X_A}(e^{-s})=\det(I-(e^{-s})A)^{-1}\) is
  \(2\pi i\)-periodic in \(s\).
  More generally, let
  \[
    F(s)=\sum_{n\ge 1} a_n n^{-s}
  \]
  be a Dirichlet series with abscissa of convergence \(\sigma_c\). Assume
  that there exists at least one index \(n\ge 2\) with \(a_n\neq 0\),
  and let
  \[
    n_1:=\min\{n\ge 2:a_n\neq 0\}.
  \]
  Then \(F\) is not \(2\pi i\)-periodic on any right half-plane. Moreover
  \(F\) cannot agree with \(\zeta_{X_A}(e^{-s})\) on any non-empty open
  subset of \(\Re(s)>\sigma_c\) on which the determinant model is
  holomorphic; equivalently, the two functions do not agree
  meromorphically on any open subset of that half-plane. In particular,
  the Riemann \(\zeta\)-function cannot agree with
  \(\det(I-(e^{-s})A)^{-1}\) on any non-empty open subset of \(\Re(s)>1\)
  where the determinant model is holomorphic.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The determinant formula is classical for shifts of finite type; see
  Artin and Mazur \cite{ArtinMazur1965}, Bowen and Lanford
  \cite{BowenLanford1970}, and
  \cite[Chapter~6]{LindMarcus1995}. The scalar determinant periodicity
  has already been computed in the statement.

  Choose \(\sigma_0>\sigma_c\). Since the Dirichlet series converges at
  \(\sigma_0\), its terms are bounded there: there is \(M>0\) with
  \(\abs{a_n}n^{-\sigma_0}\le M\) for all \(n\). Hence
  \(\abs{a_n}\le Mn^{\sigma_0}\), and for every
  \(\sigma>\sigma_0+2\) the series is absolutely convergent at
  \(\sigma\). In particular, for each fixed \(N\ge2\),
  \[
    \sum_{n\ge N}\abs{a_n}n^{-\sigma}
    \le M\sum_{n\ge N}n^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0)}
    =O(N^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0-1)})
    \qquad(\sigma\to+\infty),
  \]
  where the implied constant may depend on \(N\) and \(\sigma_0\), but
  not on \(\sigma\). Thus, for large real \(\sigma\), termwise
  subtraction is justified and
  \[
    F(\sigma+2\pi i)-F(\sigma)
    =\sum_{n\ge 2}a_n n^{-\sigma}(e^{-2\pi i\log n}-1).
  \]
  Separating the first non-zero index \(n_1\), the tail with \(n>n_1\)
  is bounded by
  \[
    \sum_{n>n_1}\abs{a_n}n^{-\sigma}
    \le M\sum_{n\ge n_1+1}n^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0)}
    =O\!\bigl((n_1+1)^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0-1)}\bigr),
  \]
  uniformly for \(\sigma>\sigma_0+2\). Hence the dominant term as
  \(\sigma\to+\infty\) is
  \[
    a_{n_1}n_1^{-\sigma}(e^{-2\pi i\log n_1}-1)
    +O\!\bigl((n_1+1)^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0-1)}\bigr).
  \]
  The error term is \(o(n_1^{-\sigma})\), because
  \[
    \frac{(n_1+1)^{-(\sigma-\sigma_0-1)}}{n_1^{-\sigma}}
    =(n_1+1)^{\sigma_0+1}
      \Bigl(\frac{n_1}{n_1+1}\Bigr)^\sigma
    \longrightarrow0.
  \]
  The coefficient \(e^{-2\pi i\log n_1}-1\) is non-zero. Indeed,
  \(e^{-2\pi i\log n_1}=1\) would imply \(\log n_1\in\ZZ\), say
  \(n_1=e^k\) with \(k\in\ZZ\); for \(n_1\ge2\) this has \(k\ge1\), but
  Hermite's theorem that \(e\) is transcendental implies \(e^k\) is
  transcendental, contradicting \(n_1\in\ZZ\). See, for instance,
  \cite[Chapter~11]{HardyWright2008}. In the Riemann zeta case this is
  the concrete assertion \(\log 2\notin\ZZ\), so
  \(e^{-2\pi i\log2}\ne1\). Thus the
  difference \(F(\sigma+2\pi i)-F(\sigma)\) is non-zero for all
  sufficiently large \(\sigma\), so \(F\) is not \(2\pi i\)-periodic on
  any right half-plane.

  If \(F\) agreed with \(\zeta_{X_A}(e^{-s})\) on a non-empty open
  subset \(U\subset\{\Re(s)>\sigma_c\}\) on which the determinant model
  is holomorphic, let
  \[
    \Omega:=\{s:\Re(s)>\sigma_c\}
      \setminus\{s:\det(I-(e^{-s})A)=0\}.
  \]
  On every connected component of \(\Omega\), the identity theorem
  propagates equality from any open subset of that component. Let
  \(\Omega_0\) be the component meeting \(U\). Since the determinant
  zeros are isolated, \(\Omega\) is obtained from a right half-plane by
  deleting a discrete set. Given any point of \(\Omega_0\) and any
  sufficiently large real \(\sigma\), a polygonal path from the point
  to \(\sigma\) meets only finitely many deleted points; replacing small
  segments near those points by short semicircles gives a path in
  \(\Omega\). Thus \(\sigma\in\Omega_0\) for arbitrarily large real
  \(\sigma\). Choose \(\sigma\) so large that
  \(\|(e^{-s})A\|<1\) for all \(s\) with \(\Re(s)\ge\sigma\). The
  far-right half-plane \(\Re(s)>\sigma\) then contains no determinant
  zero and is contained in the same component \(\Omega_0\). Equality on
  \(\Omega_0\) therefore holds at both large real \(s=\sigma+1\) and
  at \(s+2\pi i\). Periodicity of the determinant model gives
  \(F(\sigma+2\pi i)=F(\sigma)\). This contradicts the
  large-real-\(\sigma\) calculation above. The Riemann
  \(\zeta\)-function satisfies the hypothesis because \(a_n=1\neq 0\)
  for all \(n\ge 2\), so taking \(F(s)=\zeta(s)\) with \(n_1=2\) gives
  the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
  \Cref{thm:euler-product-natural-boundary,prop:finite-zeta-imaginary-periodicity}
  are complementary background tests. The first detects the boundary
  geometry of prime-supported Euler products. The second detects the
  periodicity forced on scalar determinant models by the logarithmic
  change of variables.
\end{remark}
