PART ONE — THE HOUSE STYLE, LEARNED FROM ACTUAL RECENT PAPERS
1. What recent JNT papers actually look like
I treated “recent” as approximately 2021–2026 and compared the manuscript with a cross-section of papers published or accepted in JNT: short classical-number-theory papers, medium-length analytic and probabilistic papers, and at least one article exceeding forty pages. The relevant comparison set includes Browning–Shparlinski on square-free polynomial values, Chow–Jones on the Fibonacci partition function, Li–Wang–Wang–Yi on covering systems, Grantham–Granville on primes in recurrence sequences, and recent longer papers on Iwasawa modules and random characteristic polynomials. The journal accepts a broad range of authorial styles, so there is no rigid template, but there is a recognizable editorial register. arXiv+4科学直通车+4arXiv+4
How a typical paper opens
A normal JNT paper opens by putting a familiar arithmetic object or problem on the page immediately. It then gives enough history to locate the precise question, formulates the new result, and moves into proofs. The opening is usually problem-driven rather than manifesto-driven.
The main theorem commonly appears on pages 2–4. It need not be on the first page. Browning–Shparlinski first state the square-free-value problem, describe the earlier range, define the family being averaged, and then give Theorem 1.1. Chow–Jones introduce the Fibonacci partition function, explain the misleading naïve prediction, show the numerical phenomenon, and state Theorem 1.1 on the third preprint page. The covering-systems paper gives a somewhat longer history and the necessary function-field definitions before stating its main theorem near the end of the introduction. Thus JNT readily permits either “problem, history, theorem” or “problem, example/computation, theorem,” but it does not normally postpone the result until after a general essay on the paper’s architecture. arXiv arXiv arXiv
For an ordinary 15–30-page paper, the introduction is often roughly 8–15% of the article. It may rise toward 15–20% when substantial history, examples, or conjectural heuristics are essential. In a forty- or fifty-page article, an introduction of five or six pages is not itself abnormal. The deciding question is whether those pages create a hierarchy or merely inventory everything that follows.
Your introduction occupies a little over six pages before Section 2 starts, so approximately 12% of the 52-page main article. The proportion is normal for JNT. The density and function of those pages are not.
How much explanatory prose is normal
Recent JNT papers are not austere lemma warehouses. Authors commonly give:


one or two paragraphs at the beginning of a section explaining the next mathematical move;


a sentence before a proposition explaining why it is needed;


a brief observation inside a proof when a construction changes direction;


a remark after a result when there is a genuine arithmetic consequence, limitation, or comparison.


But the body remains theorem-proof dominated. As a rough reading impression rather than a measured journal statistic, perhaps two-thirds to three-quarters of a conventional JNT body consists of definitions, estimates, formal arguments, and proofs. Explanatory prose is concentrated at points of transition. It is not repeated before and after each theorem.
Chow–Jones are a useful close comparator. Their introduction gives motivation and numerical evidence, but once the proof begins, the prose is local and operational: a case is introduced because it must be counted, a recurrence is motivated because the previous count produces it, and the proof proceeds. Browning–Shparlinski similarly give substantial contextual prose in the introduction, then move to compact preliminaries and sustained technical work. arXiv arXiv
How proofs are written
A short proof that delegates its substance to an existing theorem is entirely acceptable in JNT when the result really is an immediate application, a standard estimate, or a change of conventions. Browning–Shparlinski, for example, prove one preliminary bound by referring directly to Stewart, Lagrange’s theorem, and Hensel’s lemma. arXiv
But the journal expects the article to carry the argument at precisely those points where the authors claim a new translation, endpoint treatment, arithmetic normalization, or strengthening. “Apply [X]” is not enough when the contribution consists in showing that [X] applies with different indexing or at a previously excluded endpoint. Conversely, once such a transfer has been verified, JNT does not reward reproducing every branch of the source theory merely to demonstrate thoroughness.
Routine verifications are normally handled in one of three ways:


a sentence inside the proof;


a short lemma when the fact will be reused materially;


an appendix only when the computation is lengthy and genuinely distracts from the principal argument.


A formally named theorem whose proof is “this is exactly the preceding corollary” is not the usual register. Nor is a succession of formally named statements each memorializing one stage of the authors’ checking process.
Appendices and supplementary material
The dominant custom in the recent JNT papers I examined is to carry the mathematical article in one document. Many have no appendix at all. Browning–Shparlinski’s table of contents runs directly from the introduction through preliminaries, technical sections, proofs, and references. The accepted covering-systems paper similarly has three main sections and no appended second article. Chow–Jones is essentially introduction, the main counting argument, recurrence solution, references. arXiv arXiv arXiv
My practical estimate of the house custom is:


0–10% appended material: entirely ordinary;


10–20%: noticeable but acceptable when it consists of computations, data, technical uniformity lemmas, or convention-heavy checks;


about 27%, as in a 52-page main article plus a 19-page supplement: distinctly unusual.


The issue is not a formal prohibition. It is that a supplement of that size often signals one of two editorial problems: either essential proof material has been displaced from the paper, or the package contains a second paper that has not been separated.
The uploaded object contains only the 52-page main article; it ends with the references and author addresses on page 52. The supplement is described in the article as containing “conditional arithmetic and integer moment consequences,” and later as containing “conditional microcanonical, dyadic-ray, and integer-moment consequences outside the main theorem spine,” but the supplement itself is not attached. I can therefore assess the 52:19 architecture and the categories named by the authors, but not the supplement’s paragraph-level style or internal organization.   
Prose register
First-person plural is normal: “we prove,” “we shall use,” “we now apply.” Impersonal constructions also occur, but JNT does not expect a forced passive voice. Results are referred to by number. A section opening may remind the reader of the preceding result and state the next objective. Motivation is repeated only when the mathematical perspective genuinely changes.
The register is confident but not promotional. Authors do not usually keep an account, throughout the paper, of which sentence is imported, which result is a transfer, which claim is not being made, which adjacent manuscript does what, and which possible theorem remains open. That information belongs principally in one well-written passage in the introduction and, where necessary, in a precise local attribution.
The principal tells of a non-JNT register
The following are the common warning signs:


the introduction reads like an extended abstract plus a response to earlier referees;


every theorem is followed by a miniature proof synopsis;


elementary consequences and major results receive the same ceremonial presentation;


a paper repeatedly assures the reader that it is not claiming things;


an independent second theorem family begins late in the article;


the appendix or supplement is large enough to be another article;


transitions describe the document rather than advancing the mathematics;


the conclusion repeats the contribution inventory already given in the abstract and introduction.


Your manuscript exhibits several of these, despite having a genuinely JNT-like mathematical core.

The paper gets to mathematics immediately: the Fibonacci weights, raw sum, fold, visible window, and central finite problem are all introduced on page 1. 
Theorem A appears on page 3, followed immediately by Theorem B. The large-deviation theorem appears on page 5 and the joint theorem on page 6. That placement is entirely compatible with JNT.   
The introduction also does an unusually good job of stating the exact finite nature of the first results. The sentence that no limiting normalization or transducer is required is useful because it distinguishes the result from a merely asymptotic coding statement. 
What does not fit
The introduction explains the paper four times.
First, pages 2–3 distinguish the transferred pressure and extremal information from the new finite-window conclusions. 
Second, each of Theorems A–D is followed by a proof synopsis and a sentence saying where its full theorem appears. For example, the paragraph after Theorem A lists Fourier inversion, the congruence, and the boundary decomposition; Theorem C is followed by almost a paragraph of orbit-filling mechanics.  
Third, subsection 1.4 gives a detailed “Contribution boundary and organization,” again enumerating what is new, what is imported, what is derived from Weinstein, what the companion manuscript proves, what it does not prove, and which section contains each result.

This is the clearest point at which the paper reads as machine-assisted or referee-conditioned. It behaves as though every possible misunderstanding must be preempted in every available location. A human journal article normally trusts one precise contribution paragraph to do that work.

Reduce the introduction to approximately four to five pages.
A workable structure would be:


1–1.5 pages: object, finite fold, and why exact fibre information is not supplied by the existing pressure theory;


Theorem A and Theorem B, with no more than two sentences explaining their different roles;


one compact paragraph identifying the known pressure input;


Theorem C;

Delete subsection 1.4 in its present form. Its attribution content should be condensed to one paragraph placed before the organization paragraph. Mention the companion manuscript once, in a final sentence or footnote, only insofar as necessary to delimit dependence.

4. Does it read as machine-assisted?
Yes, in its editorial layer it does.
I do not mean that the mathematics reads fabricated or that the proofs lack mathematical individuality. The affine transfer, the orbit-filling construction, and the critical renewal argument have real internal shape. The machine-assisted impression comes from how the text packages and repeatedly explains them.
The strongest specific signs are these.
Repeated contribution ledgers
The paper gives essentially the same imported-versus-new accounting in:


the abstract;


the second page of the introduction;


subsection 1.4;


Remark 5.14;


the conclusion.


Compare the early distinction between transferred and finite-window information, the full contribution inventory in subsection 1.4, the source audit in Remark 5.14, and the closing inventory.    
A human author usually resolves this anxiety once and then writes the paper. Here, the prose keeps reopening the boundary as though every section must be independently safe against misreading.
Throat-clearing and structural announcements
There are many sentences of the form:


“The following lemma records exactly…”


“For clarity, eventual equality is a separate statement…”


“We now show that…”


“We record the direct finite-layer recovery…”


“It remains to…”


“We make the boundary passage … explicit.”


“The following pressure statement is retained because…”


Some of these are useful. In aggregate they create the sense that the text is continually announcing its own procedure instead of simply performing it. The sentence that a pressure statement is “retained because” its normalization is used later is especially revealing: it sounds like an editorial note left in the published narrative.   
Exhaustive enumeration where selection is needed
The introduction gives four headline theorems, their formulas, proof locations, and proof mechanisms. Subsection 1.4 then enumerates every new item and every imported item. Remark 5.14 repeats the source classification almost result by result.
The issue is not accuracy. The issue is editorial judgment. A journal article should choose which three ideas the reader must carry. This manuscript repeatedly gives the reader all of them.
Flat emphasis
Theorem 3.1, whose proof is the preceding corollary, is presented with the same theorem typography as the affine correspondence. The candidate pressure strip, later superseded by the exact pressure theorem, receives a named theorem and numerical width. A non-solution of an arithmetic interface receives a proposition plus two long remarks. Meanwhile, the genuinely decisive orbit-filling idea appears amid the same sequence of named statements.
The mathematics is uneven, as good mathematics should be. The presentation suppresses that unevenness.
Symmetrical sentence construction
The prose repeatedly uses balanced pairs:


first identity/second identity;


pointwise description/multiset description;


imported pressure/finite-window conclusion;


exposed slopes/nonexposed slopes;


one-dimensional filling/two-dimensional filling;


companion paper uses this but not that, while this paper uses that but not this.


Some of this reflects real structure. But the recurrence is strong enough that the prose acquires a templated rhythm.
Overall diagnosis
The manuscript does not read like raw machine output. It reads like a technically serious paper that has been machine-assisted through several rounds of defensive expansion and symmetry-seeking revision. The cure is not cosmetic synonym replacement. It is deletion and hierarchy:


say the contribution boundary once;


allow minor facts to remain minor;


let the central proof be much longer than its surroundings;


stop explaining why every included paragraph was included.


That would make the authors’ mathematical voice much more visible.

PART TWO — YOU ARE THE REFEREE
Recommendation: Major revisions
To the Editor:
The manuscript studies a finite folding map from binary Fibonacci sums to legal Zeckendorf windows. Its principal results give two exact descriptions of the fibre multiplicities, transfer these descriptions to local multiplicity spectra, identify critical finite-size laws at the freezing point of the associated pressure, and prove a full large-deviation principle by supplying lower bounds at the nonexposed slopes. A marked version yields a joint cost–multiplicity rate function with an affine coexistence face.
The subject is appropriate for the Journal of Number Theory. The exact affine correspondence and two-layer identity provide a clear arithmetic entry point, and the orbit-filling argument used to complete the large-deviation lower bound is sufficiently concrete that the probabilistic conclusions remain attached to the underlying Fibonacci arithmetic. The manuscript is also careful about attribution: the known golden-ratio Bernoulli-convolution pressure and the established extremal classification are not presented as new.
My recommendation is nevertheless major revision rather than acceptance in the present form.
The central problem is not the amount of technical detail by itself. It is that the article has not chosen a sufficiently strict main spine. It currently reads as a combination of:


an article on exact finite-window correspondences;


an article on local and critical Fibonacci-partition thermodynamics;


an article on one-layer and marked large deviations;


a prospectus for conditional prime-support and active-cutoff questions;


a substantial separate supplement.


The first three topics are mathematically connected, but the manuscript presents each as a nearly autonomous theorem suite. This gives subordinate, transferred, intermediate, and principal results comparable textual weight.
The introduction illustrates the problem. The main results are stated early, which is welcome, but each is accompanied by a proof synopsis and location notice. A later subsection then gives a full contribution boundary, a companion-paper disclaimer, and a section-by-section map. Closely related source and contribution inventories recur in Remark 5.14 and the conclusion.   
Several sections retain material that appears to document the development of the project rather than the final proof architecture. Theorem 5.5 confines the pressure boundary to a numerical strip, but Proposition 5.9 subsequently determines the boundary exactly. Proposition 5.12 formulates a finite-prime-support interface, while the following remark explains in considerable detail why the desired active-cutoff theorem is not proved. These results may be useful elsewhere, but they interrupt the route from the exact finite model to the critical law and full LDP.  
The most serious structural issue is Section 6. After the finite-window LDP and critical law have been proved, the manuscript restates and substantially re-proves analogous results intrinsically for one Fibonacci layer, and then proves a joint marked LDP. The section occupies approximately seven pages and reads as a second paper rather than a short application. Moreover, the joint one-layer theorem is advertised as one of the four principal results despite the finite-window title.  
I would ask the authors to reorganize the article around the following sequence:


exact affine and interval correspondences;


local stabilization and the weighted renewal identity;


pressure normalization at the freezing corner;


critical finite-size law;


orbit-filling proof of the full finite-window LDP.


The transferred extremal classification should be stated but its full convention audit can be placed in an appendix. The long reconstruction of the Weinstein branch formulas should be shortened in the article while retaining a complete proof of the endpoint improvement actually needed. The intermediate transition-strip theorem and compact-operator remark should be deleted or moved. The finite-prime-support interface should be placed in the supplement. Section 6 should be reduced to concise corollaries, or separated from the article.
The prose also requires a substantial editorial pass. The manuscript repeatedly uses contribution disclaimers, structure announcements, and statements that explain why another statement is being retained. The result is unusually defensive and gives an impression of machine-assisted uniformity. The authors should state the contribution boundary once in the introduction, retain exact local citations, and remove the repeated audits from the abstract, Remark 5.14, and conclusion.
The stated 52-page article with a 19-page supplement is also outside the usual balance for this journal. I have been supplied only the main article, so I cannot assess the supplement internally. On the description in the main text, however, the conditional arithmetic and integer-moment consequences appear nonessential to the main theorem chain. They should remain separate, and if they constitute a substantial coherent article they should not be presented as a nineteen-page supplement.
I do not believe that new mathematics is generally required to make the article acceptable. The main gap is editorial selection and reorganization. There is one possible exception: if the authors insist on retaining the joint marked theorem as a headline result under the present finite-window title, they should either formulate and prove the corresponding finite-window marked theorem or explain much more directly why the one-layer formulation is the natural finite-window consequence. Otherwise it should be demoted or moved.
The single objection most likely to sink the paper is the absence of a single controlled article architecture. A referee could reasonably conclude that the paper contains too many adjacent results, repeated source disclaimers, and a second theorem suite after the main theorem has already been proved. This objection is fixable. I would invite a substantial revision rather than recommend rejection, because the exact finite-window correspondences and the completion of the nonexposed large-deviation lower bounds form a coherent and publishable JNT article once the surrounding material is disciplined.

The manuscript is not yet at the state I would accept, but the acceptance gap is overwhelmingly one of craft rather than missing mathematics.
