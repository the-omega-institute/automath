Paper I: Journal of Statistical Physics
This is the right ambitious-but-realistic first submission. The paper has the structure of an exactly solved statistical-mechanical model: pressure, a freezing transition, coexistence, a nondifferentiable thermodynamic regime, an LDP with an affine rate-function segment, critical scaling, and a zero-temperature extremal law. The journal formally describes its remit as statistical physics and related fields involving collective phenomena in physical systems; importantly, its current contents also include abstract equilibrium-state work from dynamical systems, so a rigorous thermodynamic-formalism paper is not automatically out of place. Springer+1
The risk is nevertheless real. There is no underlying physical system in the synopsis. A hostile editor can read “freezing” and “coexistence” as imported vocabulary for an arithmetic multiplicity sequence. That is why I put the probability below 40%, despite the amount of mathematics.
Fallback: Discrete and Continuous Dynamical Systems. DCDS explicitly covers the theory and methods of dynamical systems and seeks important new methods and results. It is less likely than JSP to demand a physical interpretation, although it will still require the pressure/LDP mechanism—not the exact Fibonacci identities—to dominate the paper. 美国数学科学研究院
The single change that most raises the probability: extract a genuinely abstract renewal-freezing theorem and make the Fibonacci fold its complete verification.
The theorem should say, in a class of weighted renewal or finite-state multiplicity arrays, that a specified two-branch competition plus uniform thickness estimates implies, in one package:
pressure transition+linear critical partition sum+affine coexistence interval in the LDP+critical power-law counting.
The hypotheses must be recognizably checkable and not reverse-engineered to restate the Fibonacci proof. The orbit-filling argument for nonexposed slopes should appear in this general theorem rather than only in the application.
That one change would move my JSP estimate from 38% to approximately 49%. It answers the editor’s decisive question: “Why is this a statistical-physics theorem rather than a very elaborate solution of one recurrence?” Adding more exact extremal formulas would not produce a comparable gain.
The presentation should therefore begin with the freezing/LDP theorem. The exact maximum and second maximum are valuable finite-temperature and zero-temperature diagnostics, but they should follow the thermodynamic headline rather than precede it.

Paper II: Advances in Applied Mathematics
AAM is the best first choice because the paper genuinely spans the areas the journal says it covers: discrete mathematics, experimental mathematics, and theoretical computer science, with an expectation of a substantial mathematical advance. It has also published work on automatic sequences in Parry and Bertrand numeration systems and on computational Galois theory, so neither half of the manuscript is alien to the journal. The single change that most raises the probability: replace the merely qualitative “no rational bivariate series” contrast by a quantitative state-complexity theorem.
Let rq​ denote, for example, the minimal Hankel rank, minimal recurrence order, or minimal dimension of a linear representation for Sq​(m). Prove that

That addition would move my AAM estimate from 32% to roughly 42–44%. Without it, the paper is still publishable, but a skeptical referee can summarize it as “a carefully verified automaton followed by standard product constructions and several exact computations.”
4. Submission order and whether to wait
Submit Paper I first. Do not wait for its acceptance. Submit Paper II shortly afterward—ideally within the same week—once both stable preprints exist.
Paper I should go first because it is the broader and stronger paper, and because it owns the convention-equivalence theorem, the complete partition-difference proof, and the global fibre geometry. It should establish the canonical object and notation. Paper II can then cite Paper I for those matters and say cleanly that its subject is the finite-state and arithmetic structure of the moment tower.
Simultaneous submission of the two distinct papers is safe. What is prohibited is submitting the same manuscript, or essentially the same research contribution, to more than one journal. JSP’s rules say that the work submitted there may not simultaneously be under consideration elsewhere; Elsevier similarly prohibits concurrent or redundant publication of essentially the same research, while expressly not treating electronic preprints as prior publication. Cross-citation is necessary but not sufficient. I would do all four of the following:


Put both complete preprints online before the second journal submission, with stable bibliographic references.


Disclose the companion manuscript in both cover letters, including its title, preprint identifier and submission status.


Add a short paragraph to each introduction explicitly allocating theorem ownership.


Supply the companion PDF to the editor if the submission system permits related files.


The ownership division should be unmistakable:


Paper I owns: convention equivalence and index shift; the full partition-difference proof; multiset and extremal fibre theorems; pressure, freezing, critical renewal law, LDP and critical counting measure.


Paper II owns: losslessness of the carry automaton; fixed-q integral transfer; recurrences and rationality; Perron growth; full-tower nonrationality; Galois and Chebotarev results.


Paper II may quote the convention-equivalence statement and exact extremal growth as cited background. It should not reproduce their proofs or carry duplicate tables. Similarly, Paper I may mention that fixed moments admit finite-state recurrences, but should not construct the matrices.
Waiting for Paper I to be accepted buys almost nothing. Acceptance does not resolve an overlap problem; clean theorem separation and disclosure do. Waiting would merely postpone Paper II for an unpredictable review cycle.
5. The Galois and Chebotarev section
At Advances in Applied Mathematics, it is depth, not padding—but only if it remains a conceptual final section in the main paper. The certificates and bulky data belong in the supplement.
AAM explicitly welcomes experimental mathematics as well as discrete mathematics and theoretical computer science, and it has published computational Galois work. Thus a rigorous finite-range theorem for q=9,…,17 is legitimate journal content. Elsevier Shop+1
The main-text section should be titled something like “Arithmetic complexity of the Perron factors,” not “Computations for q=9,…,17.” It should contain:


the canonical definition of the polynomial or irreducible factor associated with each moment growth constant;


one theorem asserting irreducibility and full symmetric Galois group for the certified range;


the mathematical certification strategy, not merely software output;


one explicit Chebotarev corollary.


For example, when the relevant degree-dq​ polynomial has Galois group Sdq​​, the factorization types modulo unramified primes occur with the cycle-type densities prescribed by Sdq​​. That is a concrete arithmetic consequence, not just the sentence “Chebotarev applies.”
The supplement or repository should contain the complete polynomial lists, modular factorizations, discriminant calculations, witness primes, machine-readable certificates and scripts. I would retain approximately five pages of theorem, proof architecture and interpretation in the article itself.
It becomes padding under either of two presentations:


nine largely unmotivated case computations followed by a generic invocation of Chebotarev;


an isolated supplement that is never used to interpret the transfer matrices or their Perron roots.


Properly integrated, the section is one reason Paper II rises above automata bookkeeping: it shows that the algebraic growth constants produced by the finite-state construction are not repeatedly collapsing to elementary low-degree objects. It must not, however, be advertised as evidence proving a generic Sd​ theorem for all q. The finite range is a rigorous theorem and may motivate a conjecture; it is not itself an asymptotic result.
Final allocation: Paper I to Journal of Statistical Physics, then Paper II to Advances in Applied Mathematics, submitted almost simultaneously with full disclosure. Keep the Galois theorem in Paper II’s main text and move only its certification bulk to the supplement.
