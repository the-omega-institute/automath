PART ONE — THE HOUSE STYLE, LEARNED FROM ACTUAL RECENT PAPERS
1. What recent JFA papers actually look like
I used a representative group of recent JFA papers in analysis, probability, entropy, semigroups, and functional inequalities, rather than trying to infer a style from the journal’s scope statement. The sample included Polarized Hardy–Stein Identity, The Poisson Transport Map, Stability for the Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality, Asymptotic Behavior of Solutions to the Heat Equation on Noncompact Symmetric Spaces, Esscher Transform and the Central Limit Theorem, Stability for the Logarithmic Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev Inequality, and the substantially longer Optimal Transport of Stationary Point Processes. These papers do not share a cosmetic template, but they do share a recognizable editorial architecture.
How a JFA paper normally opens
The accepted paper usually begins with the mathematical object or inequality itself, not with a taxonomy of contributions.


The Poisson Transport Map opens by stating the classical transport-map mechanism and immediately explaining the discrete obstruction. Its first new main theorem appears on page 3. arXiv+1


Stability for the Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality labels its first subsection “Main result” and states its main theorem on page 2. arXiv+1


Anker–Papageorgiou–Zhang state their first main theorem on page 2 and their second principal theorem by page 3; the introduction ends there. arXiv+2arXiv+2


Bobkov–Götze introduce the normalized sums on the first page and state Theorem 1.1 on page 2. arXiv+1


Carlen gives the classical inequality first, then states his own principal stability theorem on page 3. arXiv+1


The 48-page Erbar–Huesmann–Jalowy–Müller paper is a legitimate long-form exception: its introduction occupies about seven pages because it is building a new transport geometry. Even there, Theorem 1.1 occurs on page 3, further flagship results are stated through the introduction, and the eight-page introduction has a clear progression from metric construction to entropy gradient flow and consequences. arXiv+1


Polarized Hardy–Stein Identity formally proves its central results later, but the exact identities are displayed on pages 1–2 and the authors say immediately which theorem establishes each one. arXiv+2arXiv+2


The working norm is therefore:


a short abstract, usually one paragraph;


the problem or central formula on page 1;


a precise new theorem, or an exact preview of it, within pages 1–3;


history after the reader knows what is being proved;


an introduction of roughly 3–7 pages, commonly about 10–18% of the article.


There are exceptions, but “the reader knows the paper’s main mathematical transaction by page 3” is a strong JFA regularity.
Prose versus formal statement and proof
The prose is concentrated rather than uniformly distributed.
A typical accepted paper uses prose in four places:


the introduction, to define the problem and explain the novelty;


the opening paragraph of a section, to say what technical obstacle the section resolves;


immediately before a difficult proof transition, often in one or two sentences;


a remark after a theorem, to explain sharpness, limitations, or relation to earlier work.


Inside proofs, the register becomes economical. A proof may say, for example, that the next estimate handles lack of integrability, or that one passes from compactly supported inputs by approximation. It normally does not explain the architecture again after every lemma.
On a rough page-count basis, recent JFA papers often devote 10–20% of the article to introductory and interpretive prose. The rest is definitions, statements, and proofs. The proportion can be higher for a paper constructing a new framework, but even then the exposition advances the mathematics rather than repeatedly describing the document.
What JFA expects from proofs
A short proof consisting mainly of citations is entirely acceptable when the result is genuinely standard, a direct corollary, or included only to fix normalization. JFA does not require authors to reprove standard semigroup theory, Fourier inversion, classical quadrature, or familiar compactness arguments.
But the new proof chain is normally carried by the article.
The directly relevant comparator is Polarized Hardy–Stein Identity. It has a large appendix block, but the main new identities and their principal proofs are in the numbered body sections. The appendices contain auxiliary Bregman inequalities, Lp-calculus, convexity facts, an alternative proof in a restricted range, a Gaussian variant, and a generator identification. The appendix block begins on page 19 of a 32-page preprint, yet it does not serve as a vault containing the only proofs of a chain of article-level new lemmas. arXiv+1
Routine verifications are handled in one of three ways:


one or two lines in the proof;


a precise citation;


an integrated appendix containing the complete technical argument.


What is uncommon is a succession of prominently stated, article-specific lemmas whose proofs are each replaced by “See the proof following [same title] in the Supplementary Material.”
Main text, appendices, and supplementary material
There is no rigid JFA ratio. The honest empirical picture is:


many recent papers have no appendix at all;


a routine appendix burden is approximately 0–15% of the article;


a technical paper can put 25–40% in appendices, as the Hardy–Stein paper does;


a separate supplement is much less characteristic than appendices integrated into the article.


Thus the manuscript’s numerical ratio—87 pages main and 33 supplement, so 27.5% of the total in the supplement and a supplement 38% as long as the main article—is high but not intrinsically disqualifying. There is direct JFA precedent for an auxiliary block of comparable relative size.
The decisive question is what has been relegated. JFA appendices normally hold:


long elementary inequalities;


coefficient tables and extended calculations;


standard background needed only by a subset of readers;


alternative proofs;


technical approximation or regularization lemmas;


secondary variants.


They do not normally hold enough of the central proof chain that the main article becomes a catalogue of theorem statements and cross-references.
Register and signposting
First-person plural is the normal register: “we prove,” “we now show,” “we first establish.” Impersonal phrasing is used occasionally, but sustained passive prose is not the norm.
Results are signposted directly:


“Our first main result is…”


“The main difficulty is…”


“Theorem 1.3 follows from…”


“We next prove…”


The accepted register is confident but not promotional. Novelty is usually identified once in the introduction, perhaps qualified once in a remark. Motivation is not repeated in every later section.
Tells that a manuscript does not fit the register
The most common signs are:


an abstract that functions as an introduction, priority memorandum, and limitation ledger at once;


several pages of history or defensive qualification before the reader can identify the central theorem;


a four- or five-page theorem registry in the introduction;


a different apparent paper in each major section;


repeated assurances about what is “not claimed”;


internal project-management language such as “hypothesis ledger,” “source map,” “mechanism comparison,” or “organization guide”;


proofs of central new assertions systematically displaced outside the article;


appendical material left in the numbered main body despite being described by the authors themselves as secondary;


equal rhetorical emphasis given to central theorems, peripheral refinements, convention-robustness statements, and imported machinery.


JFA tolerates length. It is much less tolerant of the impression that the editor has received an assembled research dossier rather than a shaped paper.

Title and abstract
The title sounds like one coherent JFA paper: a stable-flow dissipation theorem followed by an optimal stable-kernel asymptotic theorem. The body does not initially deliver that paper.
The abstract is far outside the usual JFA scale. It runs for almost two pages and contains at least five distinct layers:


the stable-flow dissipation theorem;


the stable-reference interpolation result;


a priority and non-priority account;


the optimal stable moment exponent;


the abstract positive-proxy theorem;

“Contribution” and novelty discussion
The contribution discussion is factually careful but rhetorically over-defended. It repeatedly uses the pattern:

A human-edited JFA introduction would choose. It would say, in effect:

The classical identity is X. The missing point is Y. We prove Y under Z. The principal asymptotic result is W.

It would not litigate every neighbouring theorem before stating the paper’s own theorem.

Section 3: Poisson smoothing and entropy asymptotics

More seriously, many article-specific results have proofs consisting only of a pointer to the supplement. Numerically: unusual but defensible. Substantively: not right.
A 33-page technical supplement to an 87-page JFA paper is not automatically excessive. The directly relevant Hardy–Stein comparator devotes roughly the final twelve of thirty-two pages to appendices. But that paper keeps its main new identity and principal proof in the article and uses the appendices for auxiliary estimates, calculus, an alternative proof, and related variants. arXiv+1
Here, the main text indicates that the supplement contains:


proofs of numerous named article-specific lemmas;


a domain-and-differentiation package;


comparison and motivation material;


a hypothesis ledger;


a notation guide;


coefficient calculations;

That is too many functions for one supplement.
The correct redistribution is not simply “move twenty pages back into the main article,” because the main article is already too broad. It is:


Move out of this paper: most of Sections 3, 4.5–4.8, 5.1, 5.3, most of Section 6, and all of Sections 7–8.

After the split, a good JFA configuration would be approximately 55–70 pages of article and 8–15 pages of technical appendix. A longer appendix remains defensible if it contains complete auxiliary estimates, but the reader should be able to follow the new proof chain without repeatedly leaving the article.

4. Does it read as machine-assisted?
Yes, in identifiable stretches.
Again, that is a judgment about the prose, not a claim about how it was produced.
The strongest tells are:


the nearly two-page abstract, which gives every result, every priority qualification, every mechanism boundary, and every non-claim;


the four-page “Contribution” discussion, in which almost every positive sentence is balanced by a negative sentence;


the “Proof roadmap,” which inventories proof routes and document locations rather than simply beginning the proof;


the repeated phrases “no assertion is made,” “not claimed,” “deliberately,” “in the prescribed ansatz,” and “the novelty claim is correspondingly narrow”;


the exhaustive convention analysis in the latter part of Section 5;


the long “Role of the strip and lattice results” paragraph;


the flat allocation of emphasis: the stable-flow theorem, a normalization convention, an order-eight coefficient certificate, a proxy gauge, and an imported lattice criterion are all introduced with approximately the same rhetorical gravity.


A human mathematician is uneven on purpose. In the strongest part of this manuscript, Theorem 4.2 gets a long proof because it needs one. That feels natural. Elsewhere, the manuscript seems unwilling to let a small matter remain small. Every possible interpretive boundary receives a paragraph, proposition, or named convention. The resulting flatness is more damaging than the sentence-level prose, which is generally grammatical and technically precise.
The repair is not to make the prose more casual. It is to restore hierarchy: two or three things should matter greatly; several things should receive one sentence; many things should disappear.
PART TWO — YOU ARE THE REFEREE
Recommendation: reject and invite resubmission after substantial reconstitution.
Report to the Editor
The manuscript contains several substantial results that appear, on the basis of the present reading and the prior technical assessment assumed by the authors, to be potentially appropriate for the Journal of Functional Analysis. In particular, I regard the relative-entropy dissipation theorem for two symmetric stable heat flows, the endpoint representation, the optimal finite-moment exponent for stable-kernel first-unmatched-moment asymptotics, and the positive tail-jet decomposition as a serious and coherent possible JFA contribution.
My recommendation is nevertheless not major revision but rejection with encouragement to resubmit. The required changes are too structural for a revision of the present submission.
The article currently contains at least three largely independent papers.
The first is a one-dimensional Cauchy/Cayley paper on all-order entropy coefficients, moment thresholds, Gaussian quadrature, and coefficient defects. The second is a stable-semigroup paper on entropy dissipation, optimal moment exponents, and law-by-law tail defects. The third is an RKHS and integer-lattice sampling paper for Poisson observation channels. There are genuine connections among these subjects, but the manuscript does not turn those connections into a single theorem-driven narrative. Instead, it preserves almost every result obtained from a common collection of quotient calculations.
This causes a serious mismatch between title, opening, and body. The title promises stable-flow dissipation and stable-kernel moment exponents, whereas the introduction begins with the Cayley transform and the first twenty-seven pages are predominantly devoted to Cauchy and radial Poisson coefficient machinery. The general stable-flow theorem begins only on page 28. The main stable asymptotic theorem appears still later, after further Cauchy, multidimensional Poisson, quadrature, and critical-tail developments. Sections 7 and 8 are explicitly described by the authors as secondary and outside the stable-entropy spine, yet remain in the main article.
The introduction does not solve this problem. Its abstract is much too long, and its contribution discussion reads as a comprehensive novelty and limitation ledger. The three “Main Theorems” are compound theorem catalogues rather than a usable introduction to the paper. The reader is given many exact formulas and qualifications before being told which two or three results organize the work. There is also extensive repetition of priority and scope boundaries later in the article.
A second major concern is the division between the main text and the 33-page supplement. I have not been supplied the supplement itself, but the main article repeatedly states new lemmas and propositions whose proofs are deferred there. Some of those results are routine calculations and could appropriately be relegated. Others form the quotient-expansion and entropy-transfer chain on which article-level theorems rest. In a JFA paper, the principal new argument should be inspectable in the article. A large technical appendix is acceptable; a main text that repeatedly functions as an index to its own proofs is not.
The strongest portion is Section 4 through the proof of Theorem 4.2. It states a clear analytic problem, isolates the noncompact Green-pairing issue, proves the required quotient estimates, and carries the argument at an appropriate level of detail. The stable critical-exponent and positive proxy results in Sections 5.4–5.6 could form a second part of the same paper. These sections indicate what the resubmitted article should be.
I would recommend that the authors build a new manuscript around the following spine:


stable-flow relative-entropy dissipation and endpoint representation;


the critical stable translate-quotient estimate;


the optimal moment exponent at arbitrary first unmatched order;


the law-by-law positive tail-jet decomposition and abstract kernel criterion;


sharpness and one concise Poisson or covariance application.


The Cauchy coefficient hierarchy, Gaussian quadrature results, higher coefficient defects, RKHS completion, and lattice sampling results should be removed and developed separately. The proofs of all new lemmas essential to the retained spine should be incorporated in the article or in clearly labeled appendices included with it.
The prose also needs substantial compression. The repeated negative formulations—what is not claimed, what is not canonical, what branch is not covered, what ansatz is not classified—make the paper read as though every paragraph has been written to pre-empt a hypothetical objection. One precise novelty paragraph and one scope remark would be stronger. The convention-robustness discussion should be reduced to the variants mathematically needed for the theorem.
The single objection most likely to sink the paper is therefore the absence of a single paper-level architecture. This is not a superficial stylistic defect. It prevents the editor and referee from determining what the article is asking to be accepted as, and it obscures the strongest theorems behind extensive secondary developments and displaced proofs.
The objection is fixable. I do not see, from the main article, a need for a new flagship theorem if the authors are willing to split and reorganize the material. It is not fixable by shortening paragraphs, adding another roadmap, or moving a few lemmas between files. It requires a new article assembled from the present material around the stable-flow and stable-kernel results.
I would be willing to review such a resubmission.
