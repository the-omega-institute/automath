\section{Unconditional extremal fibers and the real-tilt interface}
\label{sec:extremal-ldp}

The affine coefficient model gives one description of the fiber spectrum.  For
extremal questions there is a second, particularly rigid description: the
fibers are consecutive values of the ordinary Fibonacci partition function on
two explicit intervals.  This removes the recurrence assumptions that are
sometimes imposed in experimental treatments of the largest fibers.

\subsection{An exact interval model}

\begin{theorem}[Fiber--partition interval identity]
\label{thm:fiber-partition-interval}
Let \(m\ge0\) and \(0\le r<F_{m+2}\) be integers.  Then
\[
 d_m^\#(r)=
 \begin{cases}
 R(F_{m+2}+r),&0\le r\le F_{m+1}-2,\\
 R(r),&F_{m+1}-1\le r<F_{m+2}.
 \end{cases}
\]
An interval with lower endpoint larger than its upper endpoint is understood
to be empty.  In particular, this convention covers \(m=0,1\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The raw value \(N_m(\omega)\) is a sum of a subset of
\(F_2,F_3,\ldots,F_{m+1}\).  Since the single unit weight is \(F_2\), the
number of words with raw value \(n<F_{m+2}\) is exactly \(R(n)\).  By
\Cref{prop:single-overflow}, a word in the fiber over residue \(r\) has raw
value either \(r\) or \(F_{m+2}+r\).

Suppose first that \(0\le r\le F_{m+1}-2\).  Then
\[
 F_{m+2}+r\le F_{m+3}-2<F_{m+3}.
\]
Consequently every ordinary Fibonacci partition of \(F_{m+2}+r\) uses only
parts from \(F_2,\ldots,F_{m+2}\).  Those using \(F_{m+2}\) are in bijection,
by deletion of that part, with the \(R(r)\) partitions of \(r\).  Those not
using \(F_{m+2}\) are precisely the raw words of value \(F_{m+2}+r\).  The
fiber also contains the \(R(r)\) raw words of value \(r\).  Therefore the two
copies of \(R(r)\) cancel between this decomposition and the fiber sum, giving
\[
 d_m^\#(r)
 =R(r)+\bigl(R(F_{m+2}+r)-R(r)\bigr)
 =R(F_{m+2}+r).
\]

Now suppose that \(F_{m+1}-1\le r<F_{m+2}\).  The largest possible raw value is
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^m F_{j+1}=F_{m+3}-2,
\]
whereas
\[
 F_{m+2}+r\ge F_{m+3}-1.
\]
Thus no word in this fiber can have the overflow value \(F_{m+2}+r\).  Every
partition of \(r<F_{m+2}\) uses only \(F_2,\ldots,F_{m+1}\), so the remaining
raw words are counted by \(R(r)\).  This proves the second case.
\end{proof}

For a compact statement of the maximizing residues, put
\[
 G_j:=F_{j+1}\qquad(j\ge0),
\]
so \(G_0=G_1=1\) and \(G_{j+1}=G_j+G_{j-1}\).
For \(k\ge5\), define
\[
 I_{2k+1}:=
 \begin{cases}
 G_{k+1}G_{k-3}+1,&k\text{ even},\\
 G_kG_{k-2}+1,&k\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\]
For \(k\ge7\), define
\[
 I_{2k}:=
\begin{cases}
 G_{k+2}G_{k-5}+G_3+1,&k\text{ even},\\
 G_{k+1}G_{k-4}+G_3+1,&k\text{ odd},
 \end{cases}
\]
\[
 J_{2k}:=
 \begin{cases}
 G_{k+1}G_{k-3}+1,&k\text{ even},\\
 G_kG_{k-2}+1,&k\text{ odd}.
 \end{cases}
\]

\begin{conventionlemma}
Let \((G_j)_{j\ge0}\) denote the convention of
\cite{KocabovaMasakovaPelantova2005}, namely \(G_0=G_1=1\), with
representations using the parts \(G_1,G_2,\ldots\).  Then
\(G_j=F_{j+1}\) for every \(j\ge0\), and the source representation-count
function is exactly \(R\).  In particular, the source formulas
\[
 \max_{G_{2a+1}\le n<G_{2a+2}}R(n)=G_{a+1},\qquad
 \max_{G_{2a+2}\le n<G_{2a+3}}R(n)=2G_a
\]
become
\[
 \max_{F_{2a+2}\le n<F_{2a+3}}R(n)=F_{a+2},\qquad
 \max_{F_{2a+3}\le n<F_{2a+4}}R(n)=2F_{a+1}.
\]
The generic maximizing arguments in the source are
\[
 G_{2k+1}-1+I_{2k+1},\qquad G_{2k+2}-1-I_{2k+1}
\]
in odd source length \(2k+1\), and
\[
 \begin{split}
 G_{2k}-1+I_{2k},&\quad G_{2k+1}-1-I_{2k},\\
 G_{2k}-1+J_{2k},&\quad G_{2k+1}-1-J_{2k}
 \end{split}
\]
in even source length \(2k\).  The exceptional source arguments and their
translated fiber residues are
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 \text{source length}&\text{partition arguments}&\text{fiber residues}\\ \hline
 9&63,71,79&8,16,24\\
 12&270,296,304,312,338&37,63,71,79,105.
 \end{array}
\]
Here the last column subtracts \(G_9=F_{10}=55\) or
\(G_{12}=F_{13}=233\), respectively.
\end{conventionlemma}

\begin{proof}
The identity \(G_j=F_{j+1}\) follows from the initial values and recurrence.
The admissible source parts \(G_1,G_2,\ldots\) are therefore exactly our
parts \(F_2,F_3,\ldots\), including a single unit part.  The maximum and
argument formulas are Theorems~4.7 and~5.3 of
\cite{KocabovaMasakovaPelantova2005}; direct substitution gives the displayed
formulas and endpoint subtractions.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact largest fibers]
\label{thm:unconditional-extremal-fibers}
Let \(D_m=\max_{x\in X_m}d_m(x)\).  Then
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 D_0=D_1=1,\qquad D_{2k}=F_{k+2}\quad(k\ge0),\\
 D_{2k+1}=2F_{k+1}\quad(k\ge1).
 \end{gathered}
\]
No auxiliary recurrence hypothesis is assumed.

For every \(m\ge0\), a visible word \(x\in X_m\) is a maximizer if and only if
its residue \(r=V_m(x)\) belongs to the following set.  If \(m=2k\ge10\), the
set is
\[
 \mathcal M_{2k}
 =\{I_{2k+1}-1,\;G_{2k}-1-I_{2k+1}\}.
\]
If \(m=2k-1\ge13\), the set is
\[
 \mathcal M_{2k-1}
 =\{I_{2k}-1,\;J_{2k}-1,\;
       G_{2k-1}-1-I_{2k},\;G_{2k-1}-1-J_{2k}\}.
\]
The remaining maximizing sets are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|l}
m&D_m&\mathcal M_m\\ \hline
0&1&\{0\}\\
1&1&\{0,1\}\\
2&2&\{0\}\\
3&2&\{0,1,3\}\\
4&3&\{0,3\}\\
5&4&\{3\}\\
6&5&\{3,8\}\\
7&6&\{3,8,11,16\}\\
8&8&\{8,16,24\}\\
9&10&\{16,24,29,37\}\\
11&16&\{37,63,71,79,105\}.
\end{array}
\]
The windows \(m=10\) and \(m=12\) are covered by the generic even formula
for \(\mathcal M_{2k}\); they require no additional exceptional rows.
In particular, apart from the displayed initial exceptions, the degeneracy of
the maximum is exactly \(2\) in even windows and exactly \(4\) in odd windows.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
 M_\ell:=\max_{F_{\ell+1}\le n<F_{\ell+2}}R(n).
\]
By the Fibonacci-convention lemma, Theorem~4.7 of
\cite{KocabovaMasakovaPelantova2005} gives
\[
 M_{2a+1}=F_{a+2}\quad(a\ge0),
 \qquad
 M_{2a+2}=2F_{a+1}\quad(a\ge1),
\]
with \(M_1=M_2=1\).
The same paper classifies every argument attaining these maxima
\cite[Thm.~5.3]{KocabovaMasakovaPelantova2005}.

By \Cref{thm:fiber-partition-interval}, the first block of the fiber list is
\[
 \bigl(R(n)\bigr)_{F_{m+2}\le n\le F_{m+3}-2}.
\]
Its maximum is \(M_{m+1}\).  The second block consists of values \(R(n)\) with
\(F_{m+1}-1\le n<F_{m+2}\).  For \(m\ge4\), its maximum is at most \(M_m\), and
the displayed formulas give \(M_m<M_{m+1}\): if \(m=2a\), then
\(2F_a<F_{a+2}\), while if \(m=2a+1\), then
\(F_{a+2}<2F_{a+1}\).  Hence \(D_m=M_{m+1}\) for \(m\ge4\).  The same equality
for \(m=0,1,2,3\), with the stated exceptional degeneracies, follows by direct
enumeration of the at most eight raw words.  Substitution of \(m=2k\) and
\(m=2k+1\) into the formulas for \(M_{m+1}\) proves the asserted closed forms.

It remains to translate the arguments of the maximum.  The
Fibonacci-convention lemma states that the two generic maximizing integers of
odd source length \(2k+1\) are
\[
 G_{2k+1}-1+I_{2k+1},
 \qquad
 G_{2k+2}-1-I_{2k+1}.
\]
Subtracting the left endpoint \(G_{2k+1}=F_{m+2}\), as prescribed by the first
case of \Cref{thm:fiber-partition-interval}, gives
\(I_{2k+1}-1\) and \(G_{2k}-1-I_{2k+1}\).  Similarly, the four generic
maximizing integers of even length \(2k\) are obtained by using \(I_{2k}\) and
\(J_{2k}\) in the same two symmetric positions.  Subtraction of
\(G_{2k}=F_{m+2}\) gives the four residues displayed in the theorem.  The
exceptional-argument table in that lemma gives \(m=8\) and \(m=11\) after the
same endpoint subtraction.  Direct evaluation of the finitely many shorter
cases gives the remaining rows of the theorem.  Since \(V_m\) is bijective,
this residue list classifies the visible maximizing words themselves.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact two-step extremal recurrence]
\label{cor:extremal-fiber-recurrence}
For every integer \(m\ge6\),
\[
 D_m=D_{m-2}+D_{m-4}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If \(m=2k\), then the three terms are \(F_{k+2},F_{k+1},F_k\), respectively.
If \(m=2k+1\), they are \(2F_{k+1},2F_k,2F_{k-1}\), respectively.  In both
cases the assertion is the Fibonacci recurrence.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Higher extremal ranks]
\label{rem:higher-extremal-ranks-open}
The interval identity is an unconditional reduction of every distinct
multiplicity level and its degeneracy to the corresponding level sets of
\(R(n)\) on the two displayed intervals.  The cited extremal theorem classifies
the first distinct level, but it does not classify the second or arbitrary
fixed \(k\)-th distinct level.  Consequently no closed formula for all top-
\(k\) levels is asserted here.  Establishing such formulas is a separate open
interface; the accompanying finite computations are not used as a substitute
for that proof.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Negative-temperature freezing and the residual LDP interface}

For real \(t\), define the real collision sum
\[
 S_t(m):=\sum_{x\in X_m}d_m(x)^t.
\]
This is well defined for all \(t\in\RR\), since every fiber is nonempty.  The
interval-completeness lemma following \Cref{thm:affine-transfer} proves this
nonemptiness before negative powers are introduced.  The positive integer
specializations agree with the moments in
\Cref{def:difference-spectrum}.

The following lemma records exactly the convention transfer and uniform bound
needed below.

\begin{layercountlemma}
For \(j\ge0\), set
\[
 \mathcal I_j=[F_{j+1}-1,F_{j+2}-1),\qquad
 N_j(k)=\card{\{n\in\mathcal I_j:R(n)=k\}}.
\]
Let \(\Psi(k)\) be Weinstein's number of generating orbits at level \(k\).
Then
\[
 N_0(1)=0,\qquad N_j(1)=1\quad(j\ge1),
\]
and, for every \(j\ge0\) and \(k\ge2\),
\[
 N_j(k)\le 2\Psi(k).
\]
For clarity, eventual equality is a separate statement: \(N_j(2)=2\) for
\(j\ge4\), while for \(k>2\) one has
\(N_j(k)=2\Psi(k)\) for \(j\ge2k\).  Weinstein's published theorem states
this for \(j>2k\); the endpoint \(j=2k\) follows from the largest-index
argument below.
\end{layercountlemma}

\begin{proof}
Weinstein uses \(f_0=f_1=1\)
\cite[Secs.~3 and~5]{Weinstein2016}.  Thus
\[
 f_j=F_{j+1}\qquad(j\ge0),
\]
and his half-open layer \([f_j-1,f_{j+1}-1)\) is exactly
\(\mathcal I_j\).  His partition function also equals \(R\), because its
admissible parts \(f_1,f_2,\ldots\) are our \(F_2,F_3,\ldots\).

For \(n\ge1\), write Weinstein's Zeckendorf index partition as
\[
 Z(n)=\langle z(n,1),\ldots,z(n,\lambda(n))\rangle
\]
and define its largest index by
\[
 L(n):=z(n,\lambda(n))=\max Z(n).
\]
Weinstein's Lemma~2.19, together with
\(R(f_i-1)=1\), gives the endpoint-sensitive equivalence
\begin{equation}
 R(n)>1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \bigl(n\in[f_j-1,f_{j+1}-1)\iff L(n)=j\bigr).
 \label{eq:layer-largest-index}
\end{equation}
Indeed, after the left endpoint \(f_j-1\) and the right endpoint
\(f_{j+1}-1\), both of which have partition value \(1\), are removed,
Lemma~2.19 identifies the remaining integers in the layer with those whose
largest Zeckendorf index is \(j\).

At level \(1\), Weinstein's description
\(\{n:R(n)=1\}=\{f_i-1:i\ge1\}\), together with the half-open endpoint
convention, places exactly the left endpoint \(f_j-1\) in each
\(\mathcal I_j\) for \(j\ge1\); \(\mathcal I_0\) is empty.

At level \(2\), Weinstein gives \(\Psi(2)=1\) and the two families
\[
 f_i-1+f_{i+3},\qquad f_{i+2}-1+f_{i+4}\qquad(i\ge0).
\]
The first lies in \([f_{i+3}-1,f_{i+4}-1)\), since the distances from
the two endpoints are \(f_i\) and \(f_{i+1}\); the second lies in
\([f_{i+4}-1,f_{i+5}-1)\), with the analogous upper distance
\(f_{i+1}\).  Hence a layer contains at most one member of each family,
so \(N_j(2)\le2=2\Psi(2)\), with equality for \(j\ge4\).

Now let \(k>2\).  By Weinstein's Theorem~3.8, every solution of
\(R(n)=k\) has a unique representation
\begin{equation}
 [a]\tau^\varepsilon\sigma^\delta(g),
 \qquad
 g\in G(k),\quad a\ge0,\quad \varepsilon,\delta\in\{0,1\},
 \label{eq:weinstein-orbit-normal-form}
\end{equation}
where \(G(k)\) is the set of \(\Psi(k)\) generating solutions.  We derive the
largest-index behavior of all four branches from Weinstein's explicit
formulas~(17)--(19).

First consider a number \(n\) in Weinstein's sheet \(N_1\), and let
\(\theta(n)\) and \(Z'(n)\) denote the initial-block parameter and the
Zeckendorf tail used in those formulas.  Thus \(Z'(n)\) is a nonempty terminal
subpartition of \(Z(n)\), and in particular \(\max Z'(n)=L(n)\).  Formula~(18)
states that
\[
 [a](n)=f_{2\theta(n)-2+2a}-1+\lVert f_{Z'(n)+2a}\rVert.
\]
The definition of \(\theta(n)\) gives
\begin{equation}
 z(n,\theta(n))\ge2\theta(n).
 \label{eq:theta-tail-separation}
\end{equation}
To see this directly, if \(\theta(n)=1\), then the defining first index is at
least \(3\).  If \(\theta(n)>1\), the indices preceding
\(z(n,\theta(n))\) differ successively by \(2\), the first index is at least
\(1\), and the gap at \(\theta(n)\) is at least \(3\).  Hence
\(z(n,\theta(n))\ge1+2(\theta(n)-2)+3=2\theta(n)\).
The identity
\[
 f_{2q}-1=f_1+f_3+\cdots+f_{2q-1}\qquad(q\ge0),
\]
with the sum empty for \(q=0\), and
\eqref{eq:theta-tail-separation} show that the displayed sum is already the
Zeckendorf decomposition
\[
 Z([a]n)
 =\langle1,3,\ldots,2\theta(n)+2a-3\rangle
   \sqcup\bigl(Z'(n)+2a\bigr).
\]
The terminal subpartition contains the largest index.  Hence
\begin{equation}
 L([a]n)=L(n)+2a.
 \label{eq:L-a-shift}
\end{equation}

Formula~(19) gives
\[
 \tau(n)=
 \begin{cases}
 f_{2\theta(n)}-1+\lVert f_{Z'(n)+1}\rVert,
   &z(n,\theta(n))\ \text{odd},\\
 f_{2\theta(n)-4}-1+\lVert f_{Z'(n)-1}\rVert,
   &z(n,\theta(n))\ \text{even}.
 \end{cases}
\]
Expanding the first terms by the same identity and using
\eqref{eq:theta-tail-separation} shows that the shifted tail begins at least
two indices after the prefix and therefore supplies the largest index.
Consequently
\begin{equation}
 L(\tau n)=
 \begin{cases}
 L(n)+1,&z(n,\theta(n))\ \text{odd},\\
 L(n)-1,&z(n,\theta(n))\ \text{even},
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:L-tau-shift}
\end{equation}
In either case,
\[
 L(\tau n)\equiv L(n)+1\pmod2.
\]
Because \(\tau n\in N_1\), applying \eqref{eq:L-a-shift} to \(\tau n\)
also gives the exact formula
\begin{equation}
 L([a]\tau n)=L(\tau n)+2a.
 \label{eq:L-a-tau-shift}
\end{equation}
Finally, the definition of Weinstein's involution \(\sigma\) adds \(1\) to
every Zeckendorf index on \(N_1\).  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 Z(\sigma n)=Z(n)+1,
 \qquad L(\sigma n)=L(n)+1
 \qquad(n\in N_1).
 \label{eq:L-sigma-shift}
\end{equation}

Fix \(g\in G(k)\) and a layer index \(j\).  By
\eqref{eq:L-a-shift} and \eqref{eq:L-a-tau-shift}, the largest indices in the
two \(N_1\)-branches are
\[
 L([a]g)=L(g)+2a,
 \qquad
 L([a]\tau g)=L(\tau g)+2a,
\]
and hence range over the two progressions
\[
 \{L(g)+2a:a\ge0\},
 \qquad
 \{L(\tau g)+2a:a\ge0\}.
\]
Each progression contains at most one occurrence of \(j\), and
\eqref{eq:L-tau-shift} gives them opposite parities.  Their union therefore
contains at most one occurrence of \(j\).  By \eqref{eq:L-sigma-shift}, the
two \(\sigma\)-branches meet layer \(j\) exactly when their corresponding
\(N_1\)-branches meet layer \(j-1\), so together they contribute at most one
additional point.  Thus the orbit of \(g\) contributes at most two points to
every layer, including the early layers.  The freeness of Weinstein's action
and the fundamental-domain property of \(G(k)\) make the generating orbits
disjoint.  Summing over them yields
\[
 N_j(k)\le2\lvert G(k)\rvert=2\Psi(k).
\]
We record the endpoint needed for eventual equality.  Weinstein's
maximal-generator lemma states that every \(g\in G(k)\) satisfies
\(g\le f_{2k}-2<f_{2k}\).  Since a Zeckendorf partition with largest index
at least \(2k\) has value at least \(f_{2k}\), it follows that
\begin{equation}
 L(g)\le2k-1.
 \label{eq:generator-largest-index-bound}
\end{equation}
Together with \eqref{eq:L-tau-shift}, this implies that the two
\(N_1\)-progressions contain exactly one point of every layer \(j\ge2k\).
They also contain one point of layer \(2k-1\): if \(L(g)\) is odd, use the
first progression, while if \(L(g)\) is even, the odd initial index
\(L(\tau g)=L(g)\mathbin{\pm}1\) is at most \(2k-1\).  Therefore, for every
\(j\ge2k\), the \(N_1\)-branches contribute one point in layer \(j\), and
their \(\sigma\)-images contribute one point because the corresponding
\(N_1\)-branches meet layer \(j-1\).  The two sheets are disjoint, so each
generating orbit contributes exactly two points.  Hence
\(N_j(k)=2\Psi(k)\) for \(j\ge2k\).  Weinstein's Theorem~5.1 supplies the
same conclusion away from the endpoint; the preceding argument proves the
additional case \(j=2k\).

Finally, Weinstein often writes the auxiliary layer with both endpoints.
The shared endpoint has partition value \(1\), so this distinction is
irrelevant for \(k\ge2\); the level \(1\) endpoint was assigned directly
above.  This completes the treatment of endpoints and early layers.
\end{proof}

We now show that an all-real analyticity ansatz cannot hold.  The obstruction
is a proved frozen half-line for the Fibonacci partition spectrum.  Let
\(\sigma_0>2\) be the unique real solution of
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\zeta(\sigma_0-1)}{\zeta(\sigma_0)}=2.
 \label{eq:freezing-exponent}
\end{equation}
Numerically, \(\sigma_0=2.478750785733960\ldots\).
The number \(-\sigma_0\) is initially obtained as a Dirichlet-series freezing
bound.  The weighted renewal argument in
\Cref{thm:exact-negative-pressure} subsequently proves that it is the exact
zero-pressure boundary.

\begin{theorem}[Negative-temperature freezing]
\label{thm:negative-temperature-freezing}
For every real \(t\le-\sigma_0\), the pressure exists unconditionally and
\[
 P(t):=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log S_t(m)=0.
\]
Consequently there is no real-analytic function on \(\RR\) which agrees with
the finite-window pressure limits wherever those limits exist and also
satisfies \(P(0)=\log\varphi\).  In particular, no everywhere finite pressure
extension can be both real analytic and strictly convex on \(\RR\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(s>2\), put
\[
 A(s):=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\phi_{\rm E}(n)}{n^s}
 =\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}-1,
\]
where \(\phi_{\rm E}\) is Euler's totient function.  Weinstein's free-monoid
decomposition and Tonelli's theorem give the following equality in
\([0,+\infty]\):
\begin{equation}
 1+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Psi(k)}{k^s}
 =\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}A(s)^r.
 \label{eq:weinstein-level-dirichlet-series}
\end{equation}
The geometric series is finite exactly when \(A(s)<1\), equivalently when
\(\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s)<2\); in that region its value is
\((2-\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s))^{-1}\).  The totient Dirichlet series also shows
that the ratio is strictly decreasing from \(+\infty\) to \(1\) on
\((2,\infty)\).  Hence \Cref{eq:freezing-exponent} has exactly one solution,
and \Cref{eq:weinstein-level-dirichlet-series} is finite precisely for
\(s>\sigma_0\).

By \Cref{thm:fiber-partition-interval}, the collision sum is exactly the sum
of \(R(n)^t\) over two adjacent Fibonacci layers:
\begin{equation}
 S_t(m)=\sum_{n=F_{m+1}-1}^{F_{m+3}-2}R(n)^t.
 \label{eq:real-collision-two-layers}
\end{equation}
If \(t<-\sigma_0\), the uniform layer-count lemma and
\Cref{eq:weinstein-level-dirichlet-series} give, uniformly in \(m\),
\[
 1\le S_t(m)
 \le 2+4\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\Psi(k)k^t<\infty.
\]
Thus \(m^{-1}\log S_t(m)\to0\).

At \(t=-\sigma_0\), fix \(\varepsilon>0\).  Only levels \(k\le D_m\)
occur, and for each such level
\[
 k^{-\sigma_0}\le D_m^\varepsilon k^{-(\sigma_0+\varepsilon)}.
\]
The same layer-count estimate therefore gives
\[
 S_{-\sigma_0}(m)
 \le 2+4D_m^{\varepsilon}
       \sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\Psi(k)}{k^{\sigma_0+\varepsilon}}.
\]
The series is finite, while
\(m^{-1}\log D_m\to\frac12\log\varphi\) by
\Cref{thm:unconditional-extremal-fibers}.  Therefore
\[
 0\le\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log S_{-\sigma_0}(m)
 \le\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log\varphi.
\]
Letting \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) proves the endpoint assertion.

Finally, an analytic function which vanishes on the open interval
\(( -\infty,-\sigma_0)\) vanishes identically, whereas
\[
 P(0)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log|X_m|=\log\varphi.
\]
This proves the analytic and strict-convexity obstructions.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[No globally analytic compact-operator representation]
\label{prop:no-analytic-compact-representation}
There do not exist a complex Banach space \(\mathcal B\), a connected open
set \(U\subset\mathbb C\) containing \(\RR\), and an operator-norm analytic
map
\[
 z\longmapsto\mathcal L_z\in\mathcal K(\mathcal B)
 \qquad(z\in U)
\]
into the compact operators with the following properties.  For every real
\(t\), the operator \(\mathcal L_t\) has a positive algebraically simple
isolated eigenvalue \(r(t)\); for every \(t_0\in\RR\), this eigenvalue is
separated from the remainder of the spectrum by a positive gap uniformly for
\(t\) in some real neighborhood of \(t_0\); and the family represents the
finite-window sums in the exponential sense
\[
 \lim_{m\to\infty}\frac1m\log S_t(m)=\log r(t)
 \qquad(t\in\RR).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For each \(m\ge1\), the function
\[
 p_m(t):=\frac1m\log S_t(m)
\]
is convex on \(\RR\).  Indeed, all fiber multiplicities are positive by the
interval-completeness lemma, and H\"older's inequality gives the defining
convexity inequality for \(S_t(m)\).  The representation hypothesis says that
\(p_m(t)\) converges pointwise on \(\RR\) to
\[
 P(t):=\log r(t).
\]
The finite pointwise limit \(P\) is therefore convex.  A finite convex function
on the open interval \(\RR\) is continuous, so \(r(t)=e^{P(t)}\) is continuous
on \(\RR\).

Fix \(t_0\in\RR\).  Choose a positively oriented contour \(\Gamma\) enclosing
\(r(t_0)\) and no other point of the spectrum of \(\mathcal L_{t_0}\).  For
complex \(z\) sufficiently close to \(t_0\), the contour remains in the
resolvent set and the Riesz projection
\[
 \Pi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma
  (\lambda I-\mathcal L_z)^{-1}\,d\lambda
\]
is analytic.  Its rank is locally constant and equals the algebraic
multiplicity of \(r(t_0)\), hence is one.  Analytic perturbation theory
\cite[Chap.~VII, Sec.~1]{Kato1995} therefore supplies a unique analytic
eigenvalue branch \(\mu(z)\) inside \(\Gamma\), with
\(\mu(t_0)=r(t_0)\).  The continuity of \(r(t)\) puts \(r(t)\) inside
\(\Gamma\) for all real \(t\) sufficiently close to \(t_0\); the local
spectral separation and rank-one Riesz projection then force
\(r(t)=\mu(t)\).  Thus \(r\), and hence \(t\mapsto\log r(t)\), is locally real
analytic at every \(t_0\).

The representation hypothesis makes this function equal to \(P(t)\).  By
\Cref{thm:negative-temperature-freezing}, it vanishes on
\(( -\infty,-\sigma_0)\), and therefore vanishes identically.  This
contradicts \(P(0)=\log\varphi\).
\end{proof}

The all-real renewal analysis in \Cref{thm:all-real-pressure} shows that the
pressure is real analytic and strictly convex on the positive-pressure phase,
but it also shows that the present freezing transition has a genuine corner:
the right derivative at \(-\sigma_0\) is strictly positive.  Thus the former
differentiability hypothesis at the transition is false, and the
G\"artner--Ellis theorem does not by itself provide lower bounds for slopes in
the interval between the two one-sided derivatives.  A full large deviation
principle across this coexistence interval remains open; no unconditional full
LDP is asserted here.
