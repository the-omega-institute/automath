1. Paper A
Estimated length: 30–34 pages
A clean Paper A would need approximately:


5–6 pages of introduction, history, and contribution boundary;


the present Brocot section, roughly 8 pages;


4–6 pages setting up the minimal Weinstein generator renewal needed for the application;


5–6 pages for the critical tail, renewal expansion, stable law, and Fibonacci consequences;


3–4 pages for conclusion and references.


Some material now spread through Sections 5–7 would have to be consolidated, so this is not simply “pages 15–22 plus pages 65–67.” But it should remain comfortably near 32 pages.
TAMS: 55–65%, central estimate 60%
Yes: Paper A would clear a bar that the 73-page article probably would not.
That is the decisive fact.
Paper A has a coherent theorem architecture:


a structural total-variation condensation law for a recognized continued-fraction family;


the exact context calculation and correction of Dushistova’s leading coefficient;


the scalar denominator-layer asymptotic produced by the same proof;


an arithmetic critical specialization giving the exact regularly varying tail;


a stable-renewal and noninteger finite-size consequence for the standard Fibonacci partition function.


The total-variation theorem itself gives the joint context law, defect, location, side lengths, and denominator factorization, rather than merely a partition-sum estimate. The manuscript is appropriately restrained in saying that these are structural consequences specific to the nonmultiplicative continuant model, not a new universal condensation mechanism.  
The scalar consequence then corrects the leading coefficient of the pre-existing local sum while disclaiming the remaining coefficients.  At the critical point, that arithmetic result supplies the tail constant, the renewal correction of order m3−σ0​, and the stable domain of attraction.  
The fact that the downstream application uses the scalar corollary rather than the full total-variation conclusion is not a coherence defect in Paper A. A paper may prove a stronger structural theorem and then use one sharp corollary in an application. The problem in the 73-page version is not that logical fact; it is that another 40-plus pages develop a largely autonomous second theory around it.
Paper A would therefore have:


substantially higher significance density;


a single visible arithmetic-probabilistic mechanism;


no competition between the Brocot theorem and recovered pressure results;


a correction that functions as part of a proof mechanism rather than as a separate priority headline;


a natural reason to include the Fibonacci material.


I would not call it a certain TAMS paper. The remaining rejection route is significance: a referee could still decide that the total-variation theorem is a specialized sharpening of a known scalar asymptotic and that the stable-renewal application, though exact, is too narrow. But after the split, that becomes a genuinely contestable judgment rather than an almost automatic coherence objection.
JNT: 82–90%, central estimate 86%
Paper A is very strong for JNT. It has classical number-theoretic objects, an exact correction to an existing continued-fraction calculation, a structural probability theorem, explicit zeta constants, and a new critical-renewal consequence. Its likely JNT failure modes would be a serious priority collision or a problem with the correction, not scope or architecture.
Thus the rational submission sequence for Paper A is TAMS first, JNT as a strong fallback.
2. Paper B
Estimated length: 42–48 pages
Paper B is not merely residue. It contains several autonomous pieces:


the affine coefficient-spectrum correspondence;


the pointwise partition-difference formula;


the two-interval identity;


the complete finite-window transfer of the known extremal classification;


exact local spectral stabilization;


the critical finite-window Gibbs and coexistence laws;


the nonexposed-slope orbit-filling argument completing the full LDP;


the joint cost/multiplicity LDP and its affine coexistence face.


The manuscript’s own theorem summary shows that the exact affine and interval correspondences are pointwise finite identities, while the local-spectrum and critical-law results are genuine finite-window statements.  The large-deviation theorem contains a real new step: the pressure corner prevents a black-box Gärtner–Ellis lower bound, and the orbit-filling construction supplies the missing nonexposed slopes. 
Its weakness is equally clear. Much of the pressure theory is explicitly imported from the golden-ratio Bernoulli-convolution literature, and the extremal classification is transferred rather than newly proved. The manuscript correctly distinguishes those recoveries from the surviving contributions. 
TAMS: 10–20%
Paper B alone is not a plausible TAMS package in my judgment. Its best results are technically serious, but the article would still be organized around:


a bespoke finite-window fold;


several elegant but elementary exact correspondences;


transferred extremal information;


a pressure theorem whose principal phase diagram is prior work;


new refinements and LDP completion built around that framework.


That is substantial specialist mathematics, but not enough field-level displacement for TAMS once the Brocot theorem has been removed.
JNT: 50–65%
JNT is realistic but not automatic. Paper B would have to be rebuilt around the exact finite-window correspondence and the nonexposed-slope LDP—not around the recovered pressure or the transferred maximum formulas.
I would put its median outcome slightly below the median outcome of the present combined article, because it loses the Brocot theorem as its strongest externally recognizable result. But it remains a credible JNT submission.
Respectable specialist venue: 80–90%
Splitting does not cost the authors a publishable paper. It relocates Paper B.
Even if JNT declined it on significance or density grounds, the exact correspondences, local spectral results, and completed LDP are enough for a respectable specialist venue in number theory, numeration, arithmetic dynamics, or probabilistic combinatorics. This is not a collection of appendices that survives only because Theorem A is nearby.
The important qualification is editorial rather than mathematical: Paper B must stop presenting every recovered pressure statement, transferred extremal result, and auxiliary interface at equal visual weight. Its publishable core is narrower than its present theorem count, but the core is real.

There is one viable third architecture:
Keep in the main article


the present Section 2 in substance;

the weighted generator renewal identity, preferably in a one-layer form first;


the exact critical denominator-layer tail and constant;


the stable law;


the m3−σ0​ correction;


at most a short finite-window corollary obtained from the two-layer identity.


Move out of the main article
The following current material should move as a single defined block:


essentially all of current Sections 3 and 4: the residue fold, Fourier formula, affine subset-sum transfer, and partition-difference correspondence;


Section 5.1: the full extremal transfer, maximizing-residue tables, and recurrence;


Proposition 5.5 and the recovered frozen-pressure discussion;


Theorems 6.1–6.5: local spectrum, unnormalized limits, Gibbs pole, Tauberian orbit law, and transition-strip bounds;


Lemmas 6.7–6.8 and Proposition 6.9: Knauf normalization and pressure recovery;


Theorem 6.10: the full finite-window LDP;


Proposition 6.12 and the active-cutoff interface;


Theorems 7.1 and 7.2: the full one-layer and joint large-deviation packages.


The main article would then be approximately 37–42 pages, with a 28–34-page supplement.
That would raise the TAMS probability to roughly 45–55%. It would improve the first-read density and make the main theorem spine obvious. But I do not recommend it.
First, the supplement would contain almost a complete second paper. Reviewers would still see that fact, and some would simply request a split. Second, the manuscript already has a separately compiled supplement for conditional arithmetic, dyadic, and integer-moment consequences; adding another large autonomous block would produce an awkward main-paper-plus-two-shadow-papers package.   Third, this option destroys most of Paper B’s publication value while gaining less TAMS probability than an actual split.
A modest supplement move—only Fourier details, extremal tables, pressure-recovery proofs, and the prime-support interface—would save perhaps 12–16 pages, but it would not cure the two-narrative problem. The article would remain a Brocot paper followed by a large autonomous Fibonacci thermodynamics paper.
So the third option exists, but it is dominated by splitting.
4. What I would do as an author
I would split.
I would submit Paper A to TAMS. I would prepare Paper B separately for JNT, with its introduction centered on the exact finite-window correspondence and the completion of the LDP across the freezing corner.
I would not submit the present combined article directly to JNT merely because that is the predicted median outcome. That is the safer one-paper decision, but it gives away the principal option value:


Paper A becomes more likely than not to pass TAMS after the split;


it has a strong JNT fallback;


Paper B remains independently publishable and has a real JNT chance;


the split is intellectually natural rather than artificial, because the manuscript itself now states the logical autonomy of the two sides.


The asymmetric-dependency revision has therefore done more than improve the introduction. It has supplied the authors with the correct cut line.
My author decision would be: two papers, Paper A to TAMS first, Paper B to JNT.
