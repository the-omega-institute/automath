\section{Discussion and scope}\label{sec:discussion-new}

The paper gives a complete exact worked analysis of one quartic spectral family.
Its main outputs are all tied to the specialization \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\): the elliptic normalization of the projective quartic, the exact finite branch values, the real-spectral phase diagram, the proof that the positive branch is globally dominant on \(y>0\), and the endpoint cosine-law asymptotics at \(y=0\).
The degree-growth law, the boundary zero at \(y=-1\), and the quarter-power splitting at projective infinity are supporting consequences and consistency checks for this fixed family rather than separate headline classifications or general theories.
Section~\ref{sec:genus-one} also records three auxiliary balanced-family observations that organize the computation: the uniform Weierstrass reduction of Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family}, the finite real-projection criterion of Theorem~\ref{thm:balanced-real-projection} and its closed comparison algorithm Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-finite-comparison-algorithm} as applied to this specialization, and the fixed neighbouring-collision normalization of Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization}.
Those statements are finite algebraic infrastructure for the specialization, not an invitation to read the article as a general classification of balanced quartics or of the residual one-parameter family obtained when the second eigenvalue is not fixed.

Relative to Biggs's equimodular-curve picture \cite{Biggs2002}, the Beraha--Kahane--Weiss framework, and the recurrence literature of Tran, Forg\'acs--Tran, Tran--Zumba, Ndikubwayo, and related authors, the gain here comes from specializing completely to one quartic.
That specialization makes it possible to write the normalization, the branch values, the real component images, and the dominant positive branch in closed form.
The novelty is the closed-form integration of elliptic normalization, branch geometry, dominance analysis, and endpoint zero asymptotics in a family where the relevant geometry is still tractable.

One conceptual point emerging from the calculation is the separation between the full ramification locus and the dominant-modulus envelope.
At \(y=0\), the branch collision occurs on the dominant branch and governs the first edge zeros.
At \(y=1\) and \(y=y_{\mathrm{sub}}\), the collisions are real but subdominant and therefore do not produce the same boundary behavior.
The real-spectral phase diagram makes this distinction visible, while the localized two-branch oscillation law of Section~\ref{sec:positive-maximal-modulus-branch} converts it into the odd-integer endpoint quantization for fixed indices, equivalently for fixed compact endpoint windows.

At the opposite end of the parameter range, Appendix~\ref{app:projective-infinity} records that the large-\(y\) expansion is controlled by a double tangency at projective infinity, producing the quarter-power splitting of the dominant root.
Together with the degree law of Section~\ref{sec:support-law}, this gives only a recurrence-side consistency check: the top-degree sector is a rigid algebraic boundary rather than an exponentially large multiplicity regime.

The calculation also indicates natural questions beyond the present scope.
One may ask whether this quartic family has a distinguished combinatorial or statistical-mechanical origin, which would attach an interpretation to the coefficients rather than merely to the spectral curve.
One may also vary the balanced parameters: the real branch-locus quintic \eqref{eq:balanced-quintic} and the interval comparison of Theorem~\ref{thm:balanced-real-projection} provide finite data for each smooth member, but this article uses them only for the specialization and does not develop a global balanced-quartic classification.
Finally, the endpoint zero calculation is local in a fixed scaling window; a macroscopic zero-distribution theorem on the full negative interval, or a structural recurrence theory built from the exact support and boundary-zero identities, would require additional estimates and comparison arguments not present here.
