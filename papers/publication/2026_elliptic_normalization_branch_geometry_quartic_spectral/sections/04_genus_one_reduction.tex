\section{Genus-one reduction of the quartic spectral curve}\label{sec:genus-one}

The present spectral polynomial is treated through a finite algebraic reduction tailored to this quartic.
The substitution \(\xi=y-\lambda^2\) embeds it in a balanced four-parameter family whose coefficients are tuned so that the unwanted \(\lambda^4\), \(\xi\lambda^2\), and \(\lambda^2\) terms cancel simultaneously; the resulting Weierstrass model, real-projection comparisons, and double-root normalization are then specialized to \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\).

The argument has four layers.
First, the balanced-reduction layer proves Theorem~A: the change of variables, smoothness criterion, projective normalization, and ramification equation.
Second, the fixed-member real-projection layer gives the auxiliary finite criterion needed for one already chosen smooth real balanced member; its canonical specialization is the result named Theorem~B.
Third, the canonical-normalization layer proves Theorem~C, selecting the normalized double root by algebraic conjugation and scaling rather than by dominance.
Fourth, the specialization layer evaluates these algebraic certificates for the quartic \(\Pi\), producing the branch values and discriminant consequences used by Theorems~D and~E.
Preparatory lemmas in this section serve one of these four layers; Theorems~A--C are the labelled inputs carried forward to the later branch-geometry argument, while dominance and endpoint-zero assertions are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:positive-maximal-modulus-branch}.

\subsection{A balanced quartic family}

The affine normalization, squarefreeness criterion, and finite ramification equations for the balanced family are algebraic over every base field \(K\) of characteristic different from \(2\) and \(3\), as recorded in Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-base-field}.
The theorem and corollaries used later in the manuscript are stated over \(\CC\), obtained by taking \(K=\CC\) in that lemma.
The phrase ``smooth projective completion'' always means the smooth projective curve obtained from the affine Weierstrass model after the change of variables below; equivalently, for the plane quartic it is the normalization of the projective closure.
Statements about real ovals, real images, and interval comparisons are made only after the parameters \(a,c,d,g\) and the variable \(y\) are specialized to \(\RR\).
Complex branch and dominance statements use the complexification of that real curve.

\begin{lemma}[Base-field compatibility of the balanced normalization]\label{lem:balanced-base-field}
Let \(K\) be a field with \(\operatorname{char}K\ne2,3\), and let \(a,c,d,g\in K\).
The formulas
\[
\xi=y-\lambda^2,
\qquad
Y=2\xi-c,
\qquad
y=\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}
\]
define mutually inverse affine changes of variables over \(K\) between
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0
\]
and
\[
Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\]
Moreover this identification, the squarefreeness condition for the cubic on the right, and the finite ramification equation used below are preserved by every field extension \(L/K\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The displayed formulas involve only addition, multiplication, and division by \(2\), so they are defined over \(K\) because \(\operatorname{char}K\ne2\).
Substituting \(\xi=y-\lambda^2\) gives
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=\xi^2-c\xi+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g,
\]
and multiplying the equation \(\xi^2-c\xi+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g=0\) by \(4\) after setting \(Y=2\xi-c\) gives exactly
\[
Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\]
The inverse formula for \(y\) recovers \(\xi=(Y+c)/2\), hence recovers the original point, so the two affine curves are isomorphic over \(K\).
Squarefreeness of the cubic is equivalent to nonvanishing of its discriminant; because \(\operatorname{char}K\ne3\), the usual derivative and discriminant criterion for a cubic applies and remains unchanged after scalar extension.
On the smooth locus, differentiating the Weierstrass equation and the formula for \(y\) gives the same algebraic equation
\[
2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d
\]
for finite ramification; since this equation has coefficients in \(K\), its base change to any extension \(L/K\) is precisely the corresponding ramification equation over \(L\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Balanced quartic family]\label{prop:balanced-family}
For parameters \(a,c,d,g\in\CC\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-family}
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)
=
\lambda^4+a\lambda^3+(c-2y)\lambda^2+d\lambda+y(y-c)+g.
\end{equation}
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-xi}
\xi=y-\lambda^2,
\qquad
Y=2\xi-c=2y-2\lambda^2-c.
\end{equation}
Then the affine curve \(P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0\) is isomorphic to the affine curve
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-weierstrass}
Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\end{equation}
The inverse map is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-inverse}
y=\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}.
\end{equation}
If \(a\neq 0\) and the cubic polynomial
\[
F_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda)=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g
\]
has no repeated root, then the smooth projective completion \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) is a genus-one curve, and the function \(y\) defines a degree-\(4\) map
\[
y:E_{a,c,d,g}\to\mathbf P^1_y.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Using \(\xi=y-\lambda^2\), one rewrites \eqref{eq:balanced-family} as
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)
=
(y-\lambda^2)^2-c(y-\lambda^2)+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g
=
\xi^2-c\xi+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g.
\]
Thus
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0
\iff
\xi^2-c\xi+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g=0.
\]
Multiplying by \(4\) and completing the square gives
\[
(2\xi-c)^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\]
This is \eqref{eq:balanced-weierstrass} with \(Y=2\xi-c\); equivalently, it is the specialization of Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-base-field} to \(K=\CC\).
Conversely, from \(Y=2\xi-c\) one gets \(\xi=(Y+c)/2\), hence \eqref{eq:balanced-inverse}.
Therefore the affine change of variables \((\lambda,y)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,Y)\) is polynomial and invertible, so the two affine curves are isomorphic.

Assume now that \(a\neq 0\) and \(F_{a,c,d,g}\) has no repeated root.
Then \eqref{eq:balanced-weierstrass} is a smooth cubic Weierstrass model.
The projection \((\lambda,Y)\mapsto \lambda\) is a double cover of \(\mathbf P^1_\lambda\) branched at the three finite roots of \(F_{a,c,d,g}\) and, because \(\deg F_{a,c,d,g}=3\), also at infinity.
Hence there are four simple branch points.
By Riemann--Hurwitz,
\[
2g-2=2(-2)+4,
\]
so \(g=1\).

Let \(O\) be the point at infinity on the smooth projective completion.
For a cubic Weierstrass model one has
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(\lambda)=-2,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_O(Y)=-3.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:balanced-inverse},
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(y)
=
\operatorname{ord}_O\!\left(\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}\right)
=
\min(-4,-3)
=
-4.
\]
Therefore \(y\) has a unique pole of order \(4\) at \(O\), so the map \(y:E_{a,c,d,g}\to\mathbf P^1_y\) has degree \(4\).
\end{proof}


\begin{corollary}[Smooth genus-one condition]\label{cor:balanced-smooth}
For \(a,c,d,g\in\CC\) with \(a\neq 0\), the cubic \(F_{a,c,d,g}\) has no repeated root if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-genus-condition}
64d^3+27a(c^2-4g)^2\neq 0.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
F_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda)
=
-4a\left(\lambda^3+\frac{d}{a}\lambda+\frac{4g-c^2}{4a}\right).
\]
For a monic cubic \(u^3+pu+q\), the discriminant is \(-4p^3-27q^2\).
Substituting
\[
p=\frac{d}{a},
\qquad
q=\frac{4g-c^2}{4a}
\]
gives the condition
\[
-4\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)^3-27\left(\frac{4g-c^2}{4a}\right)^2\neq 0,
\]
which is equivalent to \eqref{eq:balanced-genus-condition} after clearing denominators.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Projective completion for the balanced quartic]\label{lem:balanced-projective-completion}
Assume \(a\neq0\) and \eqref{eq:balanced-genus-condition}.
Let \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\subset \mathbf P^2_{[\Lambda:W:Z]}\) be the projective closure of the affine plane quartic
\(P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0\), with \(\lambda=\Lambda/Z\) and \(y=W/Z\).
Let \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) be the smooth projective Weierstrass curve obtained from
\[
Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\]
Then the affine isomorphism of Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family} extends uniquely to a finite birational morphism
\[
\nu_{a,c,d,g}:E_{a,c,d,g}\longrightarrow \overline C_{a,c,d,g},
\]
and \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) is the nonsingular projective model of the function field of the original quartic, equivalently the normalization of \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family}, the affine quartic \(P_{a,c,d,g}=0\) and the affine Weierstrass curve are isomorphic on the common affine chart \(Z\neq0\).  We use here the standard normalization of a projective plane curve by the nonsingular projective model of its function field \cite[Chap.~I]{Miranda1995}.
Hence they have the same function field, namely
\[
\CC(\lambda,y)=\CC(\lambda,Y),
\qquad
Y=2y-2\lambda^2-c.
\]
The discriminant condition \eqref{eq:balanced-genus-condition} is equivalent to squarefreeness of the cubic by Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-smooth}; therefore the projective Weierstrass model \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) is smooth and projective.

We record the local form at infinity, since this is the point where the plane quartic closure is singular.
Homogenizing \eqref{eq:balanced-family} gives
\[
\Lambda^4+a\Lambda^3Z+(cZ-2W)\Lambda^2Z+d\Lambda Z^3+W(W-cZ)Z^2+gZ^4=0.
\]
On the line \(Z=0\) this equation is \(\Lambda^4=0\), so the plane closure has the unique point at infinity \([0:1:0]\).
Let \(O\) be the point at infinity of the smooth Weierstrass cubic.
For a cubic model of odd degree, or equivalently from the usual local parameter at \(O\),
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(\lambda)=-2,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_O(Y)=-3
\]
\cite{Silverman2009,Miranda1995}.
The inverse formula \eqref{eq:balanced-inverse} gives
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(y)
=
\operatorname{ord}_O\!\left(\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}\right)
=-4,
\]
because the summands have distinct orders \(-4\), \(-3\), and \(0\).
Thus, on the affine chart \(W\neq0\) at \([0:1:0]\), the local coordinates
\[
u=\frac{\Lambda}{W}=\frac{\lambda}{y},
\qquad
z=\frac{Z}{W}=\frac{1}{y}
\]
pull back to regular functions at \(O\) with
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(u)=2,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_O(z)=4.
\]
Hence the affine isomorphism has a well-defined limit at infinity, namely \([u:1:z]=[0:1:0]\), and the only point of \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) mapping to the line \(Z=0\) is \(O\), since \(y\) has no other pole.

The affine isomorphism gives a rational map from the smooth projective curve \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) to the projective curve \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
Because the source is nonsingular, a rational map from it to a projective variety has no indeterminacy at a codimension-one point; for a curve this means the map extends across the single point at infinity of the Weierstrass model.
Thus the rational map extends uniquely to a morphism \(\nu_{a,c,d,g}:E_{a,c,d,g}\to\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
Its restriction to the dense affine chart is the isomorphism above, so \(\nu_{a,c,d,g}\) is birational onto \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
Since a nonconstant morphism from a projective curve to a curve is finite, \(\nu_{a,c,d,g}\) is finite.
The source is normal because it is a smooth curve, and a finite birational morphism from a normal curve to an integral curve is the normalization.
Therefore \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) is the normalization of \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\), as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Finite ramification in the balanced family]\label{cor:balanced-ramification}
Let \(a\neq 0\) and assume \eqref{eq:balanced-genus-condition}.
Then the finite ramification points of the degree-\(4\) map \(y:E_{a,c,d,g}\to\mathbf P^1_y\) satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-ramification-eq}
2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d.
\end{equation}
Eliminating \(Y\) with \eqref{eq:balanced-weierstrass} yields the quintic equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-ramification}
4\lambda^2\bigl(c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g\bigr)-(3a\lambda^2+d)^2=0,
\end{equation}
equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced-quintic}
16a\lambda^5+9a^2\lambda^4+16d\lambda^3+(16g+6ad-4c^2)\lambda^2+d^2=0.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating \(\xi^2-c\xi+a\lambda^3+d\lambda+g=0\) gives
\[
(2\xi-c)\,\dd\xi+(3a\lambda^2+d)\,\dd\lambda=0.
\]
Since \(Y=2\xi-c\),
\[
\dd\xi=-\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{Y}\,\dd\lambda.
\]
Using \(y=\lambda^2+\xi\), we obtain
\[
\dd y
=
\left(2\lambda-\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{Y}\right)\dd\lambda
=
\frac{2\lambda Y-(3a\lambda^2+d)}{Y}\,\dd\lambda.
\]
Therefore the finite ramification condition is \eqref{eq:balanced-ramification-eq}.
Squaring and substituting \(Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g\) gives \eqref{eq:balanced-ramification}, and expanding gives \eqref{eq:balanced-quintic}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Balanced elliptic normalization and ramification theorem (Theorem A)]\label{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization}
For the balanced quartic family
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)
=
\lambda^4+a\lambda^3+(c-2y)\lambda^2+d\lambda+y(y-c)+g
\]
with \(a,c,d,g\in\CC\), put
\[
F_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda)=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g
\]
and let \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\subset\mathbf P^2_{[\lambda:W:Z]}\) be the projective closure of \(P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0\), where \(y=W/Z\).
Then the following normalization, degree, and finite-ramification conclusions hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The affine change of variables
\[
\xi=y-\lambda^2,
\qquad
Y=2\xi-c
\]
identifies \(P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)=0\) with the affine Weierstrass equation
\[
Y^2=F_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda),
\qquad
y=\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}.
\]
The inverse affine map is
\[
(\lambda,Y)\longmapsto
\left(\lambda,\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}\right).
\]
\item If \(a\ne0\), the smooth projective completion is a genus-one curve and the map \(y:E_{a,c,d,g}\to\mathbf P^1_y\) has degree \(4\) exactly when
\[
64d^3+27a(c^2-4g)^2\ne0.
\]
Equivalently, the cubic \(F_{a,c,d,g}\) is squarefree.
Under this hypothesis, \(E_{a,c,d,g}\) is the nonsingular projective model of the function field of the original quartic and is also the normalization of \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
The normalization map is induced on the affine chart by the inverse formula above and sends the point at infinity of the Weierstrass model to the unique point at infinity of \(\overline C_{a,c,d,g}\).
\item Under the same smoothness hypothesis, the finite ramification points of \(y\) are exactly the points satisfying
\[
2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d,
\]
or, after eliminating \(Y\),
\[
16a\lambda^5+9a^2\lambda^4+16d\lambda^3+(16g+6ad-4c^2)\lambda^2+d^2=0.
\]
For \(\lambda\ne0\), the corresponding critical value may be read without choosing the sign of \(Y\) as
\[
y_{\mathrm{crit}}(\lambda)
=
\lambda^2+\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{4\lambda}+\frac c2.
\]
The only finite critical points not represented by this rational critical-value formula can occur over \(\lambda=0\), and then the displayed differential equation forces \(d=0\); the value is read directly from the Weierstrass model as \(y=(c\pm\sqrt{F_{a,c,d,g}(0)})/2\), with the two signs coinciding at the endpoint value \(c/2\) when \(F_{a,c,d,g}(0)=0\).
\end{enumerate}
Thus Theorem~A is the normalization theorem used later: one starts with a balanced quartic, replaces it by the explicit squarefree Weierstrass model, uses the displayed degree-four map \(y\), and obtains every finite ramification value from the single quintic together with the stated \(\lambda=0\) exception.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Part~(1) is Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family}: the displayed substitution gives a polynomial inverse and hence an affine isomorphism, with inverse formula \(y=\lambda^2+(Y+c)/2\).
For \(a\ne0\), the cubic on the right has no repeated root precisely under the discriminant condition in Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-smooth}; in that case Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family} identifies the smooth projective completion as genus one and computes the order-four pole of \(y\) at infinity, giving degree \(4\).
Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-projective-completion} supplies the projective comparison with the original plane quartic: the same smooth curve is the normalization of the quartic's projective closure.
Finally, Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-ramification} differentiates the Weierstrass equation and the inverse formula for \(y\), obtaining \(2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d\); substituting \(Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g\) gives the displayed quintic.
When \(\lambda\ne0\), the ramification equation gives \(Y=(3a\lambda^2+d)/(2\lambda)\), and substituting this into \(y=\lambda^2+(Y+c)/2\) gives the displayed formula for \(y_{\mathrm{crit}}(\lambda)\).
If \(\lambda=0\), the ramification equation reduces to \(d=0\); the possible values then come directly from \(Y^2=F_{a,c,d,g}(0)\) and \(y=(Y+c)/2\), with the endpoint specialization \(F_{a,c,d,g}(0)=0\) giving the single value \(c/2\).
The preceding paragraph gives a clause-by-clause implication from the cited results to the theorem, including the finite critical-value convention used by the real-projection criterion.
Together these algebraic calculations give the asserted normalization theorem: the change of variables identifies the function field with the Weierstrass model, the discriminant calculation gives the smooth genus-one and degree-four criterion, the projective-completion lemma identifies the nonsingular projective model with the normalization of the quartic closure, and differentiating the degree-four map gives exactly the stated finite ramification equation.
Thus the theorem is a self-contained normalization and ramification result for the rest of the paper.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Real projection structure for fixed balanced members}

This subsection supplies the real-projection input for Theorem~B.
The general balanced-family material is only a short fixed-member preliminary: once a smooth real member has been chosen, endpoints and real critical values determine the interval image of each real oval.
The result used later is the canonical proposition for \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\), where all comparisons are displayed sign inequalities or named polynomial factorizations.

\begin{lemma}[Real Weierstrass ovals and regular values]\label{lem:real-weierstrass-oval-covering-count}
Let \(F\in\RR[\lambda]\) be a squarefree cubic with leading coefficient nonzero, and let \(E\) be the smooth projective real curve obtained from
\[
Y^2=F(\lambda).
\]
For each closed connected component \(J\) of \(\{\lambda\in\RR:F(\lambda)\ge0\}\), the real points lying over \(J\) form one connected component of \(E(\RR)\), except that the unique unbounded component is completed by the point at infinity.
Consequently \(E(\RR)\) has one component when \(F\) has one real root and two components when \(F\) has three real roots.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On the interior of a component \(J\), the equation has the two analytic sheets \(Y=\pm\sqrt{F(\lambda)}\); this is the standard real-locus description of a nonsingular Weierstrass double cover \cite[Chap.~III]{Silverman2009}.
If \(\beta\) is a finite endpoint of \(J\), then \(F(\beta)=0\) and squarefreeness gives \(F'(\beta)\ne0\); with a real local coordinate \(u\) on the normalization, one has \(\lambda-\beta=u^2\) up to multiplication by a positive unit on the side where \(F\ge0\), and the two signs of \(Y\) are glued through \(u=0\).
At infinity the standard Weierstrass parameter gives \(\lambda=u^{-2}+O(u^{-1})\) and \(Y=u^{-3}+O(u^{-2})\), so the two ends of the unbounded real component are joined by the single point at infinity.
Thus each closed component of \(\{F\ge0\}\) gives exactly the asserted real oval, and the count of ovals follows from the elementary alternatives for the real roots of a squarefree cubic; equivalently this is the standard real-locus theorem for a hyperelliptic double cover of \(\mathbf P^1_\RR\) branched at the real zeros of \(F\) and at infinity \cite[Chap.~I, Secs.~3--4]{Miranda1995}, \cite[Chap.~III, Sec.~1]{Silverman2009}.

\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Proper real covering away from the finite transition set]\label{lem:real-projection-proper-covering}
Let \(F\in\RR[\lambda]\) be a squarefree cubic with leading coefficient nonzero, let \(E\) be the smooth projective real curve \(Y^2=F(\lambda)\), and fix one real component \(E_J(\RR)\) lying over a closed component \(J\subset\{F\ge0\}\).
Let
\[
f:E\longrightarrow \mathbf P^1
\]
be a nonconstant real morphism whose finite critical values on \(E_J(\RR)\), endpoint values over finite endpoints of \(J\), and limiting finite values at real points of \(E_J(\RR)\) above infinity form a finite set \(T_J\subset\RR\).
Assume that, if \(E_J(\RR)\) contains the point \(O\) at infinity and \(f(O)=\infty\), then along both real ends approaching \(O\) one has \(f(p)\to +\infty\).
Then the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every connected component \(I\) of \(\RR\setminus T_J\), the restriction
\[
f:f^{-1}(I)\cap E_J(\RR)\longrightarrow I
\]
is a proper local homeomorphism and hence a finite covering.
Consequently the real fibre count over \(I\) is constant.
\item If \(E_J(\RR)\) is compact, then \(f(E_J(\RR))\) is the closed interval whose endpoints are the minimum and maximum of the values in \(T_J\cap f(E_J(\RR))\).
If \(E_J(\RR)\) is the noncompact affine component completed by \(O\) and \(f(O)=\infty\) with the preceding two-ended limit, then
\[
f(E_J(\RR))=[\min(T_J\cap f(E_J(\RR))),\infty).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First suppose that \(I\) is a connected component of \(\RR\setminus T_J\).
No point of \(f^{-1}(I)\cap E_J(\RR)\) is a critical point of the real analytic map \(f|_{E_J(\RR)}\), and no endpoint or deleted infinite limiting value lies over \(I\).
The real inverse-function theorem therefore makes the restriction a local homeomorphism.

It remains to record properness, including the unbounded oval.
Let \(K\subset I\) be compact.
If \(E_J(\RR)\) is compact, then \(f^{-1}(K)\cap E_J(\RR)\) is compact.
If \(E_J(\RR)\) is the affine part of the unbounded oval, its projective completion is obtained by adding the single point \(O\).
When \(f(O)=\infty\) and both real ends satisfy \(f(p)	o+\infty\), there is a punctured neighborhood of \(O\) on this real oval whose image is disjoint from the bounded set \(K\); the remaining closed subset of the projective oval is compact.
Thus the inverse image of \(K\) is compact also in the unbounded case.
The restriction over \(I\) is therefore a proper local homeomorphism.
We use only the elementary proper-local-homeomorphism form of the covering theorem for one-manifolds.
For \(t_0\in I\), the fibre over \(t_0\) is compact and discrete, hence finite.
Choose pairwise disjoint coordinate intervals around its points on which \(f\) is a homeomorphism onto intervals about \(t_0\).
After shrinking the target interval, properness excludes any additional preimage outside those finitely many coordinate intervals, for otherwise a convergent subnet in the compact inverse image would limit to another point over \(t_0\).
Thus a neighborhood of \(t_0\) is evenly covered.
This proves that a proper local homeomorphism between Hausdorff one-manifolds is a finite covering, and the covering degree is constant because \(I\) is connected.

For the image statement, a continuous function on a compact real oval has compact connected image, hence a closed interval, and its endpoints are attained at endpoint or critical points because every interior extremum is critical.
On the unbounded oval, the two-ended hypothesis implies properness as a map to \(\RR\) and forces the image to be closed and unbounded above.
The same extremum argument gives a finite minimum from the listed endpoint or critical values; hence the image is \([\min(T_J\cap f(E_J(\RR))),\infty)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Endpoint and critical values on a real component]\label{lem:real-component-extrema-enumeration}
Let \(a,c,d,g\in\RR\), let
\[
F(\lambda)=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g
\]
be squarefree, and let \(J\) be a closed connected component of \(\{\lambda\in\RR:F(\lambda)\ge0\}\).
On the real curve \(Y^2=F(\lambda)\), consider
\[
y(\lambda,Y)=\lambda^2+\frac{Y+c}{2}.
\]
Every finite endpoint value or finite extremum of \(y\) on the real component above \(J\) is one of the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an endpoint value \(\beta^2+c/2\) with \(\beta\in J\) and \(F(\beta)=0\);
\item a non-endpoint critical value with \(Y\ne0\) and \(\lambda\ne0\), where
\[
2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d,
\qquad
y=\lambda^2+\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{4\lambda}+\frac c2;
\]
\item an exceptional value over \(\lambda=0\), which occurs only when \(0\in J\) and \(d=0\), and is then \((c\pm\sqrt{F(0)})/2\), with the two signs coinciding when \(F(0)=0\).
\end{enumerate}
Thus no endpoint or interior extremum is lost by separating the cases \(F(\beta)=0\), \(\lambda\ne0\), and \(\lambda=0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The classical input used here is only the following local real form of a squarefree Weierstrass cubic: over the interior of \(J\), where \(F>0\), the two real branches are the analytic graphs \(Y=\pm\sqrt{F(\lambda)}\), while at an endpoint \(\beta\) with \(F(\beta)=0\) and \(F'(\beta)\neq0\) the normalization supplies one real analytic arc with \(\lambda-\beta\) a square of a local parameter \cite{Silverman2009,Miranda1995}.
Thus the real component above \(J\) is a one-dimensional real analytic manifold, with the two sheets over the interior of \(J\) glued at the endpoints where \(F=0\); all endpoint and critical-value enumeration below is then the displayed differentiation of the present function \(y\).
At such an endpoint, \(Y=0\), and substituting in \(y=\lambda^2+(Y+c)/2\) gives \(\beta^2+c/2\).

On the interior of \(J\), one has \(Y\ne0\), so \(\lambda\) is a valid local coordinate on each sheet.
Differentiating
\[
Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g
\]
gives
\[
2Y\,\frac{\dd Y}{\dd\lambda}=-12a\lambda^2-4d,
\]
and hence
\[
\frac{\dd y}{\dd\lambda}=2\lambda-\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{Y}.
\]
Therefore an interior extremum satisfies \(2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d\).
If \(\lambda\ne0\), substituting \(Y=(3a\lambda^2+d)/(2\lambda)\) gives the displayed rational critical value.
Squaring the same relation and using \(Y^2=F(\lambda)\) is precisely the ramification equation \eqref{eq:balanced-ramification}.

It remains to handle \(\lambda=0\), where the rational expression is not available.
The derivative condition at \(\lambda=0\) becomes \(-d/Y=0\) when \(F(0)>0\), so it is equivalent to \(d=0\); the two real points over \(0\) then give \((c\pm\sqrt{F(0)})/2\).
If \(F(0)=0\), the point over \(0\) is an endpoint already included in the first case, and when also \(d=0\) its value is the single number \(c/2\).
These alternatives cover all points of the real component, proving the exhaustion statement.
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}[Fixed-member endpoint-and-extrema test]\label{def:balanced-projection-datum}\label{prop:balanced-transition-support-reduction}\label{prop:balanced-projection-datum-complete}\label{prop:balanced-real-projection-fixed-extrema}\label{prop:balanced-real-projection}\label{rem:balanced-projection-reading}\label{rem:balanced-finite-comparison-algorithm}\label{rem:balanced-real-interval-alternatives}\label{rem:balanced-fixed-criterion-complete}\label{cor:fixed-member-projection-comparison}
Fix one smooth real balanced member with $a\ne0$, and let
\[
F(\lambda)=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g,
\qquad
R_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda)=16a\lambda^5+9a^2\lambda^4+16d\lambda^3+(16g+6ad-4c^2)\lambda^2+d^2.
\]
For each closed component $J\subset\{F\ge0\}$ define the reduced transition set
\begin{align}\label{eq:balanced-real-extrema-set}
\mathcal V(J)
&=
\{\beta^2+\tfrac c2: \beta\in J,\ F(\beta)=0\}
\notag\\
&\quad\cup
\left\{\lambda^2+\frac{3a\lambda^2+d}{4\lambda}+\frac c2:
\lambda\in J\setminus\{0\},\ R_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda)=0\right\}
\notag\\
&\quad\cup\mathcal Z(J),
\end{align}
where $\mathcal Z(J)$ consists of $(c\pm\sqrt{F(0)})/2$ if $0\in J$, $d=0$, and $F(0)>0$, consists of $\{c/2\}$ if $0\in J$, $d=0$, and $F(0)=0$, and is empty otherwise.
Then $\mathcal V(J)$ is exactly the reduced set of finite endpoint values and finite critical values of the morphism $y=\lambda^2+(Y+c)/2$ on the real component above $J$.
The fibre count over that component is constant on each connected component of $\RR\setminus\mathcal V(J)$.
If the component is compact, its image is the closed interval with endpoints $\min\mathcal V(J)$ and $\max\mathcal V(J)$; if it is the unbounded component, then $y$ has a pole of order $4$ at infinity and its image is $[\min\mathcal V(J),\infty)$.
Thus, in the two-oval case, the only internal-gap comparison is $M_{\mathrm c}\ge m_{\mathrm n}$, with $M_{\mathrm c}=\max\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm c})$ and $m_{\mathrm n}=\min\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm n})$.
This is only a fixed-member lemma; it is not a parameter-space chamber classification.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:real-component-extrema-enumeration} gives precisely the three displayed classes: endpoint values, the $\lambda\ne0$ critical values obtained from $2\lambda Y=3a\lambda^2+d$, and the exceptional $\lambda=0$ values.
Taking the reduced image forgets ramification multiplicities and coincident realizations, but interval endpoints and regular fibre counts depend only on the real value.
On any interval avoiding $\mathcal V(J)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:real-projection-proper-covering} makes the restriction a finite real covering, so the fibre count is constant.
The same lemma gives the compact-component image as the interval between its attained finite extrema.
On the unbounded component the Weierstrass parameter at $O$ gives $\lambda$ a pole of order $2$ and $Y$ a pole of order $3$, hence $y=\lambda^2+(Y+c)/2$ tends to $+\infty$ along both real ends; the finite lower endpoint is therefore $\min\mathcal V(J)$.
The two-oval alternative follows by taking the union of $[m_{\mathrm c},M_{\mathrm c}]$ and $[m_{\mathrm n},\infty)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Canonical quartic real projection]\label{cor:quartic-real-projection-framework}
For the specialization $(a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)$ the cubic $F(\lambda)=4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1$ has three distinct real roots $\beta_1<\beta_2<\beta_3$ (the discriminant $\Delta=37>0$ confirms this).
Thus $E_{-1,-1,1,0}(\RR)$ has two real ovals.
The finite obstruction framework for the real projection consists of
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm c})
&=
\left\{\beta_1^2-\frac12,\ \beta_2^2-\frac12,\ 1,\ y_{\mathrm{sub}}\right\},\\
\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm n})
&=
\left\{\beta_3^2-\frac12,\ 0\right\},
\end{aligned}
\]
where \(J_{\mathrm c}=[\beta_1,\beta_2]\), \(J_{\mathrm n}=[\beta_3,\infty)\), and \(y_{\mathrm{sub}}\) is the unique real zero of
\[
256y^3+411y^2+165y+32.
\]
Moreover
\[
\min\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm c})=y_{\mathrm{sub}},
\qquad
\max\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm c})=1,
\qquad
\min\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm n})=0.
\]
The compact oval has exactly the two real ramification points whose values are $y_{\mathrm{sub}}$ and $1$, and these give respectively the global minimum and maximum of $y$ on that oval.
Consequently the compact oval maps under $y$ to the interval $[y_{\mathrm{sub}},1]$, every regular interior value of this interval has exactly two real preimages on the compact oval, and the noncompact oval maps to $[0,+\infty)$.
Thus the total real image is $[y_{\mathrm{sub}},\infty)$ with an overlap on $[0,1]$.
The regular real-root count and its minimal transition table are not restated here; they are the advertised canonical real-projection statement, Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-b-real-projection-criterion}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For this specialization one has
\[
\{\lambda\in\RR:4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1\ge 0\}=[\beta_1,\beta_2]\cup[\beta_3,\infty),
\]
so \(J_{\mathrm{c}}=[\beta_1,\beta_2]\) and \(J_{\mathrm{n}}=[\beta_3,\infty)\).
The interval membership used below is explicit.
Since
\begin{gather*}
F(-9/8)<0,
\quad F(-1)>0,
\quad F(0)>0,\\
F(1/2)<0,
\quad F(3/4)<0,
\quad F(1)>0,
\end{gather*}
and \(F'(\lambda)=12\lambda^2-4\), the roots satisfy
\[
-\frac98<\beta_1<-1,
\qquad
0<\beta_2<\frac12,
\qquad
\frac34<\beta_3<1.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:ramification-poly} shows that the three finite ramification abscissae are $\lambda\in\{-1,1,\alpha\}$, where $\alpha\in(-\tfrac12,0)$ is the real root of $16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1$.
The cubic factor has exactly one real root because its derivative is \(\lambda(48\lambda-18)\), its local values at \(0\) and \(3/8\) are positive, and its limits at \(\pm\infty\) have opposite signs.
Since \(16(-1/2)^3-9(-1/2)^2+1<0\) and the value at \(0\) is positive, this root \(\alpha\) lies in \((-1/2,0)\).
Thus \(-1,\alpha\in J_{\mathrm c}\) and \(1\in J_{\mathrm n}\), while no other real ramification abscissa lies on either oval.

At a finite endpoint \(F(\beta_i)=0\), one has \(Y=0\), hence
\[
y(\beta_i,0)=\beta_i^2-\frac12.
\]
The displayed root bounds give
\[
\frac12<\beta_1^2-\frac12<\frac{49}{64}<1,
\qquad
-\frac12<\beta_2^2-\frac12<-\frac14,
\qquad
\frac{1}{16}<\beta_3^2-\frac12<\frac12.
\]
At the ramification abscissae the critical-value formula gives
\[
y(-1)=1,
\qquad
y(1)=0,
\qquad
y(\alpha)=y_{\mathrm{sub}}
=\frac{4\alpha^3-3\alpha^2-2\alpha+1}{4\alpha}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-branch-real-root}, this last number is the unique real zero of \(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\), hence \(y_{\mathrm{sub}}\in(-2,-1)\).
The required ordering follows from the displayed sign checks for \(F\), from the sign checks locating \(\alpha\), and from the polynomial sign isolation of \(y_{\mathrm{sub}}\); these are exact algebraic comparisons inside the proof.
Lemma~\ref{prop:balanced-real-projection-fixed-extrema} now shows, for this fixed member, that the displayed endpoint values and these three critical values are the complete finite obstruction framework for the real projection, giving precisely the two sets \(\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm c})\) and \(\mathcal V(J_{\mathrm n})\) in the statement.
Therefore on \(J_{\mathrm c}\) the finite endpoint and critical candidates are ordered within
\[
y_{\mathrm{sub}}< -1 < \beta_2^2-\frac12<0<\beta_1^2-\frac12<1,
\]
together with the critical maximum \(1\) at \(\lambda=-1\).
Thus \(y(E_{\mathrm c}(\RR))=[y_{\mathrm{sub}},1]\).

On \(J_{\mathrm n}\), the finite candidates are only \(0\) and
\(\beta_3^2-1/2\in(1/16,1/2)\).
The endpoint value is therefore strictly positive, while the only interior critical value on \(J_{\mathrm n}\) is \(0\) at \(\lambda=1\).
Hence the minimum is attained at \(\lambda=1\), with value \(0\).
The pole at infinity forces the image to be unbounded above, so \(y(E_{\mathrm{n}}(\RR))=[0,\infty)\).
Counting the number of real preimages in the open intervals follows from continuity and the fact that the ramification points contribute multiplicity two.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Real images for the canonical specialization]\label{cor:two-interval-specialization}
For the specialized quartic, the compact real oval maps to \([y_{\mathrm{sub}},1]\), the noncompact real oval maps to \([0,\infty)\), and the total real image is \([y_{\mathrm{sub}},\infty)\).
The definitive real-root count derived from these two fixed images is Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-b-real-projection-criterion}; the branch-value and dominance statement needed later for the canonical quartic is Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This is exactly Proposition~\ref{cor:quartic-real-projection-framework}: the compact component has extrema \(y_{\mathrm{sub}}\) and \(1\), while the noncompact component has finite minimum \(0\) and a pole at infinity.
The final counting table is not repeated here; it follows in Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-b-real-projection-criterion} by applying the two-to-one covering property over the interiors of these images.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:balanced-family-generality}
The fixed-member lemma is retained only to justify the canonical substitution.  Later arguments use only Proposition~\ref{cor:quartic-real-projection-framework}, namely the interval images for \(P_{-1,-1,1,0}\).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Double-root normalization inside the balanced family}

Two elementary operations preserve the balanced form: a shift \(y\mapsto y+\beta\) and a simultaneous scaling \((\lambda,y)\mapsto(L\lambda,L^2 y)\).
They allow us to place a chosen double root at \(y=0\) with eigenvalue \(1\) and then to fix one further real branch value.
This subsection is the proof block for Theorem~C.
Theorem~C is algebraic only.
Dominance is neither assumed nor concluded until Theorem~D.
The lemmas below first record the allowed normalizing operations and the resulting algebraic constraints; Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization} then states the canonical double-root specialization itself.
Maximal-modulus separation is proved later from the canonical branch data and is not part of the double-root forcing argument.

\begin{lemma}[Shift and scaling]\label{lem:balanced-affine}
Let $P_{a,c,d,g}$ be given by \eqref{eq:balanced-family}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For any $\beta\in\RR$, the polynomial
\[
\widehat P(\lambda,y):=P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y+\beta)
\]
equals $P_{a,c',d,g'}(\lambda,y)$ with $c'=c-2\beta$ and $g'=g+\beta(\beta-c)$.
\item For any $L\neq 0$, the change of variables $(\lambda,y)=(L\widetilde\lambda,L^2\widetilde y)$ yields
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(L\widetilde\lambda,L^2\widetilde y)=L^4 P_{\widetilde a,\widetilde c,\widetilde d,\widetilde g}(\widetilde\lambda,\widetilde y),
\]
where $\widetilde a=a/L$, $\widetilde c=c/L^2$, $\widetilde d=d/L^3$, and $\widetilde g=g/L^4$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both statements follow from direct substitution.
For (1), expand $(y+\beta)((y+\beta)-c)+g=y(y-(c-2\beta))+\beta(\beta-c)+g$ and note that the remaining coefficients do not change.
For (2), divide by $L^4$ after substituting $(L\widetilde\lambda,L^2\widetilde y)$ into \eqref{eq:balanced-family}.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-affine} shows that the algebraic double-root data can be shifted so that the chosen collision height is $y=0$ and scaled so that the chosen nonzero colliding eigenvalue equals $1$.

\begin{definition}[Normalized collision data]\label{def:normalized-collision-data}
Let $P_{a,c,d,g}$ be a balanced polynomial and let $(\rho,u)\in\RR^\times\times\RR$.
We say that $P_{a,c,d,g}$ has normalized collision data $(\rho,u)$ if the only normalization used is
\[
\lambda=\rho\widetilde\lambda,
\qquad
y=u+\rho^2\widetilde y,
\]
and the two points
\[
(\lambda,y)=(\rho,u),
\qquad
(\lambda,y)=(-\rho,u+\rho^2)
\]
are double roots in their corresponding $\lambda$-fibres, equivalently
\[
P(\rho,u)=P_\lambda(\rho,u)=P(-\rho,u+\rho^2)=P_\lambda(-\rho,u+\rho^2)=0.
\]
The normalized coordinates of these two collisions are therefore $(1,0)$ and $(-1,1)$.
\end{definition}

The following fixed-coordinate lemma solves the algebraic constraints used inside the normalized specialization theorem when the neighbouring normalized double root is fixed at $\widetilde y=1$.

\begin{lemma}[Fixed-coordinate double-root normalization]\label{lem:balanced-classification}
Let $a,c,d,g\in\RR$.
Assume that the balanced polynomial $P_{a,c,d,g}$ has double roots at $(\lambda,y)=(1,0)$ and $(\lambda,y)=(-1,1)$.
Then $(a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set $Q(\lambda,y)=P_{a,c,d,g}(\lambda,y)$.
The two double-root conditions mean that $Q(1,0)=Q_\lambda(1,0)=Q(-1,1)=Q_\lambda(-1,1)=0$.
Direct evaluation gives four linear equations:
\[
1+a+c+d+g=0,\qquad
4+3a+2c+d=0,
\]
\[
-a-d+g=0,\qquad
3a-2c+d=0.
\]
Solving yields $c=-1$, $a=-1$, $d=1$, and $g=0$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-classification} supplies the algebraic classification used to prove the next result, which is the normalized double-root specialization stated as Theorem~C in the introduction.

\begin{theorem}[Canonical double-root specialization (Theorem C)]\label{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization}
Let $u\in\RR$ and $\rho\in\RR^\times$, and let $P_{a,c,d,g}$ be a balanced polynomial.
Consider the affine $y$-shift by the chosen collision height followed by the weighted dilation of weights $1$ in $\lambda$ and $2$ in $y$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-normalization-change}
\lambda=\rho\widetilde\lambda,
\qquad
y=u+\rho^2\widetilde y,
\end{equation}
so that the chosen collision is sent to $(\widetilde\lambda,\widetilde y)=(1,0)$.
Equivalently, one first replaces $y$ by $y+u$ and then scales $(\lambda,y)$ by $(\rho,\rho^2)$; translations in $\lambda$, independent scalings of $y$, and any further affine changes are not part of the normalization.
Under this allowed normalization the transformed balanced parameters are explicitly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-transformed-parameters}
\left(
\frac a\rho,
\frac{c-2u}{\rho^2},
\frac d{\rho^3},
\frac{g+u(u-c)}{\rho^4}
\right).
\end{equation}
The following conditions are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The polynomial $P_{a,c,d,g}$ has normalized collision data $(\rho,u)$ in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:normalized-collision-data}.
\item The algebraic double-root constraints hold at the two points
\[
(\lambda,y)=(\rho,u),
\qquad
(\lambda,y)=(-\rho,u+\rho^2),
\]
that is,
\[
P(\rho,u)=P_\lambda(\rho,u)=P(-\rho,u+\rho^2)=P_\lambda(-\rho,u+\rho^2)=0.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Neither condition contains a maximal-modulus hypothesis, and neither condition asserts any positive-axis dominance conclusion.
When these equivalent conditions hold, the weighted affine normalization forces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-normalization-equation}
P_{a,c,d,g}(\rho\widetilde\lambda,u+\rho^2\widetilde y)
=
\rho^4 P_{-1,-1,1,0}(\widetilde\lambda,
\widetilde y),
\end{equation}
so the transformed parameters in \eqref{eq:canonical-transformed-parameters} are forced to be $(-1,-1,1,0)$, and hence the original parameters are uniquely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-normalization-parameters}
(a,c,d,g)=(-\rho,\,2u-\rho^2,\,\rho^3,\,u(u-\rho^2)).
\end{equation}
Conversely, the parameter quadruple in \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-parameters} has exactly this normalized collision data and satisfies \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-equation}.
Thus, after the prescribed shift and weighted dilation, the forced balanced member is the canonical specialization $P_{-1,-1,1,0}=\Pi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The assertion identifies the unique normalized balanced member forced by the two double-root constraints alone.
The words ``collision'' and ``normalization'' in this theorem refer only to multiplicity in the indicated \(\lambda\)-fibres and to the allowed affine weighted change of variables.
Theorem~C is purely algebraic.  It says that the two prescribed double-root constraints, after the allowed weighted affine normalization, force the parameter quadruple \((-1,-1,1,0)\).  It neither assumes nor proves maximal-modulus dominance.  Dominance for the resulting canonical member is Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}.
Maximal-modulus and dominance assertions are separate results proved later for the canonical member in Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance} and then transported by Lemma~\ref{lem:real-weighted-pullback-dominance} and Corollary~\ref{cor:canonical-dominance-after-normalization}.
Apply the shift part of Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-affine} with $\beta=u$ and then the scaling part with $L=\rho$.
The transformed polynomial is
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\rho\widetilde\lambda,u+\rho^2\widetilde y)
=
\rho^4
P_{a/\rho,
(c-2u)/\rho^2,
d/\rho^3,
(g+u(u-c))/\rho^4}
(\widetilde\lambda,\widetilde y).
\]
Because the change of variables \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-change} is invertible and has nonzero Jacobian determinant \(\rho^3\), multiplicity of a root in the \(\lambda\)-fibre is preserved: for fixed \(y\), a point is a double root of the quartic in \(\lambda\) if and only if its normalized image is a double root of the normalized quartic in \(\widetilde\lambda\).
Definition~\ref{def:normalized-collision-data} is precisely the assertion that the original points $(\rho,u)$ and $(-\rho,u+\rho^2)$ become the normalized double roots $(1,0)$ and $(-1,1)$ under \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-change}.
Thus the definition is equivalent to the displayed algebraic double-root constraints.
Therefore Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-classification} forces the transformed parameter quadruple to be $(-1,-1,1,0)$.
Solving
\[
\frac a\rho=-1,
\qquad
\frac{c-2u}{\rho^2}=-1,
\qquad
\frac d{\rho^3}=1,
\qquad
\frac{g+u(u-c)}{\rho^4}=0
\]
gives exactly \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-parameters}.
Conversely, if \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-parameters} holds, then the displayed transformed parameter quadruple is $(-1,-1,1,0)$, which proves \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-equation}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-classification} and its proof show that $P_{-1,-1,1,0}$ has double roots at $(1,0)$ and $(-1,1)$, and the inverse of \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-change} carries them to $(\rho,u)$ and $(-\rho,u+\rho^2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Real weighted pullback of positive-side dominance]\label{lem:real-weighted-pullback-dominance}
Let $u\in\RR$, let $\rho\in\RR^\times$, and suppose that
\[
P_{a,c,d,g}(\rho\widetilde\lambda,u+\rho^2\widetilde y)
=
\rho^4\Pi(\widetilde\lambda,\widetilde y).
\]
Assume that, for some interval $I\subset\RR$, a branch $\widetilde\lambda_*(\widetilde y)$ of the canonical fibre $\Pi(\cdot,\widetilde y)$ is simple and has strictly maximal modulus for every $\widetilde y\in I$.
This lemma has no independent canonical dominance input; it is only the invariance of already-proved modulus inequalities under the real weighted coordinate change.
Then
\[
\lambda_*(y)=\rho\widetilde\lambda_*\!\left(\frac{y-u}{\rho^2}\right),
\qquad
y\in u+\rho^2 I,
\]
is a simple strictly maximal-modulus branch of the original fibre $P_{a,c,d,g}(\cdot,y)$.
Moreover, any canonical real branch value $\widetilde y_0$ whose colliding pair is not of maximal modulus pulls back to $y_0=u+\rho^2\widetilde y_0$ with the same non-dominance property.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For fixed $\widetilde y$, the displayed identity gives a multiplicity-preserving bijection between roots of $\Pi(\cdot,\widetilde y)$ and roots of $P_{a,c,d,g}(\cdot,u+\rho^2\widetilde y)$ by $\lambda=\rho\widetilde\lambda$.
Indeed, multiplying the polynomial by the nonzero scalar $\rho^4$ does not change its roots, and the affine change in the fibre coordinate has nonzero derivative $\rho$.
Thus a simple canonical root maps to a simple original root.
For every two roots in the corresponding fibres, their moduli are both multiplied by the same positive factor $\abs\rho$.
Strict maximal-modulus inequalities are therefore preserved and reflected by the pullback.
The same comparison proves the final assertion: if a canonical colliding pair has modulus strictly smaller than another canonical root or pair at a real branch value, then after multiplying all roots by $\rho$ the same strict inequality holds in the original fibre over $u+\rho^2\widetilde y_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Real weighted pullback of Theorem D dominance]\label{cor:canonical-dominance-after-normalization}
In the situation of Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization}, suppose the chosen collision is the positive normalized one, so that after the change of variables \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-change} the colliding point is $(\widetilde\lambda,\widetilde y)=(1,0)$ and the positive side is $\widetilde y>0$.
The double-root normalization itself is purely algebraic: it only identifies the normalized polynomial as $P_{-1,-1,1,0}$.
All dominance assertions in this corollary are imported from Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}; the algebraic normalization does not supply them.
The corollary records only that the root and modulus comparisons established in Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance} are preserved under the real nonzero weighted change of variables.
For the canonical specialization, the collision at $(1,0)$ is the unique maximal-modulus square-root endpoint for the canonical branch on the positive side:
for every $\widetilde y>0$, the positive real root $\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(\widetilde y)$ of $P_{-1,-1,1,0}(\cdot,\widetilde y)$ is simple and satisfies
\[
\abs{\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(\widetilde y)}
>
\abs{\lambda}
\]
for every other root $\lambda$ of $P_{-1,-1,1,0}(\cdot,\widetilde y)$.
Equivalently, for this real nonzero scaling and only on the carried positive side, the original-variable sheet
\[
y=u+\rho^2\widetilde y,
\qquad
\lambda=\rho\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(\widetilde y)
\qquad(\widetilde y>0)
\]
is the unique maximal-modulus sheet of $P_{a,c,d,g}(\cdot,y)$ on the real side $y>u$, and the endpoint $(\lambda,y)=(\rho,u)$ is dominant relative to its neighbouring positive-side fibres.
The remaining normalized real branch values, $\widetilde y=1$ and $\widetilde y=\ySub$, are non-dominant in the sense recorded in Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization} supplies only the algebraic normalization step: after the prescribed shift and weighted dilation, the polynomial is $P_{-1,-1,1,0}$, which is the specialized quartic $\Pi$.
No dominance assertion is used in that classification.
The endpoint statement at $(1,0)$ and the non-dominance of the other two real branch values are the separate canonical-specialization conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}(2).
The strict maximal-modulus inequality for every $\widetilde y>0$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-branch-dominance}(4), equivalently Lemma~\ref{lem:positive-axis-dominance-criterion}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:real-weighted-pullback-dominance} then pulls those Theorem~D statements through the real weighted normalization \eqref{eq:canonical-normalization-change}, giving exactly the displayed original-variable sheet and endpoint.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Explicit elimination example}

The symbolic elimination that produces the ramification quintic \eqref{eq:balanced-quintic} can be displayed concretely.
Starting from \eqref{eq:balanced-ramification-eq} and \eqref{eq:balanced-weierstrass}, eliminate $Y$ as follows.
Equation \eqref{eq:balanced-ramification-eq} gives $Y=(3a\lambda^2+d)/(2\lambda)$, provided $\lambda\neq 0$ (the $\lambda=0$ case is trivial and leads again to $d=0$, excluded here).
Substituting into $Y^2=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g$ yields
\[
\frac{(3a\lambda^2+d)^2}{4\lambda^2}=c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g.
\]
Clearing denominators produces
\[
(3a\lambda^2+d)^2=4\lambda^2\bigl(c^2-4a\lambda^3-4d\lambda-4g\bigr).
\]
Expanding both sides and collecting powers of $\lambda$ gives the degree-$5$ polynomial
\[
16a\lambda^5+9a^2\lambda^4+16d\lambda^3+(16g+6ad-4c^2)\lambda^2+d^2,
\]
which is exactly \eqref{eq:balanced-quintic}.
After the specialization \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\), this polynomial becomes
\[
-16\lambda^5+9\lambda^4+16\lambda^3-10\lambda^2+1
=
-(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)(16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1),
\]
which is the factorization used later in \eqref{eq:ramification-poly}.
All central eliminations used below are displayed either here or in Appendix~\ref{app:disc}; Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility-note} indexes these certificate locations.

\subsection{Specialization to the quartic transfer family}

\begin{proposition}[Canonical computation from Theorem A: affine model and branch geometry]\label{prop:elliptic-reduction}
The spectral polynomial \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\) from \eqref{eq:spectral-poly} is the specialization
\[
\Pi(\lambda,y)=P_{-1,-1,1,0}(\lambda,y).
\]
Under the substitution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi-def}
\xi = y - \lambda^2, \qquad Y = 2\xi + 1 = 2y + 1 - 2\lambda^2,
\end{equation}
the affine spectral curve \(\Pi(\lambda,y)=0\) is isomorphic to the affine elliptic model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-xi}
\xi^2 + \xi = \lambda^3 - \lambda,
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-weierstrass}
Y^2 = 4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1.
\end{equation}
Its Weierstrass invariants are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weierstrass-invariants}
g_2 = 4, \qquad g_3 = -1, \qquad
\Delta = g_2^3 - 27g_3^2 = 37, \qquad
j = \frac{1728\,g_2^3}{\Delta} = \frac{110592}{37}.
\end{equation}
The function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y-from-E}
y = \lambda^2 + \xi = \lambda^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}Y
\end{equation}
defines a degree-\(4\) map \(E\to \mathbf P^1_y\).
Its finite branch values are exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch-values}
y=0,\qquad y=1,\qquad c(y)=0,
\quad c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32.
\end{equation}
We shall write \(\ySub\) for the unique real root of \(c\), and \(\eta,\overline\eta\) for the two non-real roots of \(c\).
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:branch-value-convention}
\Branch_{\mathrm{fin}}
=
\{0,1,\ySub,\eta,\overline\eta\},
\qquad
c(\ySub)=c(\eta)=c(\overline\eta)=0,
\qquad
\eta\notin\RR.
\end{equation}
This proves the canonical specialization.
The proposition records the concrete equations, branch values, and naming convention used throughout the real classification and the positive-real maximal-modulus analysis.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substituting \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\) into \eqref{eq:balanced-family} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pi-as-quadratic-in-y}
\Pi(\lambda,y)
=
y^2+(1-2\lambda^2)y+\lambda^4-\lambda^3-\lambda^2+\lambda.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
\Pi(\lambda,y)
=
(y-\lambda^2)^2+(y-\lambda^2)-\lambda^3+\lambda.
\]
Thus Proposition~\ref{prop:balanced-family}, equivalently the first clause of Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization}, gives the affine isomorphism \eqref{eq:elliptic-xi}--\eqref{eq:elliptic-weierstrass} and the formula \eqref{eq:y-from-E}.

The smoothness condition follows from Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-smooth}, equivalently the smoothness clause of Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization}:
\[
64d^3+27a(c^2-4g)^2
=
64\cdot 1^3+27(-1)(1-0)^2
=
37\neq 0.
\]
Hence the projective completion is genus one.
The invariants in \eqref{eq:weierstrass-invariants} are read off directly from \(Y^2=4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1\).
The degree statement follows from the degree-four clause of Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization} specialized to this member.

For the finite ramification points, Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-ramification}, equivalently the ramification clause of Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization}, gives
\[
-16\lambda^5+9\lambda^4+16\lambda^3-10\lambda^2+1=0.
\]
Multiplying by \(-1\) and factoring yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ramification-poly}
(\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1)(16\lambda^3 - 9\lambda^2 + 1)=0.
\end{equation}
The corresponding branch values are obtained from
\[
y = \lambda^2 + \xi = \frac{4\lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1}{4\lambda}.
\]
At \(\lambda=1\), this gives \(y=0\); at \(\lambda=-1\), it gives \(y=1\).
For the cubic factor, the relation between \(\lambda\) and its image \(y\) is
\[
q(\lambda,y):=4y\lambda-(4\lambda^3-3\lambda^2-2\lambda+1)=0.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-branch-elimination} eliminates \(\lambda\) from \(16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1=0\) and \(q(\lambda,y)=0\).  Hence the remaining finite branch values are exactly the roots of \(c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Cubic branch elimination]\label{lem:cubic-branch-elimination}
Let
\[
p(\lambda)=16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1,
\qquad
q(\lambda,y)=4y\lambda-(4\lambda^3-3\lambda^2-2\lambda+1).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-branch-elimination-identity}
\Res_\lambda\bigl(p(\lambda),q(\lambda,y)\bigr)
=
-64\bigl(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\bigr).
\end{equation}
Consequently, a point on the cubic ramification factor maps to the \(y\)-line only over the three roots of \(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\), and each of those roots occurs as such an image.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The resultant in \eqref{eq:cubic-branch-elimination-identity} is the Sylvester determinant
\[
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
16&-9&0&1&0&0\\
0&16&-9&0&1&0\\
0&0&16&-9&0&1\\
-4&3&4y+2&-1&0&0\\
0&-4&3&4y+2&-1&0\\
0&0&-4&3&4y+2&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Expanding the displayed determinant by fraction-free elementary row-reduction over \(\ZZ[y]\) gives
\[
-16384y^3-26304y^2-10560y-2048
=
-64(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32),
\]
which proves the displayed identity.  This is the complete elimination criterion used here: the matrix is the Sylvester matrix of the two displayed polynomials, and no unrecorded computer-algebra normalization is being invoked.  By the defining property of the resultant, a common zero of \(p(\lambda)\) and \(q(\lambda,y)\) can occur only when this cubic in \(y\) vanishes.  Conversely, if the resultant vanishes, then the two positive-degree polynomials \(p\) and \(q\) have a common zero over the algebraic closure, so every root of the displayed cubic is the image of at least one point on the cubic ramification factor.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Simplicity of the finite ramification locus]\label{lem:finite-ramification-simple}
All five zeros of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-ramification-framework}
R(\lambda):=(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)(16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1)
\end{equation}
are simple.
Consequently the five finite ramification points of the map \(y:E\to\mathbf P^1_y\) are simple.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(p(\lambda)=16\lambda^3-9\lambda^2+1\).
The two linear factors in \eqref{eq:finite-ramification-framework} are not factors of \(p\), since
\[
p(1)=8\neq0,
\qquad
p(-1)=-24\neq0.
\]
It remains to show that the cubic factor has no multiple zero.
Its derivative is
\[
p'(\lambda)=48\lambda^2-18\lambda=6\lambda(8\lambda-3).
\]
A common zero of \(p\) and \(p'\) would have \(\lambda=0\) or \(\lambda=3/8\), but
\[
p(0)=1\neq0,
\qquad
p\!\left(\frac38\right)=\frac{5}{32}\neq0.
\]
Thus \(\gcd(p,p')=1\), and no zero of \(p\) is multiple.
Together with \(p(\pm1)\neq0\), this proves \(\gcd(R,R')=1\), so the five roots of \(R\) are simple.

At a finite point of the smooth curve \(E\), ramification of \(y\) is given by \(\dd y=0\), and the displayed polynomial \(R\) is the resulting critical-point equation from Corollary~\ref{cor:balanced-ramification} after the specialization \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\).
Since its roots are simple, the differential \(\dd y\) has a simple zero at each of the five finite ramification points.
Equivalently each finite ramification index is \(2\), so every finite ramification point is simple.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Projective normalization of the quartic]\label{lem:projective-normalization}
Let \(\overline C\subset \mathbf P^2_{[\Lambda:W:Z]}\) be the projective closure of the affine quartic \(\Pi(\lambda,y)=0\), namely
\[
\overline C=\{F(\Lambda,W,Z)=0\},
\]
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projective-quartic}
F(\Lambda,W,Z)
=
\Lambda^4-\Lambda^3Z-(2WZ+Z^2)\Lambda^2+\Lambda Z^3+W(W+Z)Z^2.
\end{equation}
Then \(\overline C\) has a unique point at infinity, namely \([0:1:0]\), and this point is singular.
The morphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalization-map}
\nu:E\longrightarrow \overline C,
\qquad
(\lambda,Y)\longmapsto
\left[\lambda:\lambda^2-\tfrac12+\tfrac12Y:1\right]
\end{equation}
extends across the point at infinity \(O\in E\), satisfies
\[
\nu^{-1}([0:1:0])=\{O\},
\]
and is finite and birational.
Consequently, \(E\) is the normalization of \(\overline C\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Homogenizing \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\) gives \eqref{eq:projective-quartic}.
On the line \(Z=0\), one has \(F(\Lambda,W,0)=\Lambda^4\), so the only point at infinity is \([0:1:0]\).
The partial derivatives are
\[
F_\Lambda=4\Lambda^3-3\Lambda^2Z-2(2WZ+Z^2)\Lambda+Z^3,
\]
\[
F_W=-2Z\Lambda^2+(2W+Z)Z^2,
\]
\[
F_Z=-\Lambda^3-2(W+Z)\Lambda^2+3\Lambda Z^2+2W^2Z+3WZ^2,
\]
and all three vanish at \([0:1:0]\), so this point is singular.

On the affine chart \(Z=1\), the map \(\nu\) is inverse to the rational map
\[
(\lambda,y)\longmapsto (\lambda,Y),\qquad Y=2y+1-2\lambda^2,
\]
between the affine quartic \(\Pi(\lambda,y)=0\) and the affine cubic \(E:Y^2=4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1\).
Hence \(\nu\) is birational on dense affine opens.

Let \(O\in E\) be the point at infinity on the smooth projective cubic.
For a Weierstrass cubic one has
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(\lambda)=-2,
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_O(Y)=-3
\]
\cite{Silverman2009,Miranda1995}.
Since
\[
y=\lambda^2-\frac12+\frac12Y,
\]
it follows that
\[
\operatorname{ord}_O(y)=\min\{-4,-3,0\}=-4.
\]
Therefore \(\lambda/y\) and \(1/y\) are regular at \(O\) and vanish there.
Writing
\[
\nu=[\lambda:y:1]=[\lambda/y:1:1/y]
\]
shows that \(\nu\) extends to \(O\) and sends \(O\) to \([0:1:0]\).
Conversely, any point of \(E\) mapping to \(Z=0\) must be a pole of \(y\), and \(y\) has its unique pole at \(O\).
Thus \(\nu^{-1}([0:1:0])=\{O\}\).

Because \(E\) is projective and \(\nu\) is a nonconstant morphism onto the irreducible curve \(\overline C\), the map \(\nu\) is finite: the morphism is proper, its generic fibre is finite because the affine inverse above gives a degree-one extension of function fields, and a proper quasi-finite morphism of curves is finite \cite[Chap.~II, Sec.~5]{Miranda1995}.
It is already birational on the affine loci, so \(\nu\) is a finite birational morphism.
Finally, \(E\) is smooth and hence normal; by the universal property of normalization for algebraic curves \cite[Chap.~II, Sec.~5]{Miranda1995}, a finite birational morphism from a normal curve to \(\overline C\) identifies the source with the normalization of \(\overline C\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Riemann--Hurwitz consistency}\label{subsec:riemann-hurwitz}

The specialization above admits a strong geometric consistency check via the classical Riemann--Hurwitz formula for finite morphisms of nonsingular projective curves \cite[Chap.~II, Sec.~4]{Miranda1995}.

\begin{corollary}[Ramification budget for the degree-four projection]\label{cor:riemann-hurwitz}
The total ramification index of the degree-\(4\) map \(E \to \mathbf{P}^1_y\) equals \(8\), in agreement with the Riemann--Hurwitz formula.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The Riemann--Hurwitz formula for a degree-\(d\) map from a curve of genus \(g\) to \(\mathbf P^1\) gives
\[
2g-2=d(-2)+\sum_P(e_P-1).
\]
With \(g=1\) and \(d=4\), the total ramification must be \(8\).
At infinity the map has a single totally ramified point, contributing \(4-1=3\).
By Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-ramification-simple}, the five finite ramification points in \eqref{eq:ramification-poly} are simple, and each contributes \(1\).
Hence the finite contribution is \(5\), and \(3+5=8\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Discriminant factorization as a corollary}\label{subsec:disc-corollary}

The branch locus of the degree-\(4\) covering coincides with the vanishing locus of the \(\lambda\)-discriminant.

\begin{lemma}[Finite repeated roots and ramification]\label{lem:repeated-root-ramification}
Let \(P=(\lambda_0,Y_0)\in E\) be a point with finite \(y\)-value \(y_0\).
Then the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\lambda_0\) is a repeated root of the quartic \(\Pi(\cdot,y_0)\).
\item \(P\) is a finite ramification point of the map \(y:E\to \mathbf P^1_y\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On the affine quartic one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pi-y-equals-Y}
\Pi_y(\lambda,y)=2y+1-2\lambda^2=Y.
\end{equation}
Away from \(Y=0\), implicit differentiation of \(\Pi(\lambda,y)=0\) therefore gives
\[
\frac{\dd y}{\dd\lambda}
=
-\frac{\Pi_\lambda(\lambda,y)}{\Pi_y(\lambda,y)}
=
-\frac{\Pi_\lambda(\lambda,y)}{Y}.
\]
Thus, whenever \(Y_0\neq 0\), the conditions \(\Pi(\lambda_0,y_0)=0=\Pi_\lambda(\lambda_0,y_0)\) and \(\dd y/\dd\lambda(P)=0\) are equivalent.

It remains to rule out the case \(Y_0=0\) at a finite repeated root.
If \(Y_0=0\), then \(2y_0+1=2\lambda_0^2\), hence
\[
\Pi_\lambda(\lambda_0,y_0)
=
4\lambda_0^3-3\lambda_0^2-2(2y_0+1)\lambda_0+1
=
-3\lambda_0^2+1.
\]
Therefore \(\Pi_\lambda(\lambda_0,y_0)=0\) would force \(\lambda_0=\pm 1/\sqrt3\).
But then
\[
Y_0^2=4\lambda_0^3-4\lambda_0+1
=
1\mp \frac{8}{3\sqrt3}\neq 0,
\]
contradicting \(Y_0=0\).
So \(Y_0\neq 0\) at every finite repeated root, and the equivalence follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Discriminant factorization]\label{cor:disc-factor}
The discriminant of \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\) with respect to \(\lambda\) factors as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disc-factor-new}
\Disc_\lambda \Pi(\lambda,y) = -y(y-1)\bigl(256y^3 + 411y^2 + 165y + 32\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:repeated-root-ramification}, a finite value \(y_0\) is a branch value of \(y:E\to \mathbf P^1_y\) if and only if \(\Pi(\cdot,y_0)\) has a repeated root.
The equivalence there includes the exclusion of the only possible denominator locus in the implicit derivative, namely \(Y=\Pi_y=0\), so no finite branch value is lost before passing to the resultant.
Equivalently, the finite branch values are exactly the zeros of \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi(\lambda,y)\).
Proposition~\ref{prop:elliptic-reduction} identifies them as \(y=0\), \(y=1\), and the roots of \(c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\).
The computation in Appendix~\ref{app:disc} displays the Sylvester matrix for \(\Res_\lambda(\Pi,\partial_\lambda\Pi)\) and expands it in \eqref{eq:disc-expanded-polynomial} as \(-256y^5-155y^4+246y^3+133y^2+32y\), so \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi\) is a degree-\(5\) polynomial in \(y\) with leading coefficient \(-256\).
These roots and the leading coefficient determine the factorization uniquely, giving \eqref{eq:disc-factor-new}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Canonical spectral-quartic specialization]\label{cor:canonical-theorem-a-specialization}
For the spectral quartic \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\), Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization} specializes as follows.
The normalized double-root conditions select the unique balanced member
\[
\Pi=P_{-1,-1,1,0}
\]
of the family \eqref{eq:balanced-family}.
The affine substitution
\[
\xi=y-\lambda^2,
\qquad
Y=2y+1-2\lambda^2
\]
identifies \(\Pi=0\) with the elliptic curve
\[
E:\quad Y^2=4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1,
\qquad
y=\lambda^2-\frac12+\frac12Y,
\]
and the projective closure of \(\Pi=0\) is normalized by this \(E\).
The map \(y:E\to\mathbf P^1_y\) has degree \(4\), finite branch values
\[
0,
\quad 1,
\quad \text{and the three roots of }256y^3+411y^2+165y+32,
\]
and discriminant factorization
\[
\Disc_\lambda\Pi(\lambda,y)=-y(y-1)(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32).
\]
Thus the canonical specialization, projective normalization, branch values, and discriminant factorization are the spectral-quartic realization of the balanced normalization theorem.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The double-root selection is Theorem~\ref{thm:canonical-specialization-up-to-normalization} applied with the fixed normalized collisions \((\lambda,y)=(1,0)\) and \((-1,1)\), equivalently its fixed-coordinate input Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-classification}.
The affine model, the formula for \(y\), the degree of the cover, and the finite branch values are exactly Proposition~\ref{prop:elliptic-reduction}, which is the specialization of Theorem~\ref{thm:theorem-a-balanced-elliptic-normalization} at \((a,c,d,g)=(-1,-1,1,0)\).
Proposition~\ref{prop:elliptic-reduction} gives the smooth cubic model \(E\), and Lemma~\ref{lem:projective-normalization} proves that the resulting morphism from \(E\) to the projective quartic closure is finite and birational with \(E\) normal, hence is the normalization.
Finally, Corollary~\ref{cor:disc-factor} identifies the zeros of \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi\) with the finite branch values and fixes the leading coefficient from the Sylvester determinant computation in Appendix~\ref{app:disc}, giving the displayed factorization.
These cited results prove precisely the four components listed in the statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:genus-one-upgrade}
For the present transfer polynomial, the substitution \(\xi=y-\lambda^2\) is not an ad hoc trick.
It is the specialization of the balanced family \eqref{eq:balanced-family}, in which the same change of variables always removes the quartic obstruction and converts the curve to Weierstrass form.
This is the structural reason why the later branch-point and maximal-modulus branch analysis can be carried out exactly.
\end{remark}
