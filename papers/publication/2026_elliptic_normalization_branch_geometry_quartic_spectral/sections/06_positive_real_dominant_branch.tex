\section{Positive-real maximal-modulus branch and endpoint asymptotics}\label{sec:positive-maximal-modulus-branch}

Having classified all real branch points, we now parametrize the real branch over positive \(y\), prove by direct modulus inequalities that it is the unique maximal-modulus branch, and derive the Puiseux expansion at the endpoint \(y=0\).
The zero results in this section are local endpoint statements: the index \(k\) is fixed before \(m\to\infty\), or equivalently the scaled window \([-U^2/m^2,0)\) is fixed in advance.
After the endpoint zero theorem is complete, we add a supplementary large-\(y\) check showing that the opposite end of the same positive branch is controlled by a double tangency at projective infinity.
The section has three separate layers.
Subsections~\ref{subsec:positive-branch-parametrization}--\ref{subsec:positive-axis-dominance} prove the main positive-axis dominance theorem.
Subsections~\ref{subsec:endpoint-puiseux}--\ref{subsec:endpoint-quantization} prove the local endpoint theorem.
Subsection~\ref{subsec:large-y-splitting} is supplementary and only points to the large-\(y\) consistency check in Appendix~\ref{app:projective-infinity}.

\begin{proposition}[Positive-axis dependency spine]\label{prop:positive-axis-dependency-spine}
The positive-axis part of the proof has the following dependency spine.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Theorem~\ref{thm:positive-maximal-modulus-branch} constructs the real sheet over \((0,\infty)\) from the explicit branch formula and does not use any endpoint-zero asymptotic.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-interval-certificates}, Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate}, Proposition~\ref{prop:displayed-positive-axis-certificate-sufficiency}, Lemma~\ref{lem:positive-axis-dominance-certificate}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:global-dominance} prove that this sheet is the unique maximal-modulus sheet for every \(y>0\).
\item Theorem~\ref{thm:consolidated-branch-dominance-package} combines the genus-one branch data from Corollary~\ref{cor:canonical-theorem-a-package}, the real branch-point classification from Theorem~\ref{thm:ep-classification}, and the positive-axis certificate above; this is the theorem that supplies the dominance input for the zero analysis.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-puiseux}, Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-ep2-branching}, Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses}, Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} reduce the endpoint problem to Rouch\'e discs in the fixed scaled coordinate \(u=m\sqrt{-y}\).
\item Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization}, Corollary~\ref{prop:fixed-window-endpoint-interface}, and Corollary~\ref{cor:edge-zero-counting} are therefore fixed-window consequences of the local square-root endpoint and not global density statements for all negative zeros.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, the proof of Theorem~E uses Theorem~D only through the verified maximal-modulus endpoint at \((\lambda,y)=(1,0)\), the local expansions from Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package}, and the two-branch oscillation lemma; the supplementary large-\(y\) check is not an input to this chain.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:positive-maximal-modulus-branch} is proved from the displayed formula \eqref{eq:y-phys} and its derivative, so it constructs a positive real branch before any zero-counting argument appears.
The algebraic certificate sequence then proves maximality on the three cells \((0,1)\), \(\{1\}\), and \((1,\infty)\): Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-interval-certificates} isolates the cells and root counts, Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate} records the exact resultants and inequalities needed for modulus comparison, Proposition~\ref{prop:displayed-positive-axis-certificate-sufficiency} states that these displayed identities are sufficient, Lemma~\ref{lem:positive-axis-dominance-certificate} performs the interval comparisons, and Proposition~\ref{prop:global-dominance} identifies the branch with the spectral radius.
Thus items (1) and (2) are exactly the internal proof order of the positive-axis dominance package.

Theorem~\ref{thm:consolidated-branch-dominance-package} explicitly cites the discriminant and branch values from the genus-one package, the real branch-value and subdominance results from Theorem~\ref{thm:ep-classification}, and the positive-axis dominance package just listed.
It therefore packages, rather than reproves, the branch roles needed later.

For the endpoint part, Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-puiseux} identifies the square-root expansion at the endpoint, Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-ep2-branching} gives the abstract simple-double-point normal form, Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses} imports the model-specific constants and remainders from Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} converts those hypotheses into a compact-uniform two-branch oscillation formula.
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} applies Rouch\'e's theorem in fixed compact rectangles of the scaled \(u\)-plane.
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization} then transfers those isolated \(u\)-zeros through \(y=-u^2/m^2\), and the two following corollaries merely restate the same fixed-window information.
Consequently no statement in this endpoint chain asserts control in a growing window or over growing indices, and the large-\(y\) paragraph at the end is only a consistency check, as claimed.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Positive branch parametrization and monotonicity}\label{subsec:positive-branch-parametrization}

The following lemma records the only local facts used in this section:
simple roots vary analytically in the parameter, and a strict modulus gap persists locally by continuity.

\begin{lemma}[Local simplicity and persistence on $(0,\infty)$]\label{lem:local-simplicity-persistence}
Fix \(y_0>0\) with \(y_0\neq 1\).
Then the four roots of \(\Pi(\cdot,y_0)\) are simple.
Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{item:local-analyticity} Each root extends to an analytic function on a neighborhood of \(y_0\).
\item\label{item:local-persistence} If one of these local branches, say \(\lambda_1(y)\), satisfies
\[
\abs{\lambda_1(y_0)}>\abs{\lambda_j(y_0)}
\qquad (j\neq 1),
\]
then the same strict inequality holds for all \(y\) in a neighborhood of \(y_0\).
\end{enumerate}
At \(y_0=1\), the root \(\lambda=2\) is simple and therefore also admits a local analytic continuation.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The discriminant \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi(\lambda,y)\) was computed in Corollary~\ref{cor:disc-factor}:
\[
\Disc_\lambda\Pi(\lambda,y) = -y(y-1)(256y^3+411y^2+165y+32).
\]
This vanishes on \((0,\infty)\) only at \(y=1\), because the cubic factor has its unique real root at \(y_{\mathrm{sub}}<0\) by Theorem~\ref{thm:ep-classification}(3).
Therefore the four roots are simple at every \(y_0>0\) with \(y_0\neq1\).
The analytic continuation in \ref{item:local-analyticity} is the standard implicit-function theorem for simple roots of a polynomial with analytic coefficients \cite{Kato1976}.
The persistence statement \ref{item:local-persistence} is immediate from continuity of the local branches.
At \(y_0=1\), the factorization
\[
\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2
\]
shows directly that \(\lambda=2\) is simple, so the same local analyticity statement applies to that branch.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Reducible boundary at $y=1$]\label{rem:y1-reducible}
At $y=1$ the polynomial factors as $\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2$.
The double root $\lambda=-1$ is non-dominant (modulus $1$) while the simple root $\lambda=2$ is the maximal-modulus eigenvalue.
Since $\lambda=2$ is simple at $y=1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:local-simplicity-persistence}\ref{item:local-analyticity}---the first lemma in this section---applies to this root: the branch $\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}$ extends analytically through $y=1$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Positive-real parametrization branch on $y > 0$]\label{thm:positive-maximal-modulus-branch}
The real branch of the spectral curve connecting $(\lambda, y) = (1, 0)$ to $(\lambda, y) = (2, 1)$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y-phys}
y_{\mathrm{dom}}(\lambda)
= \lambda^2 - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1},
\qquad \lambda \ge 1.
\end{equation}
The function $y_{\mathrm{dom}}$ is strictly increasing on $(1, \infty)$ and maps $(1, \infty)$ bijectively onto $(0, \infty)$.
Consequently it has an inverse
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}:(0,\infty)\to(1,\infty).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:Pi-as-quadratic-in-y}, the spectral curve is quadratic in $y$ with solutions
\[
y = \lambda^2 - \frac{1}{2} \pm \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1}.
\]
At $(\lambda, y) = (1, 0)$ we have $4 \cdot 1 - 4 + 1 = 1$, and $1 - 1/2 - 1/2 = 0$, confirming that the minus-sign branch passes through $(1, 0)$.
Similarly, at $(\lambda, y) = (2, 1)$, one has $4 \cdot 8 - 8 + 1 = 25$ and $4 - 1/2 - 5/2 = 1$, confirming continuity to $(2, 1)$.

Differentiating \eqref{eq:y-phys} with respect to $\lambda$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dy-dlambda}
\frac{\dd y_{\mathrm{dom}}}{\dd\lambda}
= \frac{2\lambda\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1} - (3\lambda^2 - 1)}
{\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1}}.
\end{equation}
The critical points of $y_{\mathrm{dom}}$ on $(1, \infty)$ satisfy
\[
2\lambda\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1} = 3\lambda^2 - 1.
\]
Squaring both sides and simplifying yields
\[
(\lambda - 1)(\lambda+1)(16\lambda^3 - 9\lambda^2 + 1) = 0.
\]
For $\lambda > 1$, both sides of
\[
2\lambda\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1} = 3\lambda^2 - 1
\]
are strictly positive, so this squaring step introduces no spurious solution on $(1,\infty)$.
The factors $\lambda-1$ and $\lambda+1$ have no zero on $(1,\infty)$.
Moreover the polynomial $16\lambda^3 - 9\lambda^2 + 1$ has no zero on $[0, \infty)$: its derivative is $6\lambda(8\lambda - 3)$, so the unique interior minimum on $[0, \infty)$ is at $\lambda = 3/8$, where
\[
16\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^3 - 9\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^2 + 1 = \frac{37}{64} > 0.
\]
Hence the numerator of \eqref{eq:dy-dlambda} has no zero on $(1, \infty)$.
At $\lambda = 2$ it equals $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 - 11 = 9 > 0$, so continuity implies that it is strictly positive throughout $(1,\infty)$; the denominator in \eqref{eq:dy-dlambda} is also strictly positive there.
Therefore $y_{\mathrm{dom}}$ is strictly increasing on $(1, \infty)$.

Since $y_{\mathrm{dom}}(1) = 0$ and $y_{\mathrm{dom}}(\lambda) \to +\infty$ as $\lambda \to +\infty$ (the leading term is $\lambda^2$), the map is a bijection $(1, \infty) \to (0, \infty)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Comparison of the two positive branches for \(y>1\)]\label{lem:ygt1-sheet-comparison}
For \(\lambda>1\), define
\[
y_-(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\frac12-\frac12\sqrt{4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1},
\qquad
y_+(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\frac12+\frac12\sqrt{4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1}.
\]
Then \(y_+(\lambda)>y_-(\lambda)\) for every \(\lambda>1\), and \(y_+\) is strictly increasing on \((1,\infty)\).
If \(y>1\) and
\[
y=y_-(q)=y_+(p)
\]
for positive roots \(0<p<q\) of \(\Pi(\cdot,y)\), then \(q>p\).
Consequently, for \(y>1\) the larger positive root of \(\Pi(\cdot,y)\) lies on the minus-sign branch \(y_-=y_{\mathrm{dom}}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The strict inequality \(y_+(\lambda)>y_-(\lambda)\) is immediate from the definition.
Moreover,
\[
y_+'(\lambda)=2\lambda+\frac{3\lambda^2-1}{\sqrt{4\lambda^3-4\lambda+1}}>0
\qquad (\lambda>1),
\]
because both summands are positive.
Hence \(y_+\) is strictly increasing on \((1,\infty)\).

Now suppose \(y=y_-(q)=y_+(p)\) with \(0<p<q\).
Then
\[
y_+(q)>y_-(q)=y=y_+(p).
\]
By the strict monotonicity of \(y_+\), this implies \(q>p\).
Therefore the root on the minus-sign sheet is the larger of the two positive roots for \(y>1\).
\end{proof}


\subsection{Algebraic dominance certificate on the positive axis}\label{subsec:positive-axis-dominance}


\begin{proposition}[Finite interval certificates on the positive axis]\label{prop:finite-interval-certificates}
For the spectral quartic \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\), the positive real axis is certified by the three rational cells
\[
(0,1),\qquad \{1\},\qquad (1,\infty).
\]
On these cells the following exact data hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The discriminant and endpoint resultants are
\begin{align}
\Disc_\lambda \Pi
&=-y(y-1)c(y),\label{eq:positive-axis-discriminant}\\
\Pi(-1,y)&=y(y-1),
&\Pi(0,y)&=y(y+1),
&\Pi(1,y)&=y(y-1),\label{eq:positive-axis-resultants}
\end{align}
where \(c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\).
Moreover \(c(y)>0\) for \(y\ge0\).
Thus \(\gcd(\Pi,\partial_\lambda\Pi)=1\) for \(y\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)\), and the only positive-axis multiple fibers are the rational endpoints \(y=0,1\).
\item For \(0<y<1\), one has \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi>0\) and hence four real simple roots.  Indeed, for a real monic quartic with simple roots, the discriminant \(\prod_{i<j}(r_i-r_j)^2\) is positive when the number of non-real conjugate pairs is even and negative when it is odd; the sign changes below then force at least four real roots.  The signs in \eqref{eq:positive-axis-resultants} are
\[
\Pi(-1,y)<0,
\qquad
\Pi(0,y)>0,
\qquad
\Pi(1,y)<0.
\]
Together with \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\to+\infty\) as \(\lambda\to\pm\infty\), these signs put exactly one root in each of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-axis-root-boxes}
(-\infty,-1),
\qquad
(-1,0),
\qquad
(0,1),
\qquad
(1,\infty).
\end{equation}
The signs themselves are witnessed by the explicit factorizations
\begin{gather*}
\Pi(-1,y)=-y(1-y),
\quad
\Pi(0,y)=y(y+1),\\
\Pi(1,y)=-y(1-y)
\qquad(0<y<1),
\end{gather*}
and the discriminant sign by
\[
\Disc_\lambda\Pi=y(1-y)c(y)>0
\qquad(0<y<1),
\]
with \(c(y)>0\) on \([0,\infty)\).
\item For \(y=1\), the exact gcd certificate is
\[
\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2,
\qquad
\gcd(\Pi(\lambda,1),\partial_\lambda\Pi(\lambda,1))=\lambda+1.
\]
\item For \(y>1\), one has \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi<0\), so by the same real-quartic discriminant sign convention there are exactly two real simple roots and one non-real conjugate pair.  The real-phase diagram places the real roots on the noncompact oval, hence at positive abscissae \(0<p<q\).  Equivalently, the finite obstruction values for real-root changes are exactly \(0,1,\ySub\), and no further positive value occurs because \(c(y)>0\) on \([0,\infty)\).
More intrinsically, both real roots are positive without appealing to a drawing of the oval: for \(x\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots}
\Pi(-x,y)=x^4+x^3-(2y+1)x^2-x+y(y+1)>0
\qquad (y>1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, as a quadratic polynomial in \(y\), the right-hand side is
\[
y^2+(1-2x^2)y+x^4+x^3-x^2-x.
\]
If \(0\le x^2\le 3/2\), it is increasing for \(y\ge1\) and its value at \(y=1\) is
\[
x^4+x^3-3x^2-x+2=(x-1)^2(x+1)(x+2)
\]
which is nonnegative; strict positivity for \(y>1\) follows from the positive derivative except at the harmless endpoint value.
If \(x^2>3/2\), its minimum over all real \(y\) occurs at \(y=x^2-1/2>1\) and equals
\[
\frac14(4x^3-4x-1)>0,
\]
because \(x>\sqrt{3/2}\).
Thus \(\Pi\) has no non-positive real root when \(y>1\), and the two real roots forced by the discriminant sign must satisfy \(0<p<q\).
\item Opposite-modulus coincidences away from the branch endpoints are also excluded exactly by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:opposite-modulus-resultant}
\Res_x\bigl(\Pi(x,y),\Pi(-x,y)\bigr)
=16y^3(y-1)^2(y+1).
\end{equation}
Here no black-box elimination is needed: since
\[
\Pi(-x,y)-\Pi(x,y)=2x(x^2-1),
\]
and \(\Pi\) is monic of degree four,
\[
\Res_x(\Pi(x,y),\Pi(-x,y))
=\Res_x(\Pi(x,y),2x(x^2-1)).
\]
By multiplicativity of the resultant in the second variable this equals
\[
2^4\Res_x(\Pi,x)\Res_x(\Pi,x-1)\Res_x(\Pi,x+1)
=16\Pi(0,y)\Pi(1,y)\Pi(-1,y),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:opposite-modulus-resultant} by \eqref{eq:positive-axis-resultants}.
Thus on \((0,1)\) the comparison with the negative root cannot degenerate except at the already isolated endpoint \(y=1\), and on \((1,\infty)\) the non-real pair cannot collide with the positive branch through an unrecorded finite real obstruction.
\item The displayed sign, discriminant, positivity, and resultant identities form the primary finite algebraic certificate cover of the positive axis.
Appendix~\ref{app:positive-axis-sturm} records an independent primitive Sturm and pseudo-remainder certificate for reproducibility; it is not used to derive the root-count cases above.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The formula for \(\Disc_\lambda\Pi\) is the discriminant computation of Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-reduction}; the three endpoint resultants are direct evaluations of \(\Pi\) at \(\lambda=-1,0,1\).
By Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-branch-real-root}, the cubic \(c\) has its unique real zero in \((-2,-1)\), and since \(c(0)=32>0\), it is positive on \([0,\infty)\).
The gcd assertion follows because a monic polynomial and its derivative have a common root exactly when the discriminant vanishes.

For \(0<y<1\), the sign changes at \(-1,0,1\), together with \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\to+\infty\) as \(\lambda\to\pm\infty\), give at least one real root in each interval of \eqref{eq:positive-axis-root-boxes}.
Since \(\Pi\) is a quartic, these are exactly the four roots, and all are simple by the non-vanishing discriminant.
The factorization at \(y=1\) is immediate by multiplication, and the gcd is then visible.
For \(y>1\), the discriminant is negative, so exactly one non-real conjugate pair is present and exactly two roots are real; the displayed positivity certificate \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots} puts both of them on the positive axis.
The resultant identity \eqref{eq:opposite-modulus-resultant} follows from the displayed computation by multiplicativity of resultants, and its positive zeros are only the already isolated endpoint values \(0\) and \(1\).
The preceding algebraic argument proves that the three cells listed in the statement are a reconstructible finite certificate cover of the positive axis.
The appendix certificate, Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-sturm-chain}, is an independent reproducibility check: it records the corresponding primitive Sturm data and shows directly that the few denominators introduced by Euclidean division do not create additional root-count cells.
\end{proof}


\begin{proposition}[Positive-axis algebraic certificate]\label{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate}
The dominance proof on the positive axis uses exactly the following symbolic certificate.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The parameter cells are
\[
(0,1),\qquad \{1\},\qquad (1,\infty),
\]
with algebraic endpoints $0$ and $1$.
The only other real branch value is $\ySub$, the unique real zero of $c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32$, and it lies in $(-2,-1)$; hence it is not a positive-axis endpoint.
\item The sign certificates used on the cells are
\begin{gather*}
\Pi(-1,y)=y(y-1),
\quad \Pi(0,y)=y(y+1),
\quad \Pi(1,y)=y(y-1),\\
\Disc_\lambda\Pi=-y(y-1)c(y),\\
\Res_x(\Pi(x,y),\Pi(-x,y))=16y^3(y-1)^2(y+1).
\end{gather*}
Thus on $(0,1)$ the signs are $\Pi(-1,y)<0$, $\Pi(0,y)>0$, $\Pi(1,y)<0$, and $\Disc_\lambda\Pi>0$; on $(1,\infty)$ one has $\Disc_\lambda\Pi<0$, $c(y)>0$, and the displayed resultant is nonzero.
At the endpoint $y=1$ the exact fibre is
\[
\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2.
\]
\item The inequalities used to compare moduli are the symbolic inequalities
\[
\partial_\lambda\Pi(-x,y)
=-4x^3-3x^2+2(2y+1)x+1
\le -(x-1)(4x^2+7x+1)
\]
for $x\ge1$ and $0<y<1$, with strict negativity for $x>1$ and with exact endpoint value $4(y-1)<0$ at $x=1$, together with
\[
\Pi(-r_4,y)-\Pi(r_4,y)=2r_4(r_4^2-1)>0
\]
for the positive root $r_4>1$.
For $y>1$ the sign condition excluding non-positive real roots is
\[
\Pi(-x,y)>0\qquad (x\ge0),
\]
proved in \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots}.  The proof of \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots} uses only the displayed identities
\[
\Pi(-x,y)=y^2+(1-2x^2)y+x^4+x^3-x^2-x,
\]
\begin{gather*}
\Pi(-x,1)=(x-1)^2(x+1)(x+2),\\
\min_y \Pi(-x,y)=\frac{4x^3-4x-1}{4}
\quad(x^2>3/2),
\end{gather*}
where the minimum is taken at $y=x^2-1/2$.  After factoring the two positive roots as $0<p<q$, the non-real pair $z,\overline z$ satisfies
\[
\abs z^2=pq-\frac{pq+1}{p+q}<pq<q^2.
\]
This identity follows by comparing coefficients in
\begin{align*}
\Pi(\lambda,y)&=(\lambda-p)(\lambda-q)(\lambda^2+a\lambda+\abs z^2),\\
a&=p+q-1,
\end{align*}
and using the coefficient of \(\lambda\), namely
\[
(p+q)\abs z^2-(p+q-1)pq=-1.
\]
\item The denominators introduced only in the reproducibility Sturm chain of Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-sturm-chain} are powers of
\[
16,
\qquad
16y+11,
\qquad
256,
\qquad
(y^2-17y-2)^2.
\]
On $y>0$, $16$, $256$, and $16y+11$ are positive.
The last denominator vanishes at the single positive algebraic point
\[
\alpha=\frac{17+\sqrt{297}}{2},
\]
but $\alpha\ne1$ and $c(\alpha)>0$, because the only real zero of $c$ is $\ySub<0$.
Therefore $\Disc_\lambda\Pi(\cdot,\alpha)=-\alpha(\alpha-1)c(\alpha)\ne0$, so $\alpha$ is not a root-count or dominance cell boundary.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently the positive-axis dominance argument has no unlisted cells, no unverified denominator sign, and no hidden algebraic endpoint.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two items are exactly the discriminant, endpoint-resultant, and branch-cubic signs proved in Proposition~\ref{prop:finite-interval-certificates}; Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-branch-real-root} supplies $\ySub\in(-2,-1)$ and hence $c(y)>0$ for $y\ge0$.
The third item is the full list of inequalities used in Lemma~\ref{lem:positive-axis-dominance-certificate}: for $0<y<1$ it rules out the negative root as a modulus competitor, and for $y>1$ it puts the two real roots on the positive axis and bounds the modulus of the residual conjugate pair.
The fourth item is read from the displayed Sturm chain in Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-sturm-chain}; the only positive zero of $y^2-17y-2$ is $\alpha$, and the discriminant check above shows that the fibre is simple there.
Thus clearing denominators and passing between the symbolic inequalities and the Sturm reproducibility data introduces no additional positive-axis case.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Displayed sufficiency of the positive-axis certificates]\label{prop:displayed-positive-axis-certificate-sufficiency}
The finite certificate data in Propositions~\ref{prop:finite-interval-certificates} and~\ref{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate} are independently checkable from the displayed identities alone.
More precisely, after the fixed quartic \(\Pi\) and the cubic \(c(y)=256y^3+411y^2+165y+32\) have been written down, the following operations are sufficient and no undisplayed certificate is used:
\begin{enumerate}
\item evaluate \(\Pi\) at \(\lambda=-1,0,1\), multiply the factorization at \(y=1\), and use \(c(y)>0\) for \(y\ge0\) to obtain the positive-axis cells and root counts;
\item use the identity \(\Pi(-x,y)-\Pi(x,y)=2x(x^2-1)\) and multiplicativity of the resultant to obtain \eqref{eq:opposite-modulus-resultant};
\item use the displayed derivative inequality on \((-\infty,-1]\), the positivity identity \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots}, and the coefficient comparison in \((\lambda-p)(\lambda-q)(\lambda^2+a\lambda+\abs z^2)\) to prove all modulus comparisons;
\item use the primitive pseudo-remainder identities in Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-sturm-chain} only as an independent verification of denominator clearing, not as an additional hidden hypothesis.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently every sign, resultant, denominator, and modulus comparison entering Theorem~\ref{thm:consolidated-branch-dominance-package} is a displayed algebraic identity or an immediate inequality between displayed expressions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Item~(1) is exactly the content of \eqref{eq:positive-axis-discriminant}, \eqref{eq:positive-axis-resultants}, the displayed factorization \(\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2\), and Lemma~\ref{lem:cubic-branch-real-root}, which gives the unique real zero of \(c\) in \((-2,-1)\) and hence \(c(y)>0\) on \([0,\infty)\).
These displayed signs force the root boxes on \((0,1)\); on \((1,\infty)\), the negative discriminant and \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots} give exactly two positive real roots and one conjugate pair.
Item~(2) is the computation in \eqref{eq:opposite-modulus-resultant}: because \(\Pi(-x,y)-\Pi(x,y)=2x(x^2-1)\), a common root of \(\Pi(x,y)\) and \(\Pi(-x,y)\) is detected by the displayed factors \(x\), \(x-1\), and \(x+1\), and multiplicativity gives \(16\Pi(0,y)\Pi(1,y)\Pi(-1,y)\).
For item~(3), when \(0<y<1\), the derivative computation shows \(\partial_\lambda\Pi(-x,y)<0\) for all \(x\ge1\): the displayed upper bound is strict for \(x>1\), while at \(x=1\) the derivative equals \(4(y-1)<0\).
Together with \(\Pi(-r_4,y)-\Pi(r_4,y)=2r_4(r_4^2-1)>0\), this gives \(|r_1|<r_4\).
When \(y>1\), \eqref{eq:ygt1-no-negative-roots} puts the two real roots at \(0<p<q\), and the displayed coefficient comparison gives \(\abs z^2=pq-(pq+1)/(p+q)<pq<q^2\), so the conjugate pair is also subdominant.
Item~(4) follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-sturm-chain}: its pseudo-remainders are displayed after denominator clearing, and its proof checks that the only positive zero introduced by a Euclidean denominator is not a discriminant zero.
Thus no step in the dominance package appeals to data beyond the displayed algebra.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma}[Interval dominance certificate]\label{lem:positive-axis-dominance-certificate}
For each \(y>0\), the root on the branch \(\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)>1\) is strictly larger in modulus than every other root of \(\Pi(\cdot,y)\).  More explicitly:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \(0<y<1\), the roots are real and ordered
\[
r_1<r_2<r_3<r_4,
\qquad
r_1<-1<r_2<0<r_3<1<r_4,
\]
with \(r_4=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)\), and
\[
\abs{r_1},\abs{r_2},\abs{r_3}<r_4.
\]
\item If \(y=1\), then
\[
\Pi(\lambda,1)=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-1)(\lambda+1)^2,
\]
and \(2=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(1)\) is the unique root of maximal modulus.
\item If \(y>1\), the two real roots are positive, say \(0<p<q\), the remaining two roots are a non-real conjugate pair \(z,\overline z\), one has \(q=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)\), and
\[
p<q,
\qquad
\abs z<q.
\]
\end{enumerate}
Together with the certificate in Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate}, the intervals \((0,1)\), \(\{1\}\), and \((1,\infty)\) give a complete branch-comparison cover of the positive axis.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(0<y<1\), Corollary~\ref{cor:real-phase-diagram} gives the displayed ordering of the four real roots, and Theorem~\ref{thm:positive-maximal-modulus-branch} identifies \(r_4\) as the unique root greater than \(1\).
It remains only to rule out dominance by the negative root.
For \(x\ge1\),
\begin{align*}
\partial_\lambda\Pi(-x,y)
&=-4x^3-3x^2+2(2y+1)x+1\\
&\le -4x^3-3x^2+6x+1
=-(x-1)(4x^2+7x+1).
\end{align*}
This upper bound is strictly negative for \(x>1\), while at \(x=1\) the exact derivative is \(4(y-1)<0\); hence \(\partial_\lambda\Pi(-x,y)<0\) for every \(x\ge1\).
Hence \(\lambda\mapsto\Pi(\lambda,y)\) is strictly decreasing on \((-\infty,-1]\).  Since \(r_4>1\) is a root,
\[
\Pi(-r_4,y)-\Pi(r_4,y)=2r_4(r_4^2-1)>0,
\]
so \(\Pi(-r_4,y)>0\).  The decreasing function \(\Pi(\lambda,y)\) vanishes at \(r_1\in(-\infty,-1)\); therefore \(r_1>-r_4\), and \(\abs{r_1}<r_4\).  The inequalities \(\abs{r_2}<1<r_4\) and \(0<r_3<r_4\) are immediate from the ordering.

At \(y=1\) the stated factorization follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:ep-classification}(2), and \(2\) is visibly the unique root of largest modulus.  The equality \(2=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(1)\) follows from \(y_{\mathrm{dom}}(2)=1\).

For \(y>1\), Corollary~\ref{cor:real-phase-diagram} says that only the noncompact oval contributes real points, so the two real roots are positive; write them as \(0<p<q\).  Lemma~\ref{lem:ygt1-sheet-comparison} identifies the larger positive root as the one on the minus-sign sheet, hence \(q=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)\).  Factor
\[
\Pi(\lambda,y)=(\lambda-p)(\lambda-q)(\lambda^2+a\lambda+b),
\]
where \(b=\abs z^2>0\).  Comparing coefficients of \(\lambda^3\) and \(\lambda\) gives
\[
a=p+q-1,
\qquad
b=\frac{(p+q-1)pq-1}{p+q}
=pq-\frac{pq+1}{p+q}<pq<q^2.
\]
Thus \(\abs z=\sqrt b<q\), and the remaining real root also satisfies \(p<q\).  These three cases exhaust \((0,\infty)\), proving the certificate.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Global dominance on the positive axis]\label{prop:global-dominance}
For every $y>0$, the root $\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)$ is the spectral radius:
\[
r(y)=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y).
\]
Hence the branch parameterized by \eqref{eq:y-phys} is exactly the maximal-modulus branch over $y>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:positive-axis-dominance-certificate}, with the algebraic certificate in Proposition~\ref{prop:positive-axis-algebraic-certificate}, supplies a complete interval-by-interval comparison: on \((0,1)\) it compares the positive branch with the possible negative competitor, at \(y=1\) it handles the reducible boundary explicitly, and on \((1,\infty)\) it compares the larger positive root with the non-real conjugate pair.
In each interval the branch \(\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)\) is strictly larger in modulus than every other root of \(\Pi(\cdot,y)\).  Therefore the spectral radius is \(r(y)=\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)\) for all \(y>0\), and the parametrized branch \eqref{eq:y-phys} is exactly the positive-real maximal-modulus sheet over the whole positive axis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Endpoint at \texorpdfstring{\(y=0\)}{y=0} and Puiseux expansion}\label{subsec:endpoint-puiseux}

The endpoint $y = 0$ is not an ordinary analytic point of the inverse function $\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)$, but a square-root ramification point.
The local expansion is a classical Puiseux series \cite{BrieskornKnorrer1986,Walker1950}.

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint Puiseux expansion at \(y=0\)]\label{prop:endpoint-puiseux}
Setting $\lambda = 1 + \varepsilon$, the maximal-modulus branch satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y-puiseux}
y_{\mathrm{dom}}(1 + \varepsilon)
= 2\varepsilon^2 - 5\varepsilon^3 + 21\varepsilon^4 - 94\varepsilon^5 + O(\varepsilon^6).
\end{equation}
Inverting, the maximal-modulus eigenvalue on the maximal-modulus branch over \(y>0\) expands as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-puiseux}
\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)
= 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,y^{1/2}
+ \frac{5}{8}\,y
- \frac{43}{64\sqrt{2}}\,y^{3/2}
- \frac{1}{16}\,y^2
+ O(y^{5/2}).
\end{equation}
The logarithmic spectral radius
$f_{\mathrm{dom}}(y) = \log\lambda_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)$
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f-puiseux}
f_{\mathrm{dom}}(y)
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,y^{1/2}
+ \frac{3}{8}\,y
- \frac{217\sqrt{2}}{384}\,y^{3/2}
+ O(y^2),
\end{equation}
and the scaled logarithmic slope
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-endpoint}
\sigma(y):=y\,f_{\mathrm{dom}}'(y)
= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,y^{1/2}
+ \frac{3}{8}\,y
- \frac{217\sqrt{2}}{256}\,y^{3/2}
+ O(y^2).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At $\lambda = 1 + \varepsilon$,
\[
4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1
= 1 + 8\varepsilon + 12\varepsilon^2 + 4\varepsilon^3.
\]
Taking the square root by the binomial series:
\[
\sqrt{4\lambda^3 - 4\lambda + 1}
= 1 + 4\varepsilon - 2\varepsilon^2 + 10\varepsilon^3 - 42\varepsilon^4 + 188\varepsilon^5 + O(\varepsilon^6).
\]
Substituting into \eqref{eq:y-phys}:
\begin{align*}
y_{\mathrm{dom}}(1 + \varepsilon)
&= (1 + \varepsilon)^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}
  - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + 4\varepsilon - 2\varepsilon^2 + 10\varepsilon^3 - 42\varepsilon^4 + 188\varepsilon^5 + \cdots\bigr) \\
&= 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}
  - \tfrac{1}{2} - 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 - 5\varepsilon^3 + 21\varepsilon^4 - 94\varepsilon^5 - \cdots \\
&= 2\varepsilon^2 - 5\varepsilon^3 + 21\varepsilon^4 - 94\varepsilon^5 + O(\varepsilon^6).
\end{align*}
To invert the series, write
\[
\varepsilon = A y^{1/2} + B y + C y^{3/2} + D y^2 + O(y^{5/2}).
\]
Substituting this ansatz into \eqref{eq:y-puiseux} and matching the coefficients of
\(
y,\ y^{3/2},\ y^2,\ y^{5/2}
\)
gives
\[
2A^2=1,\qquad 4AB-5A^3=0,
\]
\[
4AC+2B^2-15A^2B+21A^4=0,
\]
\[
4AD+4BC-15A^2C-15AB^2+84A^3B-94A^5=0.
\]
Choosing the positive branch \(A=1/\sqrt2\) and solving successively yields
\[
A=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\qquad
B=\frac58,\qquad
C=-\frac{43}{64\sqrt2},\qquad
D=-\frac{1}{16},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:lambda-puiseux}.
Then
\[
\log(1+\varepsilon)=\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^3}{3}+O(y^2)
\]
gives \eqref{eq:f-puiseux}, and term-by-term differentiation yields \eqref{eq:rho-endpoint}.
\end{proof}

Equation \eqref{eq:rho-endpoint} shows that the scaled logarithmic slope does not open linearly from the endpoint \(y=0\), but with a square-root singularity:
\[
\sigma(y) \sim \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,y^{1/2}
\qquad (y \to 0^+).
\]

\subsection{Standard square-root branching and local cosine law}\label{subsec:endpoint-quantization}

The same square-root branch point controls the first zeros of \(Z_m\) on the negative real axis.
There are two distinct ingredients.
First, the spectral polynomial itself forces a square-root splitting whenever \(\partial_\lambda P\) vanishes simply in the \(y\)-direction.
Second, once a maximal-modulus pair with symmetric amplitudes has been isolated and the remaining branches are shown to be uniformly smaller on the complex microscopic scale, the polynomial family reduces to a shifted cosine law.
The next two lemmas are standard local tools stated only in the specialized form needed later:
Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-ep2-branching} is the usual Puiseux square-root branching at a simple double point \cite{Walker1950,BrieskornKnorrer1986}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} is a localized two-branch version of the standard Beraha--Kahane--Weiss analysis of zeros of spectral sums \cite{BerahaKahaneWeiss1975,BerahaKahaneWeiss1978,Sokal2004}.
In the BKW--Sokal mechanism, limiting zeros occur where two or more dominant spectral branches have equal modulus, provided the corresponding amplitudes do not remove the leading competition; the lemma below is the microscopic square-root endpoint specialization of that mechanism after the global dominance estimates have already reduced the spectral sum to one conjugate pair.

\begin{lemma}[Generic square-root branching at a simple double point]\label{lem:generic-ep2-branching}
Let \(P(\lambda,y)\) be holomorphic near \((\lambda_c,y_c)\in\CC^2\), and assume
\[
P(\lambda_c,y_c)=P_\lambda(\lambda_c,y_c)=0,
\qquad
P_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_c,y_c)P_y(\lambda_c,y_c)\neq 0.
\]
Then, after setting \(y=y_c+s^2\), there exist \(s_0>0\) and holomorphic branches \(\lambda_\pm(s)\) for \(|s|<s_0\) such that
\[
P(\lambda_\pm(s),y_c+s^2)=0,
\qquad
\lambda_\pm(0)=\lambda_c,
\]
and
\[
\lambda_\pm(s)=\lambda_c\pm \alpha s+\beta s^2+O(s^3),
\qquad
\alpha^2=-\frac{2P_y}{P_{\lambda\lambda}}(\lambda_c,y_c).
\]
After relabeling, one may choose the branches so that \(\lambda_-(s)=\lambda_+(-s)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write \(h=\lambda-\lambda_c\).
Taylor expansion at \((\lambda_c,y_c)\) gives
\[
P(\lambda_c+h,y_c+s^2)
=
\frac{1}{2}P_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_c,y_c)h^2
+P_y(\lambda_c,y_c)s^2
+R(h,s),
\]
where
\[
R(h,s)=O\!\bigl(|h|^3+|h||s|^2+|s|^4\bigr).
\]
Set \(h=su\) and define
\[
G(u,s)=
\begin{cases}
s^{-2}P(\lambda_c+su,y_c+s^2), & s\neq 0,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}P_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_c,y_c)u^2+P_y(\lambda_c,y_c), & s=0.
\end{cases}
\]
The remainder estimate shows that \(G\) is holomorphic near \((u,s)=(\pm\alpha,0)\), where \(\alpha\) is any square root of \(-2P_y/P_{\lambda\lambda}\).
Moreover,
\[
G(\pm\alpha,0)=0,
\qquad
\partial_u G(\pm\alpha,0)=P_{\lambda\lambda}(\lambda_c,y_c)(\pm\alpha)\neq 0.
\]
By the implicit-function theorem, there exist holomorphic functions \(u_\pm(s)=\pm\alpha+O(s)\) such that
\[
G(u_\pm(s),s)=0.
\]
Setting \(\lambda_\pm(s)=\lambda_c+s\,u_\pm(s)\) yields
\[
\lambda_\pm(s)=\lambda_c\pm\alpha s+\beta_\pm s^2+O(s^3).
\]
Replacing \(s\) by \(-s\) leaves \(y_c+s^2\) unchanged and interchanges the two roots issuing from \(\lambda_c\).
After relabeling, this gives \(\lambda_-(s)=\lambda_+(-s)\), and hence \(\beta_-=\beta_+=:\beta\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Localized two-branch oscillation law]\label{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}
Let \(y_c\in\RR\), and let \(\{Z_m(y)\}_{m\ge 0}\) be holomorphic in a neighborhood of \(y_c\).
Assume also that \(Z_m(y)\) is real for real \(y\) near \(y_c\).
Assume that there exists \(s_0>0\) such that, for \(|s|<s_0\),
\[
Z_m(y_c+s^2)=C_+(s)\lambda_+(s)^m + C_-(s)\lambda_-(s)^m + R_m(y_c+s^2),
\]
where \(\lambda_\pm\) and \(C_\pm\) are holomorphic on \(|s|<s_0\), the branches are labelled so that
\[
\lambda_-(s)=\lambda_+(-s),
\qquad
C_-(s)=C_+(-s),
\]
and they satisfy
\[
\lambda_\pm(s)=\lambda_c\exp\!\left(\pm as + bs^2 + O(s^3)\right),
\qquad
C_\pm(s)=A_*\mp B_*s+O(s^2),
\]
with real constants \(\lambda_c>0\), \(a>0\), \(b\in\RR\), \(A_*\neq 0\), and \(B_*\in\RR\).
After possibly decreasing \(s_0\), assume also that \(\lambda_\pm(s)\ne0\) and \(C_\pm(s)\ne0\) on \(|s|<s_0\).
Assume moreover that for every compact \(K\subset\CC\) there exists \(M_K>0\) such that
\[
\sup_{u\in K}
\left|
R_m\!\left(y_c-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)
\right|
\le M_K \lambda_c^m m^{-2}
\]
for all sufficiently large \(m\).
Define
\[
F_m(u):=
\frac{e^{b u^2/m}}{2A_*\lambda_c^m}
Z_m\!\left(y_c-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right).
\]
Then, for every fixed compact \(K\subset\CC\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:universal-ep2-scaling}
F_m(u)
=
\cos\!\left(
a u-\frac{B_*}{A_*}\frac{u}{m}
\right)+O_K(m^{-2})
\end{equation}
uniformly for \(u\in K\).
Fix \(k\ge 1\) and set
\[
q_k:=\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{2},
\qquad
u_k^{(0)}:=\frac{q_k}{a}.
\]
Then there exist \(\eta_k>0\) and \(m_k\) such that, for every \(m\ge m_k\), the function \(F_m\) has exactly one zero \(u_{m,k}\) in the disc
\[
\{u\in\CC:\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}<\eta_k\},
\]
this zero is real and positive, the zeros \(u_{m,1},\ldots,u_{m,k}\) are ordered increasingly for \(m\) large, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:universal-ep2-u}
u_{m,k}
=
\frac{q_k}{a}
\left(
1+\frac{B_*}{aA_*m}
\right)
+O_k(m^{-2}),
\end{equation}
whence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:universal-ep2-y}
y_{m,k}
=
y_c-\frac{q_k^2}{a^2m^2}
-\frac{2B_*q_k^2}{a^3A_*m^3}
+O_k(m^{-4}).
\end{equation}
For every fixed integer \(K_0\ge1\), the constants and thresholds in \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-u} and \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-y} may be chosen uniformly for all \(1\le k\le K_0\) by taking the finitely many Rouch\'e discs disjoint and then taking the maximum of their constants and thresholds.
No uniformity is claimed for \(K_0\) depending on \(m\) or for the infinite family of indices.
Moreover, if \(U>0\) is fixed and is not a cosine-zero threshold, in the sense that \(aU\neq q_j\) for every \(j\ge1\), and if
\[
J(U):=\{j\ge1:q_j/a<U\},
\]
then, for all sufficiently large \(m\), the only zeros of \(F_m\) in the fixed closed half-window
\[
\{u\in\CC:0\le \Re u\le U,\ \abs{\Im u}\le U\}
\]
are the zeros \(u_{m,j}\) with \(j\in J(U)\).
No assertion is made here for windows whose endpoint \(U\) grows with \(m\), and no zero-density statement on a macroscopic negative interval is implied.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a compact set \(K\subset\CC\) and set \(s=iu/m\), so that \(y=y_c-u^2/m^2\).
For all sufficiently large \(m\), the set \(iK/m\) lies in \(|s|<s_0\), so the chosen branch and amplitude labels are used on a single domain throughout the compact window.
Because the expansions are holomorphic in \(s\), the \(O(s^3)\) and \(O(s^2)\) remainders are uniform on compact subsets of \(\CC\) near \(0\).
The nonvanishing of \(\lambda_\pm\) permits the logarithmic form on this disc, and the nonvanishing of \(C_\pm\) ensures that the leading amplitude \(A_*\) cannot be cancelled by a zero of either maximal branch.
Therefore,
\[
\lambda_\pm\!\left(\frac{iu}{m}\right)^m
=
\lambda_c^m
\exp\!\left(
\pm iau-\frac{bu^2}{m}+O_K(m^{-2})
\right),
\]
and similarly
\[
C_\pm\!\left(\frac{iu}{m}\right)
=
A_*\mp iB_*\frac{u}{m}+O_K(m^{-2}).
\]
Multiplying and adding the two maximal-modulus contributions gives
\begin{align*}
\frac{e^{b u^2/m}}{2A_*\lambda_c^m}
\bigl(C_+\lambda_+^m + C_-\lambda_-^m\bigr)
&=
\cos(au)+\frac{B_*}{A_*}\frac{u}{m}\sin(au)+O_K(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
Using
\[
\cos(\theta-\delta)=\cos\theta+\delta\sin\theta+O(\delta^2),
\qquad
\delta=\frac{B_*}{A_*}\frac{u}{m},
\]
we obtain
\[
\frac{e^{b u^2/m}}{2A_*\lambda_c^m}
\bigl(C_+\lambda_+^m + C_-\lambda_-^m\bigr)
=
g_m(u)+O_K(m^{-2}),
\]
where
\[
g_m(u):=\cos\!\left(a u-\frac{B_*}{A_*}\frac{u}{m}\right).
\]
The assumed bound on \(R_m\) then gives \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-scaling}.

Now fix \(k\ge1\).
Choose \(\eta_k>0\) so small that \(\cos(au)\) has no zero on the circle \(\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k\).
Then
\[
\delta_k:=\inf_{\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k}\abs{\cos(au)}>0.
\]
Since \(g_m\to \cos(au)\) uniformly on that circle, there exists \(m_k\) such that for all \(m\ge m_k\),
\[
\abs{g_m(u)}\ge \frac{\delta_k}{2}
\qquad
\text{whenever }\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-scaling} gives a matching bound
\[
\abs{F_m(u)-g_m(u)}\le \frac{\delta_k}{4}
\qquad
\text{whenever }\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k
\]
for all sufficiently large \(m\).
Therefore
\[
\abs{F_m(u)}\ge \abs{g_m(u)}-\abs{F_m(u)-g_m(u)}\ge \frac{\delta_k}{4}
\qquad
\text{whenever }\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k.
\]
Rouch\'e's theorem applied to the positively oriented circle \(\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}=\eta_k\) now implies that \(F_m\) and \(g_m\) have the same number of zeros in \(\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}<\eta_k\).
Since \(g_m(u)=0\) exactly when
\[
au-\frac{B_*}{A_*}\frac{u}{m}=q_k
\]
and the affine phase has a unique solution
\[
u_{m,k}^{(0)}:=\frac{q_k}{a-\frac{B_*}{A_*m}}
\]
in that disc for \(m\) large, \(F_m\) has exactly one zero \(u_{m,k}\) there.

Because \(Z_m\) is real on the real axis and the constants in the normalization of \(F_m\) are real, Schwarz reflection gives
\[
F_m(\overline u)=\overline{F_m(u)}
\]
on every sufficiently small conjugation-invariant compact set in the \(u\)-plane.
The disc \(\abs{u-u_k^{(0)}}<\eta_k\) is centered on the real axis.
If its unique zero were non-real, its complex conjugate would be a distinct zero in the same disc, contradicting uniqueness.
Hence \(u_{m,k}\in\RR\).
Since \(u_{m,k}=u_k^{(0)}+o(1)\) and \(u_k^{(0)}>0\), this zero is positive for \(m\) large.
For any fixed \(k\), the centers satisfy
\[
0<u_1^{(0)}<u_2^{(0)}<\cdots<u_k^{(0)}.
\]
Choosing the finitely many radii \(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_k\) disjoint and then taking \(m\) large gives
\[
0<u_{m,1}<u_{m,2}<\cdots<u_{m,k}.
\]

Moreover,
\[
g_m'(u_{m,k}^{(0)})
=
-\left(a-\frac{B_*}{A_*m}\right)\sin q_k
\]
stays uniformly away from \(0\).
Since \(F_m(u_{m,k})=0\) and \(F_m-g_m=O_k(m^{-2})\) uniformly on the disc, a first-order Taylor expansion about \(u_{m,k}^{(0)}\) gives
\[
u_{m,k}=u_{m,k}^{(0)}+O_k(m^{-2}).
\]
Expanding \(u_{m,k}^{(0)}\) yields \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-u}, and squaring \(u_{m,k}\) gives \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-y}.

It remains to prove the compact-window exclusion.
Fix \(U>0\) with \(aU\neq q_j\) for every \(j\ge1\), and set
\[
Q_U:=\{u\in\CC:0\le \Re u\le U,\ \abs{\Im u}\le U\}.
\]
Only finitely many zeros of \(\cos(au)\) lie in \(Q_U\), namely the points \(u_j^{(0)}=q_j/a\) with \(j\in J(U)\).
Choose pairwise disjoint discs
\[
D_j:=\{u\in\CC:\abs{u-u_j^{(0)}}<\eta_j\}
\qquad (j\in J(U))
\]
with closures contained in the interior of \(Q_U\).
The complement
\[
K_U:=Q_U\setminus\bigcup_{j\in J(U)}D_j
\]
is compact and contains no zero of \(\cos(au)\).
Hence
\[
\delta_U:=\inf_{u\in K_U}\abs{\cos(au)}>0.
\]
Since \(g_m(u)=\cos((a-B_*/(A_*m))u)\) converges uniformly to \(\cos(au)\) on \(Q_U\), and \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-scaling} gives \(F_m-g_m=O_{Q_U}(m^{-2})\), we have
\[
\abs{F_m(u)}\ge \frac{\delta_U}{3}
\qquad (u\in K_U)
\]
for all sufficiently large \(m\).
Thus \(F_m\) has no zeros in \(K_U\).
The preceding Rouch\'e argument gives exactly one zero in each disc \(D_j\) and no other zeros in \(Q_U\), which proves the compact-window exclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Endpoint cosine-law hypotheses]\label{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses}
For the quartic transfer family at the endpoint \((\lambda_c,y_c)=(1,0)\), Appendix~\ref{app:endpoint-branching} is part of the proof of the endpoint theorem and supplies every hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}.  The dependency on the appendix is the following equation-level list of the hypotheses and constants used in that lemma.
\begin{enumerate}
\item There is a common radius \(r>0\) and the branch coordinate \(y=s^2\), \(|s|<\sqrt r\), on which the two colliding roots are single-valued holomorphic functions \(\lambda_\pm(s)\) with \(\lambda_-(s)=\lambda_+(-s)\).  This is the branch domain fixed at the start of Appendix~\ref{app:endpoint-branching} and in \eqref{eq:lambda-plus-minus}.  Shrinking \(r\) if necessary, \(\lambda_\pm(s)\ne0\) and the amplitudes below satisfy \(C_\pm(s)\ne0\) on the same disc, since \(\lambda_\pm(0)=1\) and \(C_\pm(0)=1/2\).
\item On the punctured disc \(0<\abs y<r\), the local spectral decomposition
\[
Z_m(y)=\sum_{j=1}^4 C_j(y)\lambda_j(y)^m
\]
holds for \(0<\abs y<r\), with the amplitudes given by the residue formula \eqref{eq:partial-fractions}; this is \eqref{eq:spectral-decomposition} in Appendix~\ref{app:endpoint-branching}.
\item After writing \(y=s^2\), the colliding pair is holomorphic in \(s\) and satisfies
\[
\lambda_\pm(s)=1\pm \frac{s}{\sqrt2}+\frac58s^2+O(s^3),
\qquad
\log\lambda_\pm(s)=\pm\frac{s}{\sqrt2}+\frac38s^2+O(s^3),
\]
so the exponential hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} holds with \(\lambda_c=1\), \(a=1/\sqrt2\), and \(b=3/8\); these constants are recorded in \eqref{eq:appendix-cosine-constants} and derived in \eqref{eq:lambda-plus-minus}--\eqref{eq:lambda-pm-exp}, with the endpoint-scale form in \eqref{eq:lambda-power}.
\item The two amplitudes extend holomorphically in the same coordinate, satisfy the same conjugate branch symmetry \(C_-(s)=C_+(-s)\), and obey
\[
C_\pm(s)=\frac12\mp\frac{3\sqrt2}{16}s+O(s^2),
\]
so \(A_*=1/2\ne0\) and \(B_*=3\sqrt2/16\); these constants are recorded in \eqref{eq:appendix-cosine-constants} and derived from the exact identities for \(Z_0,Z_1,Z_2,Z_3\) in \eqref{eq:Bstar-m2}--\eqref{eq:C-plus-minus}, with the endpoint-scale amplitude estimate in \eqref{eq:amplitude-scale}.
\item The two noncolliding branches satisfy, uniformly for \(u\) in any compact set \(K\subset\CC\), the preliminary bounds
\begin{gather*}
\abs{\lambda_{-1}(-u^2/m^2)+1},\quad
\abs{C_{-1}(-u^2/m^2)}=O_K(m^{-2}),\\
\abs{\lambda_0(-u^2/m^2)},\quad
\abs{C_{\mathrm{small}}(-u^2/m^2)}=O_K(m^{-2}),
\end{gather*}
which are exactly \eqref{eq:y-bound}.  The logarithmic estimate \eqref{eq:log-lambda-minus-one} then gives the contribution bound \eqref{eq:minus-one-contribution-bound}, and the small-root estimate gives \eqref{eq:small-root-contribution-bound}.
\item Combining \eqref{eq:minus-one-contribution-bound} and \eqref{eq:small-root-contribution-bound}, the total remainder satisfies, uniformly for \(u\) in any compact set \(K\subset\CC\),
\[
R_m\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)=O_K(m^{-2}),
\]
which is the remainder bound required in Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} because \(\lambda_c=1\).  In the notation of the lemma, for each compact \(K\) there is a constant \(M_K\) such that the displayed bound is at most \(M_K\lambda_c^m m^{-2}\); this is \eqref{eq:appendix-remainder-bound}.
\item The polynomial coefficients of \(Z_m(y)\) are real, indeed integral by Section~\ref{sec:model}, so the Schwarz-reflection and real-zero assertions in Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} apply to the rescaled functions.
\item The compact-window exclusion in Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} uses only the same constants \(M_K\) on the rectangles \(Q_U\) and on the finitely many Rouch\'e circles around the points \(u_k^{(0)}\).  Since these sets are compact, the appendix estimates apply uniformly on every such set.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently the localized two-branch oscillation law applies to this endpoint with no additional uniformity or amplitude assumptions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two items provide the local analytic setting required before Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} can be applied: a common branch coordinate on one disc and the partial-fraction spectral decomposition on the punctured disc.
Item~(3) is the branch-expansion hypothesis of the lemma.
Equations \eqref{eq:lambda-plus-minus} and \eqref{eq:lambda-pm-exp} give the logarithmic normal form with
\[
\lambda_c=1,
\qquad
 a=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
 b=\frac38,
\]
and \eqref{eq:lambda-power} is the same estimate after substituting \(s=iu/m\), uniformly for \(u\) in compact sets.
Item~(4) is the amplitude-symmetry hypothesis.
The residue formula \eqref{eq:partial-fractions}, the coefficient comparisons \eqref{eq:Bstar-m2}--\eqref{eq:Bstar-m3}, and the expansion \eqref{eq:C-plus-minus} give
\[
A_*=\frac12,
\qquad
B_*=\frac{3\sqrt2}{16},
\]
with the rescaled compact-uniform form \eqref{eq:amplitude-scale}.
Items~(5) and (6) verify the non-maximal-modulus remainder hypothesis: the preliminary estimates \eqref{eq:y-bound}, the contribution bounds \eqref{eq:minus-one-contribution-bound} and \eqref{eq:small-root-contribution-bound}, and their sum \eqref{eq:appendix-remainder-bound} give, for every compact \(K\subset\CC\), a constant \(M_K\) such that
\[
\abs{R_m(-u^2/m^2)}\le M_K\lambda_c^m m^{-2}
\qquad (u\in K),
\]
because \(\lambda_c=1\).
These are precisely the constants and the \(O_K\)-uniformity recorded in \eqref{eq:appendix-cosine-constants}, equivalently \eqref{eq:appendix-cosine-constants-repeated}.
Item~(7) supplies the real-coefficient hypothesis used for the Schwarz-reflection and real-zero part of the lemma, and item~(8) records that the rectangles \(Q_U\) and the finitely many Rouch\'e circles used there are compact sets to which the same \(O_K\) estimates apply.
Thus every hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} is verified with the constants displayed above, and substitution of those constants gives the parameter tuple used in Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization}.
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}[Punctured endpoint isolation]\label{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation}
Let \(U>0\) be non-threshold in the endpoint scale, meaning
\[
U\ne \frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}
\qquad (j\ge1).
\]
Then there exists \(m_U\) such that, for every \(m\ge m_U\), the negative real zeros of \(Z_m\) in the punctured endpoint window
\[
-\frac{U^2}{m^2}\le y<0
\]
are precisely
\[
y_{m,j}^{(U)}=-\frac{u_{m,j}^2}{m^2}
\qquad
\left(1\le j\le N(U)\right),
\]
where each \(u_{m,j}>0\) is the unique zero supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} near
\[
u_j^{(0)}=\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2},
\]
and
\[
N(U)=\#\left\{j\ge1:\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}<U\right\}
=\left\lfloor \frac{U}{\pi\sqrt2}+\frac12\right\rfloor .
\]
Each zero in the window is simple, and the ordering is
\[
0>y_{m,1}^{(U)}>y_{m,2}^{(U)}>\cdots>y_{m,N(U)}^{(U)}.
\]
In particular, for all sufficiently large \(m\), the window \([-U^2/m^2,0)\) contains no negative real zeros of \(Z_m\) other than this listed finite set.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses} to Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} with the endpoint constants
\[
y_c=0,
\qquad
a=\frac1{\sqrt2},
\qquad
q_j=\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2}.
\]
The non-threshold condition on \(U\) is exactly the lemma's condition \(aU\ne q_j\) for every \(j\ge1\), and the lemma gives a threshold \(m_U\) such that the only zeros of
\[
u\longmapsto F_m(u)
\]
in the compact half-rectangle
\[
Q_U=\{u\in\CC:0\le \Re u\le U,\ \abs{\Im u}\le U\}
\]
are the zeros \(u_{m,j}\) with \(q_j/a<U\).  These zeros are real, positive, ordered increasingly, and each lies in a small disc containing exactly one zero counted with multiplicity.
Hence each \(u_{m,j}\) is a simple zero of \(F_m\).

The normalizing factor in the definition of \(F_m\),
\[
\frac{e^{b u^2/m}}{2A_*\lambda_c^m},
\]
is nonzero.  Thus the zeros of \(F_m\) in \(Q_U\) are exactly the zeros of
\[
u\longmapsto Z_m\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)
\]
in the same set, with the same multiplicities.  If \(y\in[-U^2/m^2,0)\) is a real zero of \(Z_m\), then the unique positive number \(u=m\sqrt{-y}\) lies in \([0,U]\subset Q_U\) and satisfies
\[
Z_m\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)=0.
\]
The compact-window exclusion therefore forces \(u=u_{m,j}\) for some \(j\) with \(q_j/a<U\).  Conversely each such \(u_{m,j}\) gives the displayed negative real zero.

Since \(u\mapsto -u^2/m^2\) has nonzero derivative at every positive \(u_{m,j}\), simplicity is preserved after passing from the \(u\)-variable to the \(y\)-variable.  The increasing order
\[
0<u_{m,1}<u_{m,2}<\cdots<u_{m,N(U)}
\]
from Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} is reversed by \(u\mapsto -u^2/m^2\), giving the stated order of the negative real zeros.  Finally, the count formula follows from
\[
\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}<U
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
j<\frac{U}{\pi\sqrt2}+\frac12,
\]
together with the non-threshold assumption, which excludes equality at the right-hand endpoint.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Endpoint zero ordering]\label{def:endpoint-zero-ordering}
Fix \(K\ge1\), and choose any non-threshold \(U\) satisfying
\[
u_K^{(0)}=\frac{(2K-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}<U<\frac{(2K+1)\pi}{\sqrt2}=u_{K+1}^{(0)}.
\]
For all sufficiently large \(m\), Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} gives exactly the first \(K\) simple negative real zeros of \(Z_m\), counted from \(0\) toward the left within the endpoint window \([-U^2/m^2,0)\).  We denote them by
\[
0>y_{m,1}>y_{m,2}>\cdots>y_{m,K},
\]
so \(y_{m,k}\) is the \(k\)th negative real zero nearest \(0\).  This notation is independent of the chosen non-threshold \(U\), because the isolation theorem gives the same ordered zeros on overlapping endpoint windows once both thresholds are large enough.  Equivalently, if \(y_{m,k}=-u_{m,k}^2/m^2\) with \(u_{m,k}>0\), then
\[
0<u_{m,1}<u_{m,2}<\cdots<u_{m,K}.
\]
The phrase ``for each fixed \(k\)'' below means that, after fixing \(k\), the assertion holds for all \(m\ge m_k\); for a finite set \(1\le k\le K\), one may take a common threshold \(m_K\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Theorem E: local-to-global fixed-window endpoint zero law]\label{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization}
For the quartic transfer family studied here, Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses} supplies the hypotheses, using the model-specific expansion package Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package} in Appendix~\ref{app:endpoint-branching}.
With the branch coordinate chosen there, namely $y=s^2$ and the labels fixed by
\[
\lambda_+(s)=1+\frac{s}{\sqrt2}+\frac58s^2+O(s^3),
\qquad
\lambda_-(s)=\lambda_+(-s),
\]
the double-root endpoint at $y_c=0$ satisfies the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} with
\[
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
b=\frac38,
\qquad
A_*=\frac12,
\qquad
B_*=\frac{3\sqrt2}{16}.
\]
Equivalently, on the negative endpoint scale used below one takes $s=iu/m$ when $y=-u^2/m^2$, with the square-root branch fixed by the displayed labels for $\lambda_\pm(s)$.
For every fixed compact set $K\subset\CC$ the following expansion is uniform for $u\in K$ as $m\to\infty$; here $K$ is chosen independently of $m$.
The subsequent zero formula is in the fixed-index sense of Definition~\ref{def:endpoint-zero-ordering}, equivalently uniformly for each fixed finite set $1\le k\le K_0$ after increasing the threshold in $m$.
The constant in $O_K(m^{-2})$ depends only on the compact set $K$ and on the fixed endpoint branch disc and holomorphic remainder bounds from Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package}; the $O_k(m^{-4})$ constant may depend on the Rouch\'e data for the fixed centre $u_k^{(0)}=\pi(2k-1)/\sqrt2$.
Thus the theorem is local in the endpoint scale and is not a macroscopic zero-density theorem.
The local-to-global assertion is the following fixed-window statement: if $U>0$ is non-threshold, meaning
\[
U\ne \frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}
\qquad (j\ge1),
\]
and
\[
N(U)=\#\left\{j\ge1:\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}<U\right\}
=\left\lfloor \frac{U}{\pi\sqrt2}+\frac12\right\rfloor,
\]
then there exists $m_U$ such that, for every $m\ge m_U$, the whole punctured endpoint window
\[
-\frac{U^2}{m^2}\le y<0
\]
contains exactly $N(U)$ negative real zeros of $Z_m$, all simple, ordered as
\[
0>y_{m,1}^{(U)}>\cdots>y_{m,N(U)}^{(U)}
\qquad\text{when }N(U)>0,
\]
and contains no other negative real zero.
For every fixed $1\le k\le N(U)$ these window zeros agree with the fixed-index endpoint zeros of Definition~\ref{def:endpoint-zero-ordering} and satisfy the expansion below, after increasing the same threshold for the finite set of indices in the window if necessary.
The dependency chain is
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{generic EP2 square-root lemma }\bigl(\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-ep2-branching}}\bigr)\\
\Downarrow\\[-2pt]
\text{model coefficients and amplitudes }(a,b,A_*,B_*)\text{ from Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package}}\\
\Downarrow\\[-2pt]
\text{two-branch oscillation normal form }\bigl(\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}}\bigr)\\
\Downarrow\\[-2pt]
\text{Rouch\'e discs around }u_k^{(0)}=\pi(2k-1)/\sqrt2.
\end{array}
\]
Consequently, uniformly for \(u\) in compact subsets of \(\CC\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-scaling}
Z_m\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)
=
\exp\!\left(-\frac{3u^2}{8m}\right)
\left[
\cos\!\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{3u}{4\sqrt{2}\,m}\right)
+ O_K(m^{-2})
\right],
\end{equation}
and, in the fixed-index sense of Definition~\ref{def:endpoint-zero-ordering}, for each fixed $k\ge 1$ the $k$th negative real zero nearest $y=0$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-zero-quantization}
y_{m,k}
=
-\frac{\pi^2(2k-1)^2}{2m^2}
-\frac{3\pi^2(2k-1)^2}{4m^3}
+ O_k(m^{-4}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-cosine-hypotheses} supplies the explicit hypotheses for Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}, and it points to the model-specific algebra in Appendix~\ref{app:endpoint-branching}.
In particular, Proposition~\ref{prop:endpoint-expansion-package} works on the uniform disc $D_r$ introduced there and proves that on the local scale \(y=s^2\) the maximal-modulus pair satisfies the square-root branching predicted by Lemma~\ref{lem:generic-ep2-branching}:
\[
\lambda_\pm(s)=1\pm \frac{1}{\sqrt2}s+\frac58 s^2+O(s^3),
\qquad
C_\pm(s)=\frac12\mp \frac{3\sqrt2}{16}s+O(s^2),
\]
and that the remaining two branches contribute only \(O_K(m^{-2})\) on compact complex sets \(K\) under the scaling \(y=-u^2/m^2\).
Thus the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} hold with the stated constants and \(\lambda_c=1\).
Applying \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-scaling} gives \eqref{eq:endpoint-scaling}, and applying \eqref{eq:universal-ep2-y} on discs around the points \(u_k^{(0)}=\pi(2k-1)/\sqrt2\) gives \eqref{eq:endpoint-zero-quantization}.
The dependence of the constants is the one in Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}: the compact-scaling constant is the constant for the chosen compact $K$, while the zero constant for a fixed $k$ is obtained on one conjugation-invariant Rouch\'e disc around $u_k^{(0)}$.
For a fixed finite set $1\le k\le K_0$, choose these discs pairwise disjoint and take the maximum of their error constants and thresholds; this gives uniformity over that finite set and nothing beyond it.
The same lemma applies with conjugation-invariant real-centered discs, because \(Z_m\in\ZZ[y]\) by Section~\ref{sec:model}; hence the extracted \(u\)-zeros are real, positive, and ordered for each fixed finite set of indices once \(m\) is large.
Their images \(y_{m,k}=-u_{m,k}^2/m^2\) are therefore the ordered negative real zeros nearest the endpoint within the fixed finite endpoint window under consideration.
It remains only to identify this fixed finite list with all zeros in an arbitrary non-threshold endpoint window.
For such a fixed $U$, Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} applies the same oscillation lemma on the compact rectangle $Q_U$ and gives a threshold $m_U$ for which the only zeros of $u\mapsto Z_m(-u^2/m^2)$ in that rectangle are the Rouch\'e zeros near the centres $u_j^{(0)}<U$.
The positive real change of variables $u=m\sqrt{-y}$ is a bijection from the real interval $[-U^2/m^2,0)$ to $0<u\le U$, and the map $u\mapsto -u^2/m^2$ has nonzero derivative at every listed centre and at every nearby zero for all sufficiently large $m$.
Thus simplicity, ordering, and exclusion of all other real zeros in the whole fixed window are exactly the conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} transferred back to the $y$-line.
Since $N(U)$ is finite, the fixed-index expansion already proved above applies simultaneously to every index $1\le k\le N(U)$ after taking the maximum of the finitely many thresholds and constants.
The non-threshold assumption is used only to keep the right endpoint of the compact window away from a limiting cosine zero, so the proof makes no assertion for growing windows or growing indices.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fixed-window endpoint theorem interface]\label{prop:fixed-window-endpoint-interface}
The endpoint-zero input used in the named theorem package is exactly the following local finite-window assertion.
Fix a non-threshold number \(U>0\), meaning that
\[
U\neq \frac{(2j-1)\pi}{\sqrt2}
\qquad(j\ge1).
\]
Then there is an integer \(m_U\), depending on this fixed \(U\) and on the endpoint branch data, such that, for all \(m\ge m_U\), the interval
\[
-\frac{U^2}{m^2}\le y<0
\]
contains precisely the finite ordered set of simple negative zeros isolated in Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation}, and those zeros are the zeros quantified by Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization}.
In particular, after a finite index set \(1\le k\le K_0\) is fixed, one may choose a single threshold and a single family of error constants for those indices, and each such zero has the expansion \eqref{eq:endpoint-zero-quantization}.
The constants and thresholds are attached to fixed endpoint windows and fixed finite index sets; no assertion is made here for windows \(U=U_m\) or indices \(k=k(m)\) growing with \(m\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization} already contains the local fixed-index expansion and the fixed-window conclusion.
For a prescribed non-threshold \(U\), its proof uses Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-neighborhood-isolation} on the fixed compact rectangle
\[
Q_U=\{u\in\CC:0\le \Re u\le U,\ \abs{\Im u}\le U\}.
\]
That isolation theorem, in turn, is obtained from Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law} and gives a threshold depending only on this compact rectangle and on the endpoint branch data.
The positive real change of variables \(u=m\sqrt{-y}\), equivalently \(y=-u^2/m^2\), identifies the real interval \([-U^2/m^2,0)\) with \(0<u\le U\), and its derivative in the positive \(u\)-range is nonzero.
Thus the isolated positive real Rouch\'e zeros in the \(u\)-window give exactly the simple negative real zeros in the stated \(y\)-window, with the order reversed by \(u\mapsto -u^2/m^2\).
For a fixed finite index set, taking the maximum of the finitely many thresholds and error constants in Theorem~\ref{thm:endpoint-zero-quantization} gives the asserted common constants.
This proves the interface statement as a consequence of the endpoint theorem rather than as an input to it.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fixed compact edge-window counting law]\label{cor:edge-zero-counting}
Define
\[
N_m(\xi)
:=
\#\left\{
y\in\RR:
Z_m(y)=0,
\quad
-\frac{\xi}{m^2}\le y<0
\right\},
\qquad
\text{for a fixed }\xi>0.
\]
If $\xi$ does not belong to the threshold set
\[
\left\{
\frac{\pi^2(2k-1)^2}{2}:k\ge1
\right\},
\]
then for all sufficiently large $m$ one has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge-counting-law}
N_m(\xi)
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{\sqrt{\xi}}{\pi\sqrt2}+\frac12
\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
This is a fixed-window endpoint count; it does not assert a macroscopic density theorem for all negative real zeros of \(Z_m\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Put \(U=\sqrt{\xi}\).
The non-threshold assumption on \(\xi\) is equivalent to
\[
U\neq \frac{q_k}{a}
\qquad(k\ge1)
\]
for the endpoint constants \(a=1/\sqrt2\) and \(q_k=(2k-1)\pi/2\), because
\[
\left(\frac{q_k}{a}\right)^2=\frac{\pi^2(2k-1)^2}{2}.
\]
By the compact-window exclusion in Lemma~\ref{lem:two-branch-oscillation-law}, all zeros of
\[
u\longmapsto Z_m\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{m^2}\right)
\]
with \(0\le \Re u\le U\) and \(\abs{\Im u}\le U\) are, for all sufficiently large \(m\), exactly the real positive zeros \(u_{m,k}\) with \(q_k/a<U\).
The real change of variables \(y=-u^2/m^2\) maps these and only these positive \(u\)-zeros to the interval \([-\xi/m^2,0)\).
Thus no additional real zeros of \(Z_m\) occur in the finite edge window, and the set counted by \(N_m(\xi)\) is exactly
\[
\left\{
k\ge1:
\frac{\pi^2(2k-1)^2}{2}<\xi
\right\},
\]
which yields \eqref{eq:edge-counting-law}.
\end{proof}

In continuum form, \eqref{eq:endpoint-zero-quantization} gives
\[
k \sim \frac{m}{\pi\sqrt2}\sqrt{|y|},
\qquad
\left|\frac{\dd k}{\dd y}\right|
\sim
\frac{m}{2\pi\sqrt{2|y|}}
\qquad
(y\uparrow 0,\ |y|=O(m^{-2})).
\]
Thus, in the fixed compact endpoint-window regime, the negative zeros nearest \(y=0\) compress with the same square-root exponent that governs the Puiseux opening of the maximal-modulus branch.
This local statement should not be read as a density theorem for all negative real zeros or for a growing macroscopic window.
