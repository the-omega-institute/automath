This is a follow-up to a question you answered earlier. You do not have that conversation, so
everything needed is restated here. Your earlier answer was good and I verified all of it; I
am coming back because one statement in it is false as written, and the corrected version
looks provable.

THE SETTING. Fix m at least 6. For an integer n with 0 <= n < 2^m, let Fold_m(n) be the
digits of the greedy Zeckendorf expansion of n in positions F_2 through F_{m+1}, with
everything above discarded. Regard the integers below 2^m as the vertices of the hypercube,
adjacent when their binary representations differ in one bit. Define the coloured-star
signature

    Phi_m(a) = ( Fold_m(a), the multiset of Fold_m(a XOR 2^i) for 0 <= i < m ).

The open lemma is that every fibre of Phi_m has at most two elements. I have verified it for m
from 6 to 19 directly. You previously supplied a reduction: writing T_m(r) = F_{m+1} r + F_m
floor((r+1)/phi) for the numbers whose expansions vanish in the retained positions, the fold
is the sawtooth remainder, an edge changes the fold by an element of an explicit at-most-eight
element set C_m(p), and pairwise disjointness of those sets implies injectivity. You certified
that disjointness for m from 13 to 1000 and proved it holds eventually via the p-adic Subspace
Theorem. I re-derived every checkable part of that against my own implementation and it all
reproduces, including your observation that the only ambiguity at m equal to 12 is between 16
and minus 128, coming from 144 = 128 + 16. I am not disputing any of it.

THE PART THAT IS FALSE. Your section 7 proposed finishing the lemma as follows. Given a
collision, match the equally coloured neighbours by a permutation, set D = b - a, let p_i and
q_j be the signed powers flipping bit i of a and bit j of b, and put u_i = q_{pi(i)} - p_i.
You gave two exact identities, that the u_i sum to -2D and that their nearest-integer-to-phi
images sum to -2 times that of D. You then wrote that in every collision you examined exactly
two of the u_i equal -D and the rest vanish, and proposed proving that the system forces that
shape.

I checked both identities on every collision at m = 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10, over every valid
matching rather than one natural choice: 243 out of 243 cases satisfy both, under either
reading of the rounding. The identities are fine.

The shape claim is not. At m = 8 take a = 66 and b = 210, so D = 144. The matching sending
(0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7) to (0,5,2,3,7,1,6,4) is valid and gives

    u = (0, 34, 0, 0, -144, -34, 0, -144),

which has four nonzero entries, not two, while both of your identities still hold. Sixteen of
the eighty valid matchings at m = 8 have this shape. The deviation is a cancelling pair plus
and minus 34, that is, the OTHER Fibonacci number with two binary ones. So the constraint
system does not force the two-coordinate form, and a proof of the universal statement cannot
exist.

WHAT SURVIVES, AND MY QUESTION. The existential version does hold. Every one of the 227
collision pairs at m from 6 to 10 admits at least one matching of the two-coordinate form.
That is the statement to aim at:

    For every collision there EXISTS a matching pi for which exactly two of the u_i equal -D
    and all others vanish.

That would give D equal to a Fibonacci number with exactly two binary ones added without
carry, and combined with the fact that only 34 and 144 qualify in the relevant range, it would
close the lemma for all m.

One. Can you prove the existential statement? A canonical construction of the good matching
would be ideal - something like matching neighbours by the position of the carry rather than
by colour alone.

Two. If not, is there a reformulation that avoids matchings entirely? The matching is an
artefact of comparing multisets; the underlying content is that the two coloured stars agree.
A formulation in terms of the sawtooth breakpoints T_m directly might be cleaner and is what I
would try first.

Three. Separately, and lower priority: is the ineffectivity of the Subspace cutoff removable
here? A Baker-type effective bound, even a terrible one, would let a finite computation finish
the whole lemma. My reading is that this is out of reach for the relevant form, but I would
like your view.

Please tell me plainly if the existential statement also fails, and be explicit about which
parts you have proved versus observed.
