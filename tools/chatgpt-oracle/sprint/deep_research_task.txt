WHAT I WANT. Read this paper deeply and find a result in it that is genuinely worth publishing and

The bar, stated plainly:

 - It must be a END result, not an intermediate step. "Lemma that would help someone prove X" is not
   what I am asking for. Prove X.
 - It must not restate what the paper already contains in different words, and it must not be a
   marginal strengthening - an epsilon improved, a hypothesis cosmetically weakened, a special case
   of something already proved. That is squeezing the tube, and I would rather have nothing.
 - It must not reprove something already in the literature. You may use other people's results
   freely and cite them; you may not rediscover their arguments and present them as ours. Before you
   commit to a direction, check whether it is already known - search the literature properly, and if
   the result exists, say so and change direction rather than writing it up.
 - It must be proved to referee standard. A numerical experiment is a sanity check, never a
   substitute for a proof. If you cannot close the argument, report exactly where it breaks rather
   than papering over the gap with "one can show".

WHERE TO LOOK. The paper's own machinery is the raw material. The productive questions are usually:
what does this technique prove that the authors did not notice it proves; what happens at the
boundary of the hypotheses; what is the natural generalisation that the method already supports; is
there a converse; is the constant sharp, and if not, what is; does the structure the paper builds
have a classification, a rigidity statement, or an obstruction the paper never asked for.

Read the paper's own discussion and open-problems section first. Then read what it cites. The best
candidate is usually something the paper gestures at and then walks away from.

HOW TO DECIDE WHAT TO PROVE. Generate several candidate statements before committing to one. For
each, give an honest estimate of whether it is (a) true, (b) provable with what is in the paper plus
standard technique, and (c) new. Then pick the one with the best product of depth and reachability,
and say why you rejected the others. I want to see the discarded candidates - a rejected direction
with a stated reason is useful to me.

SIGNIFICANCE, NOT ONLY NOVELTY. Name the paper's target journal - it is in the directory name or
the front matter - and judge your candidate against that venue before you commit to it. Three
independent referee reports on this sprint's papers found no mathematical error and rejected two of
three anyway, both on significance. One wrote: "the main result is too slight and too specifically
engineered ... its purported all-resolution content reduces to a two-letter interval-overlap test
... this is not a correctness problem, it is that it is extremely elementary." The other: "this is
a rejection for incrementality, not for a fatal mathematical error."

So "an end result, not an intermediate step" is necessary and not sufficient. In your candidate
table, add a column for whether a referee at that specific journal would call the candidate large
enough, and answer it honestly. If your best candidate is correct, new, and still too small for the
venue, say that in your report rather than writing it up - I would rather learn it from you than
from a referee.

WRITING. If you prove it, write it into the paper properly: statement, proof, and the one or two
sentences of context that tell a reader why it belongs. Academic register throughout; no colloquial
phrasing, no hedging filler, no "interestingly" or "it is worth noting". Match the surrounding
prose. Put it where it belongs in the argument, not in an appendix as an afterthought, unless the
paper's structure genuinely calls for that.

If a numerical check is appropriate, add it under artifacts/ as a script that would actually fail if
the claim were wrong, and say what you did to establish that it can fail.

RULES:

   the exact defect a recent audit spent several rounds removing: it makes the build succeed for
   whoever has that file and fail for a journal's production desk. Name the file, as above.
 - Any citation you add must be verified in Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or the official

   sides for a range of small parameters and confirm they agree. A recent run asserted that a
   difference of least residues equalled the difference of the unreduced products; it was false for
   half the cases and the theorem needed a second round to repair. The step looked obviously right,
   which is exactly why nothing caught it. Report the numbers you checked.
 - If you add or touch a numerical verifier, the tolerance is part of the claim and must be
   justified. A run's `--inject-error` switch, or any other author-supplied perturbation, proves
   nothing: it shows the assertions fire, not that the check could catch a wrong theorem. The last
   paper to ship one had an absolute tolerance larger than the quantity being measured, so two
   mutated statements of its own theorem passed. Establish the check by mutating the formula the
   theorem asserts - change an exponent, drop a factor - confirming the script fails, and restoring
   it byte for byte. Report the true margin: the largest observed residual against the tolerance.
