1. What a recent accepted Cahiers paper normally looks like
How the paper opens and where the main theorem appears
Recent papers almost invariably begin with a French Résumé followed by an English Abstract, then keywords and Mathematics Subject Classification. This is not an occasional preference: it is the visible journal form across short and long recent papers. Cahiers de Topologie+4Cahiers de Topologie+4Cahiers de Topologie+4
The introduction normally starts directly with one of three things:


the established theorem or construction being extended;


the precise object under study;


the problem that the paper resolves.


It does not ordinarily begin with a broad advocacy paragraph, an editorial account of what the paper is not, or a detailed originality ledger. Kahn begins with the Bénabou–Roubaud theorem and the inadequacy of existing proofs; Arakawa begins from the nerve construction; the short Joyal–Tierney paper combines introduction and preliminaries and states its exact alternative-proof objective immediately. A reasonable empirical description is:


in long papers, the introduction is often about 10–20% of the article;


in short papers, it may occupy 20–30%;


the main result is generally identifiable within the first few pages, either as a numbered theorem or as an exact statement that is later numbered.

Explanatory prose versus statements and proofs
Accepted Cahiers papers can be discursive. This is not a journal that demands an austere theorem-proof-theorem-proof format. It tolerates, and often rewards, careful categorical setup and explanations of constructions.
But the explanatory prose is normally local to the mathematics:


a paragraph before a theorem explains why that theorem is needed;


one or two sentences at the start of a proof announce the strategy;


a remark after the proof isolates a consequence, limitation, or comparison;


a section opening explains the transition to the next object.


The prose does not usually maintain a parallel editorial commentary throughout the paper: “this part is standard,” “this is not a classification,” “this does not claim X,” “the exact boundary is Y,” and so forth. Those things may be said once, usually in the introduction or a concluding remark. They are not ordinarily repeated after every construction.
The accepted register is therefore explanatory but mathematically directional. The prose is supposed to move the argument forward.
Proof style
The readership expects the paper to carry the proof of its actual contribution. A paper whose main theorem is dispatched by a one-line citation would generally need the cited machinery to make the new conclusion essentially immediate.
At the same time, routine categorical or homological facts may be cited or compressed. Riehl and Verity explicitly record standard properties and refer the reader elsewhere for their proofs. Kahn carries the adjoint calculations that matter to his theorem but treats a repeated adjunction calculation in one sentence and calls another deduction obvious from the preceding lemma. The practical rule is:

Carry the argument that distinguishes the paper; cite or tersely verify the infrastructure that specialists will regard as standard.

Routine verifications are often handled by:


“the other cases are analogous”;


a displayed calculation with one explanatory sentence;


a citation to a standard source;


a lemma whose proof records only the nonformal step.


The journal does not require every functoriality, normalization, or elementary subgroup calculation to be expanded merely to make the paper self-contained at textbook level.
Main text, appendices, and supplementary material
The customary form is a single self-contained article. In the recent sample, a separate supplement is uncommon. Most papers have no substantial author appendix at all. When auxiliary material is separated, its natural contents are extended computations, variants, long technical verifications, or background not needed to follow the principal argument.
As an approximate house norm:


main article: 85–100%;


appendix or externally cited technical continuation: 0–15%;


most commonly: effectively 100:0.


The journal explicitly permits a self-contained article to omit long technical proofs or appendices when a complete version is available on arXiv. Register and signposting
The standard register is first-person plural for joint papers and first-person singular for single-author papers. It is active rather than ceremonially passive:


“We prove…”


“We now construct…”


“The following lemma identifies…”


“Applying Proposition X, we obtain…”


Results are referred back to when they are actually used. Motivation may be recalled at a major structural turn, but it is not restated after each theorem in slightly altered language.
There is also genuine variation of pace. A routine definition may receive two sentences; the central construction may occupy ten pages. That asymmetry tells the reader what matters.
Tells of non-fit
The most common signs that a manuscript does not read like a Cahiers article are:


A result hierarchy that remains unclear after the introduction. The reader sees many numbered statements but cannot tell which one is the paper.


A survey or application that pulls the article into another literature without feeding back into the categorical theorem.


Standard material proved at greater length than the new material is motivated.


A response-to-referees register: repeated priority disclaimers, scope disclaimers, dependency ledgers, and assurances that no larger theorem is being claimed.


Overformatted architecture: tables and enumerations that classify every sentence by role rather than letting the mathematics establish emphasis.


A conclusion that restates not only the results but also the paper’s entire originality defence.


Failure to use the journal’s visible front-matter conventions, particularly the French Résumé.

3. Does it read as machine-assisted prose?
Yes—in its expository shell, plainly so. I do not mean that the mathematics reads generated, and I cannot infer authorship from prose. I mean that the manuscript displays the textual habits named in the question strongly enough that a reader may notice them.
The central proofs, especially Theorems 3.7 and 4.2, have mathematical texture. They slow down where the construction is genuinely delicate, introduce specific objects, and perform nontrivial checks. Those passages do not have the same problem.
The machine-assisted or audit-generated feel is concentrated in four places.
1. Exhaustive classification of the paper’s own sentences
The introduction classifies inputs, standard steps, paper-specific steps, theorem roles, two uses of cohomology, scope, companion overlap, and organization.  A human research introduction normally chooses the two or three distinctions needed to understand the result. This one tries to pre-answer every possible objection.
2. Recurrent symmetrical constructions
The manuscript repeatedly uses forms such as:


“There are two relevant changes…”


“The two terminal-fibre endpoints…”


“The mismatch has two independent parts…”


“The roles of the hypotheses are now separate…”


“The order of the argument is essential…”


“The following parts are classical inputs…”


“The two proofs requiring construction…”


None is bad alone. In aggregate they give the prose a templated symmetry. Almost every conceptual distinction is divided, balanced, and closed.
3. Throat-clearing and post-statement disclaimers
The same negative formulations recur:


not a new gerbe classification;


not a localization theorem;


not presentation-independent;


not information-theoretic;


not an invariant of a bare empirical model;


not a crossed-module comparison.


For example, the paper closes Theorem 3.7 with another statement of what it is not, repeats the fixed-presentation warning around Section 4, and spends much of the end of Section 5 denying stronger readings.   
A human author would normally make the strongest boundary disclaimer once, then trust the theorem statement.
4. Flatness of emphasis
The paper gives nearly the same rhetorical weight to:


Theorem 4.2’s object-level prestack construction;


quotient initiality for A/K;


the exact connected-overlap caveat;


the empirical-model non-transfer;


why no crossed-module theorem is asserted.


Those things are not equally important. The manuscript’s architecture makes them look equally important because each receives a named subsection, a balanced explanation, and a closing limitation.
The repair is not to make the prose less clear. It is to permit deliberate unevenness:


give Theorem 4.2 ten pages;


give quotient initiality three lines;


give the connectedness warning one full paragraph;


give the empirical-model boundary two pages;


give the absent crossed-module theorem no paragraph at all.


The present article reads less like an accepted paper than like a mathematically careful paper into which several rounds of audits, positioning memoranda, and anticipated referee responses have been incorporated verbatim.
That is fixable. The underlying mathematical prose is substantially better than the editorial scaffolding around it.
PART TWO — YOU ARE THE REFEREE
Recommendation: Major revisions.
Dear Editor,
The manuscript studies component gerbes selected by global component sections of the stackification of an abelian-banded prestack on a finite site. Its two principal contributions are, first, a rigidity theorem showing that a strictly pullback-stable choice of representatives is incompatible with terminal gluing failure under the stated H1-vanishing hypothesis, and, second, an explicit finite-site construction realizing prescribed componentwise evaluation maps while controlling the component presheaf, terminal fibre, neutrality pattern, and band. A finite-group realization result for selected covers of wedges of 2-spheres is then deduced.
The subject is suitable for the Cahiers, and the central constructions are presented with enough detail to be checked within the article. In particular, the proof of the representative-rigidity theorem and the object-level construction in Theorem 4.2 belong in the paper and are, in my view, its strongest parts.
I do not, however, recommend acceptance in the present form. The issue is primarily one of architecture and exposition rather than correctness.
The introduction does not establish a sufficiently sharp hierarchy among the results. It contains useful summaries, but these are surrounded by an extensive audit of classical inputs, paper-specific steps, theorem-count decisions, overlap with a companion manuscript, and claims deliberately not made. The companion-manuscript table in particular reads as correspondence with a referee rather than as part of a finished article. The principal theorems should instead be stated explicitly in the introduction, with the classical Giraud and UCT inputs described in one concise paragraph.
The same difficulty recurs throughout the manuscript. Important qualifications are repeated after the statements, within their proofs, and again in the conclusion. The paper repeatedly explains that a result is not a classification, not presentation-independent, not an information-theoretic theorem, or not an invariant of a bare empirical model. These qualifications are mathematically responsible, but their repetition obscures rather than clarifies the actual contribution.
There is also an imbalance in the treatment of standard and new material. The explicit prestack construction deserves its present detailed proof. By contrast, parts of the UCT, quotient, duality, and finite-abelian-group discussion are developed at disproportionate length for the expected readership. The elementary subgroup and character calculations should be compressed or cited, leaving more visible space around the realization theorem and its geometric application.
My principal reservation concerns Section 7. The negative comparison with empirical-model obstructions is precise, but at its current length it changes the perceived subject of the article. The extended survey of contextuality obstructions and of results that the construction does not provide makes the preceding categorical machinery appear to have been designed for an application that is then withdrawn. I recommend retaining a concise boundary theorem and the degree/indexing distinction, but reducing the section substantially. Proposition 7.2 and most of the subsequent survey can be omitted without weakening the main paper.
The manuscript should also be brought into the journal’s visible front-matter form, including a French Résumé. The abstract should be shortened. The conclusion should be reduced to a brief account of the two principal results and their exact presentation-relative scope.
The article is already 39 pages, with a further six-page supplement mentioned by the authors. I have not been supplied with that supplement. Any proof or naturality statement required for the claims in the article should be incorporated in a short appendix. Extended examples and lower-language boundary material may instead remain in a complete arXiv version. The final journal article should preferably remain close to the journal’s usual forty-page scale.
The single objection most likely to prevent acceptance is therefore that the manuscript reads as a defensive dossier documenting scope and priority, rather than as a focused article organized around its two genuinely paper-specific constructions. This is fixable. I do not believe that a new theorem is required. It does, however, require revision across the abstract, introduction, hierarchy of results, Sections 5–7, conclusion, and supplementary-material arrangement; it is consequently not a minor revision.
Subject to such a reorganization, I would be prepared to recommend publication.
