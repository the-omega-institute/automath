The target journal is Monatshefte fuer Mathematik. The manuscript attached is a 37-page main article with a 43-page supplement, on prime languages and finite-state obstructions, including a slender-context-free Cobham theorem and its effective refinement.

This is not a valuation question and not a correctness audit. You have already done both for this
manuscript and we have acted on the results. This question is about whether the paper READS like a
paper this journal accepts, and whether you would accept it.

Answer in three parts, in this order. Do not merge them.

PART ONE - THE HOUSE STYLE, LEARNED FROM ACTUAL RECENT PAPERS.

Call to mind papers accepted by this journal in roughly the last three to five years - not the
journal's stated aims and scope, which we can read ourselves, but the papers as written. Then
characterize the house style concretely enough that it could be applied. We want specifics, not
adjectives:

 - How does a typical accepted paper open? Where does the main theorem appear - first page, after a
   motivating example, after a history section? How long is the introduction relative to the whole?
 - How much of the paper is prose that explains why a step is being taken, versus formal statement
   and proof? Where does that prose sit - before a proof, inside it, in a remark afterwards?
 - How are proofs written in this journal? Is a short proof that discharges its work to cited
   results acceptable, or does the readership expect the argument to be carried in the paper? How
   are routine verifications handled?
 - What is the normal ratio of main text to appendices or supplementary material in this journal,
   and what KIND of material is normally relegated? Give an approximate proportion. This matters to
   us and we want your honest read of what is customary here rather than what is permitted.
 - What register does the prose use - first person plural, impersonal passive, something else? How
   are results signposted and referred back to? How much motivation is repeated?
 - What are the tells of a paper that does NOT fit this journal's register, in your experience of
   reading them?

Then apply that characterization to the attached manuscript, section by section, and say where it
departs. Be specific about location and about what to do instead. In particular say whether the
main-text-to-supplement proportion is right for this journal, and if not, what should move which
way.

We are also asking you to look for something harder to name. Text produced with machine assistance
has recognizable habits: uniform paragraph lengths, exhaustive enumeration where a human author
would choose, throat-clearing before every statement, hedges that carry no information, symmetrical
sentence structures, transitions that announce structure rather than advance an argument, and a
flatness of emphasis in which nothing is allowed to be more important than anything else. A human
mathematician writing in a journal is uneven on purpose: some things get three sentences and some
get thirty pages. If the attached manuscript reads that way to you, say so plainly and point at
the specific passages. We would rather hear it bluntly than be flattered.

PART TWO - YOU ARE THE REFEREE.

You have received this manuscript from the editor of this journal. Write your recommendation.
Would you accept it? Accept with minor revisions, major revisions, reject and resubmit, or reject?
Give the verdict first and the reasoning after. Write it as a referee report, addressed to the
editor, in the form and length this journal's referees actually use.

Do not be generous. If the honest recommendation is major revisions or reject, say so and say
exactly what drives it. A referee report we can act on is worth more to us than a favourable one.
Identify the single objection most likely to sink the paper, and say whether it is fixable.

PART THREE - THE EDITOR'S BAR.

Now change roles. You are the editor deciding on the basis of your own reading and the referee
report you just wrote. State the specific, enumerated set of changes that would move this
manuscript to a state you would accept. Not improvements in general - the actual list, ordered by
how much each one matters, with an indication of which are required and which are discretionary.
For each item, say concretely what should be done, in which section.

If some item requires mathematics we do not currently have, say so and separate it from the items
that are purely a matter of writing and organization. We need to know which part of the gap is
work and which part is craft.

If your honest conclusion at any point is that the manuscript is already at the standard you would
accept and the remaining variance is referee taste, say that plainly and stop. We will act on it.
