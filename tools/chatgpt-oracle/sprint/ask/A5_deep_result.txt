The target journal is Ergodic Theory and Dynamical Systems. The attached manuscript concerns finite
parts of dynamical zeta functions for shifts of finite type.

This is not an assessment request. We are not asking whether the paper is good, which journal fits,
or how to package it. We are asking you to do mathematics.

THE TASK. Working from the machinery in the attached manuscript, find and state a deep result.
Then stop only when you have something of genuine publication value.

CONSTRAINTS, and we mean each of them:

 - It must be a TERMINAL result, not a step toward one. Do not report intermediate lemmas, partial
   reductions, or "this suggests that". State a theorem, or state that you could not reach one.
 - It must not restate or rederive what is already in this manuscript. We know what the manuscript
   proves. A reformulation of a result already in it is worthless to us.
 - It must not reproduce reasoning already published by others. You may freely USE published
   theorems as inputs - cite them precisely, with a statement of the exact form and hypotheses you
   are invoking - but the derivation you give must not be one that already exists in the literature.
   If you find that your candidate result is already known, say so, name where, and go find another.
   That negative finding is itself useful; a rediscovery presented as new is not.
 - Do not ration the output. If you can reach three results, give three. We are explicitly asking
   you not to hold back a partial answer and wait to be asked again.
 - Write it in the register of a research paper. Definitions, hypotheses, statement, proof or a
   proof sketch complete enough that a specialist could reconstruct it. No conversational framing.

WHAT WOULD COUNT AS DEEP, so that we are asking the same question. Any of:

 - a theorem about a standard object of the field - dynamical zeta functions, transfer operators,
   Ruelle resonances, equilibrium states, subshifts of finite type, Markov measures, entropy or
   pressure - that the manuscript's machinery reaches but that the manuscript does not state;
 - an exact identification of something the manuscript treats only asymptotically or numerically,
   or an asymptotic where the manuscript has only an inequality;
 - a structural obstruction: a proof that some natural strengthening of the manuscript's results is
   impossible, with the obstruction identified;
 - a connection to a neighbouring theory that is not merely an analogy - one that transports a
   theorem across, in a direction that is not already standard;
 - a sharp constant, a sharp exponent, or a converse to a one-directional result.

WHAT WOULD NOT COUNT: a survey of directions; a list of open questions; a generalization obtained
by weakening a hypothesis with no new idea; a special case of a known theorem; an observation that
two things in the manuscript are formally similar.

BEFORE YOU FINISH, do these three things and report them:

 1. State your own confidence that the proof is correct, as a number, and name the step you are
    least sure of.
 2. State your confidence that it is genuinely new, as a separate number, and say what you checked
    it against. Distinguish "I found no antecedent" from "there is no antecedent".
 3. Say plainly whether the result is strong enough to carry a submission of its own, or whether it
    belongs as a section inside the present manuscript, or whether it is a remark. We would rather
    have an honest small theorem than an oversold one.

If after genuine effort the machinery yields nothing that meets this bar, say so directly and
explain what blocks it. A clear negative is an acceptable answer to this question. What is not
acceptable is a plausible-sounding statement you have not actually convinced yourself of.
