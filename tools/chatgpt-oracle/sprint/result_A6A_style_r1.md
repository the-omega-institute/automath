What the recent TAMS house style actually looks like
1. How the papers open, and where the main theorem appears
There is no single mandatory opening, but the dominant pattern is clear: the paper begins with the mathematical object or problem, establishes only the background needed to understand the result, and reaches a serious theorem quickly.
At one extreme, Orponen–Puliatti–Pyörälä begin their introduction with “The purpose of this paper is to prove the following theorem” and state it immediately. Huh–Matherne–Mészáros–St. Dizier give roughly a page of definitions and then state their first main theorem on page 2. Dahmani–Mj reach their uniform finiteness theorem on page 3 after defining the question. Uraltsev reaches the principal embedding theorems on page 3 after introducing the operators. Papers with a more literature-dependent problem may take longer: Abreu–Liu–Macarini state their main theorems on page 3 after two pages of contact-topological history, and Harvie–Wang have an unusually long multi-result introduction but begin the “Main Results” subsection on page 2. arXiv+5arXiv+5arXiv+5
An introduction occupying about one fifth to one quarter of the paper is entirely normal. In the comparison set, introductions run to approximately 5 pages in a 22-page harmonic-analysis paper, 4 pages in a 19-page symplectic-topology paper, 4 pages in a 17-page number-theory paper, 7 pages in a 29-page dynamics paper, and 10 pages in a 37-page geometric-analysis paper. The relevant distinction is therefore not “short versus long introduction.” It is whether the introduction spends its length doing mathematical work—defining the problem, distinguishing the result from known theorems, and explaining the proof mechanism—or repeatedly managing the paper’s claims. arXiv+4arXiv+4arXiv+4
A separate history section after the main-result section is less characteristic. History is normally interleaved with the formulation of the problem: one paragraph of background, then a theorem; another paragraph placing a consequence, then the consequence. A labelled “contribution boundary” section sounds more like submission-facing documentation than the natural progression of a TAMS article.
2. Prose versus formal statement and proof
After the introduction, the typical paper is mathematically dense. Roughly 70–85% of the remaining page area is definitions, statements, calculations, and proofs. Explanatory prose is concentrated in three places:


immediately before a principal theorem, where it says why the formulation is the right one;


at the opening of a proof, where one or two sentences identify the mechanism;


after a result, where a remark records one genuinely nonobvious consequence or limitation.


The papers do not normally provide all three around every lemma. A routine proposition often appears with no preamble and has a proof that starts immediately. A long proof may be divided by descriptive paragraphs, but those paragraphs guide an actual change of argument; they do not merely say that the next step will now be carried out.
Motivation is not normally repeated. Once the introduction has explained why a theorem matters, the body is allowed to become terse. Later references are usually functional: “By Proposition 3.4…,” “It remains to prove…,” or “Theorem 1.2 follows from….” There is less repetition of the theorem’s meaning than in the present manuscript.
3. How proofs are written
A short proof that places most of the work in a cited theorem is completely acceptable in TAMS, provided the paper does the local work that matters:


state the cited result in the exact form needed;


identify the notation;


verify every hypothesis that is not automatic;


track any nonstandard normalization or constant.


Orponen–Puliatti–Pyörälä, for example, dispose of one range of their main theorem in a short proof by invoking a cited theorem and one proposition from the paper, with the parameter passage written out. There is no expectation that a standard theorem be reproved merely to make the article self-contained. arXiv
Routine verifications are commonly put in one sentence, omitted as immediate, or moved to an appendix when they are long but structurally uninteresting. Draganić–Krivelevich–Nenadov explicitly move an almost identical earlier argument to a short appendix “for completeness.” arXiv
What TAMS readers do expect the paper itself to carry is the argument constituting the paper’s distinctive contribution. In this manuscript, the endpoint correction, the two-context domination, and the Weinstein indexing and orbit count belong in the article. A standard stable domain-of-attraction proof does not need comparable page weight.
4. Appendices and supplementary material
In my twelve-paper sample, ten had no appendix. One had a roughly two-page appendix containing a proof close to an existing embedding argument; another devoted approximately four pages to a parallel algebraic-number-theory case that was expressly described as background and analogy rather than part of the main proof. arXiv+5arXiv+5arXiv+5
The customary pattern is therefore:


no appendix at all, or


a short appendix occupying roughly 5–15% of the paper;


occasionally a larger appendix, around 20%, when it develops a coherent parallel case.


I found no example in this set in which an external supplementary file carried an essential mathematical step. Computational files may accompany a paper, but the article is not written as though the reader has an unnamed local “artifacts directory.”
For this manuscript, the present 100:0 main-text-to-appendix ratio is not itself wrong. It is close to the modal TAMS pattern. The problem is allocation within the main text: approximately two pages of standard stable-law verification and a numerical table have been given main-line status. The best TAMS-shaped choices are either:


keep no appendix and shorten those passages drastically; or


move the full characteristic-function verification to a two-page appendix, producing about a 90:10 main-text-to-appendix ratio.


There should not be a separate substantial supplement. The central Brocot proof, the endpoint correction, and the exact Fibonacci generator identity must remain in the article.
5. Register and signposting
First-person plural is normal: “we prove,” “we now apply,” “we first recall.” Active prose predominates, though passive constructions appear where the actor is irrelevant. Recent TAMS papers are not austere in the sense of forbidding explanation, but the explanation has hierarchy. The principal theorem may receive two pages of discussion; a standard corollary may receive two sentences.
Results are generally referred to by number and role: “the main theorem,” “the rigidity statement,” “the local estimate.” The paper does not repeatedly redescribe every result in full. Nor does it repeatedly reassure the reader that it has not proved something else.
The usual non-fit signals are:


an introduction that resembles a contribution ledger;


a summary theorem followed later by a second, almost identical formal theorem;


managerial headings such as “contribution boundary”;


repeated disclaimers protecting against possible overreading;


every lemma receiving a preamble, proof, and interpretive postscript of similar length;


standard consequences being presented with the rhetorical weight of the novel theorem;


a long conclusion that retells the introduction;


references to external artifacts that are not formally deposited and identified;


two substantial mathematical narratives whose unity is asserted more often than it is felt.


Application to the manuscript
Abstract
The abstract is mathematically informative, but it is too exhaustive. It gives the total-variation theorem, all major context consequences, the scalar asymptotic, the correction to Dushistova, the precise limitation concerning later coefficients, the Fibonacci renewal identity, the stable domain of attraction, and the noninteger correction. Almost every contribution receives equal syntactic weight. 
That is the first machine-assisted tell. The abstract reads as though it was optimized to ensure that no contribution or caveat could be omitted. A human TAMS abstract would choose a hierarchy.
What to do instead: make the condensation theorem the first and longest sentence; give the corrected denominator asymptotic in the second; give the Fibonacci consequence in the third; put the stable law in a subordinate clause or omit it. Remove the sentence disclaiming conclusions about the other coefficients. That limitation belongs once in the introduction and once at the precise corollary.
A better abstract would be about 160–190 words, not because TAMS imposes that length, but because the hierarchy can be expressed in that space.
Section 1, opening pages
The first two pages are strong. The paper identifies the model quickly, explains the one-large-partial-quotient phenomenon, and says why nonmultiplicativity makes the conclusion nontrivial. The transition from the Brocot model to Weinstein’s generators is concrete rather than metaphorical. 
This portion reads like a TAMS paper. I would retain most of it.
The only adjustment is emphasis. The sentence that the scalar consequence becomes input for “a different but closely linked question” is accurate, but it already reveals the architectural difficulty: the paper is announcing a second question rather than an application of the first. Rewrite the transition positively and asymmetrically:

Corollary 2.6 supplies exactly the generator-cost asymptotic needed to obtain a second-order renewal expansion for the classical Fibonacci partition function.

That says what is transferred without rhetorically opening a second paper.
Section 1.1, Main results
The placement is correct. The first condensation theorem begins on page 3; the scalar theorem follows immediately; the Fibonacci result appears by pages 4–5. That is well within TAMS practice. 
The problem is the two-level theorem system. Theorem A is a summary of Theorem 2.1; Theorem B is essentially Corollary 2.6 plus the negative-continuant identification; Theorem C is then repeated in much fuller form as Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.6. The introduction consequently spends several pages giving theorem statements that the body largely states again.
Choose one system:


either state the principal results fully in the introduction and later write “Proof of Theorem A”;


or give short informal descriptions in the introduction and reserve formal statements for Sections 2–4.


The current middle position is the least natural one: the statements are too detailed to be merely informal and too incomplete to replace the later statements.
The prose between Theorems A, B, and C also overmanages the reader. The endpoint convention, the exact overcount, the proof strategy, the fact that only the scalar result transfers, the exact renewal identity, and the standard theorem to be invoked are all explained before the body begins.  A TAMS introduction may certainly explain a proof, but it normally selects the one or two mechanisms that make the theorem possible. Here the introduction previews almost every local step.
Retain:


the endpoint explanation;


the one paragraph on two-context domination;


the exact statement that the scalar layer estimate feeds the Fibonacci application.


Cut or compress:


the three-case coefficient calculation, which is repeated in Lemma 2.3;


the full renewal identity, which is repeated in Proposition 3.1;


the detailed list of hypotheses for the cited renewal and stable theorems.


Section 1.2, “History and contribution boundary”
This is the least TAMS-like heading and register in the paper. The underlying content is useful: Dushistova’s relation must be stated exactly, the distinction from independent-sum condensation matters, and Weinstein’s role must be credited. 
But the prose has the tone of a legal boundary document. It repeatedly says what is and is not used, what is and is not implied, and what is and is not being claimed. The problem is not caution; the problem is that caution has become the organizing principle.
Rename and reorganize this material as two ordinary introduction paragraphs:


Prior work on denominator layers and condensation. State Dushistova’s result, the correction, and why independent one-big-jump theorems do not apply.


Fibonacci generator coordinates. State precisely what is taken from Weinstein and what new consequence follows from the denominator-layer asymptotic.


Delete the paragraph about the companion manuscript. Its nondependence is not part of the mathematical argument and does not belong in the introduction. 
Section 1.3, Organization
The organizational paragraph is unobjectionable, but after the long result-by-result preview it becomes one more summary layer.  Reduce it to two sentences or delete it.
Section 2, Brocot condensation
This is the section that best fits TAMS.
The definitions are economical. The full theorem has the necessary precision. The observation that the analogies with independent heavy-tailed variables do not imply the result is made once, exactly where the reader needs it. 
Proposition 2.2 should remain in the main text. The exact regular-to-negative continued-fraction conversion and the indexing shift are part of the paper’s bridge, not disposable background. Its proof is short enough to justify keeping it.
Lemma 2.3 should also remain in full. When a paper corrects a printed coefficient, the exact source of the discrepancy must be visible. The endpoint calculation and the identification of the lost restriction supply that accountability. 
The remainder of Section 2 has natural mathematical unevenness. Lemma 2.4 is short because it is a device; Proposition 2.5 is longer because it carries the actual tail estimate; the proof of the main theorem becomes short once the estimate is available. That is exactly the right hierarchy. 
My only substantive editorial change in this section would be to remove repeated reminders that no renewal theorem or regular variation has been used. One such statement is useful; several begin to sound defensive.
Section 3, Fibonacci generator renewal
Proposition 3.1 belongs in the main text. The Weinstein classification itself may be cited, but the paper must verify the indexing convention, the four orbit branches, and the layer multiplicities because these determine the exact coefficient. The branch table is useful and appropriately concrete. 
The section becomes overformal after that point.
Corollary 3.2 is a routine generating-function rearrangement. Lemma 3.3 is the standard free-monoid/Cauchy-product interpretation. Then equations (3.15) and (3.16) give algebraic, probabilistic, and pathwise versions of substantially the same renewal identity.  
A human author would normally choose two of these, not all four presentations. I would keep:


Proposition 3.1 in full;


the probability-law normalization;


one compact renewal interpretation.


I would turn Corollary 3.2 into an unnumbered displayed consequence unless it is used later by number, and compress Lemma 3.3 plus the pathwise identity into one remark of perhaps half a page.
Section 4, arithmetic critical law
Lemma 4.2 is central and should remain. It is where the corrected denominator-layer constant becomes the exact cost-tail constant. 
Theorem 4.1, however, packages three results of different status as though they were coequal:


the arithmetic tail, which is genuinely produced by this paper;


the second-order renewal term, which follows from a cited renewal theorem after verification;


the stable domain-of-attraction statement, which is a standard consequence of that tail.


That flatness of emphasis is another machine-assisted tell. 
Lemma 4.3 should be shortened to a specialized statement of the Omey–Van Gulck result followed by the two-line Karamata calculation that fixes the coefficient. The current prose identifying the exact displayed assertion, translating every notation item, and announcing that the coefficient is “not hidden in the citation” is conscientious but overdefensive. 
Lemma 4.5 is the clearest candidate for compression or an appendix. After invoking Feller, the manuscript supplies a full characteristic-function proof, including small- and large-jump estimates.  That is mathematically sound expository work, but it is not where this paper earns its TAMS place. A half-page proof checking the positive and negative tail balances, centering, and normalization is sufficient. A reader who needs the general theorem can consult Feller.
Corollary 4.6 is the correct terminal result of the application and should be made more prominent. It is the statement about the classical Fibonacci partition function, whereas the stable limit is a consequence concerning the auxiliary generator-letter law. 
Remark 4.7 should not appear in its current form. The numerical values may be useful, but the four-row convergence table does not advance the proof, and “the verification script and its unit test are included in the artifacts directory” is not journal-ready bibliographic language.  Either delete the remark, retain only the numerical value of σ0​ in one sentence, or deposit the code in a stable repository and cite it formally. There should be no reliance on an unspecified directory.
Section 5, Conclusion
The conclusion is too long for what it does. It repeats:


the nonmultiplicative nature of the model;


the two-context mechanism;


the corrected coefficient;


the limitation to the leading coefficient;


the generator renewal connection;


the stable-law limitation;


the single-critical-exponent limitation;


the independence of the companion manuscript.


Most of these points have already appeared in the abstract, the main-results subsection, the contribution-boundary subsection, and the body. 
A TAMS-shaped ending would be three short paragraphs, at most one page:


what the total-variation theorem says beyond the scalar asymptotic;


how the denominator-layer constant produces the Fibonacci correction;


one paragraph of genuine open problems.


Delete the companion-manuscript paragraph entirely.
The main-text-to-supplement judgment
The current absence of a supplement is right for TAMS. Do not create a substantial supplement.
The acceptable alternatives are:


Preferred: retain a 100:0 ratio, but shorten Lemmas 4.3 and 4.5 and delete the numerical table;


Also normal: move the full stable characteristic-function verification to a two-page Appendix A, giving approximately 90–93% main text and 7–10% appendix.


Do not move Proposition 2.2, Lemma 2.3, Proposition 2.5, Proposition 3.1, or Lemma 4.2 out of the main article. Those are the mathematical spine.
Does it read machine-assisted?
Yes, in its framing layers. No, not in its best proofs.
The machine-assisted signal is not bad grammar. It is exhaustive self-annotation:


the abstract gives every result and every limitation;


each main theorem is preceded and followed by explanation;


the correction to Dushistova and the limitation to the leading coefficient are repeated in the abstract, introduction, theorem discussion, corollary, and conclusion;


the paper repeatedly uses symmetrical contrasts of the form “not this, but that”;


“The proof is deliberately local,” “the second part transfers only…,” “History and contribution boundary,” and “the arguments are logically autonomous” are all defensible individually, but collectively they make the article sound as though it is continuously documenting its own compliance;


the renewal identity is presented algebraically, combinatorially, probabilistically, and pathwise;


the conclusion mirrors the introduction instead of selecting what remains important.


The strongest examples are the abstract, the paragraphs surrounding Theorems B and C, the whole of §1.2, the closing part of §3, the commentary in Lemma 4.3, Remark 4.7, and the conclusion.     
Section 2 does not have that flatness. It spends a paragraph where a paragraph suffices and several pages where the main estimate requires several pages. That section should be the stylistic model for the rest of the article.

PART TWO — YOU ARE THE REFEREE
Recommendation: Major revisions
Dear Editor,
The manuscript proves a total-variation condensation theorem for denominator-weighted canonical continued fractions of fixed digit sum. It derives the associated denominator-layer asymptotic with a corrected leading coefficient, identifies the endpoint overcount responsible for the coefficient printed in an earlier paper, and applies the scalar asymptotic through Weinstein’s generator coordinates to obtain a critical renewal expansion for the classical Fibonacci partition function. The principal Brocot result is substantial, the endpoint computation is explicit, and the Fibonacci application has an exact combinatorial bridge rather than a merely suggestive analogy.
My recommendation is nevertheless major revision. This recommendation is driven by the architecture and presentation of the paper, not by a new correctness objection.
The article does not yet read as one fully settled TAMS paper. Its first mathematical narrative is the Brocot condensation theorem and the corrected denominator-layer constant. Its second is the Fibonacci generator renewal, including a second-order renewal expansion and a stable domain-of-attraction consequence. The bridge between the two is exact, but it uses only the scalar denominator-layer asymptotic, not the total-variation theorem. The manuscript itself says this explicitly.  The result is that the second half currently reads less as the terminal application of the first half than as a second short paper attached to it.
The authors appear aware of this concern, but they respond by repeatedly explaining the scope and autonomy of the two mechanisms. There is a subsection entitled “History and contribution boundary,” two discussions of the companion manuscript, multiple statements that only the leading coefficient is corrected, and a long conclusion that reasserts what is and is not implied.   This does not cure the architectural problem. It advertises it.
I believe the problem is fixable. The article should be rewritten with an unambiguous hierarchy:


the total-variation condensation theorem is the principal result;


the corrected scalar asymptotic is its key arithmetic output;


the Weinstein identity is the exact bridge;


the critical Fibonacci expansion is the principal application;


the stable domain-of-attraction statement is an ancillary standard consequence of the tail.


At present, the introduction and Theorem 4.1 give these components too nearly equal rhetorical weight.
The introduction is not too long by TAMS standards, and the main theorem appears early enough. The issue is duplication. Theorem A is followed later by Theorem 2.1; Theorem B is repeated as Corollary 2.6; Theorem C is expanded into Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.6. Between these statements the manuscript previews endpoint calculations, proof decompositions, renewal hypotheses, and stable-law normalization that will all be presented again. The authors should retain either full introductory statements or full body statements, not both at nearly the same level of detail.
Section 2 is the strongest part of the paper editorially as well as mathematically. The proof structure is economical, the endpoint correction is located where it belongs, and routine arguments are subordinate to Proposition 2.5. I would make only local changes there.
Section 3 should be shortened after Proposition 3.1. The formal generating-function corollary, the free-word coefficient lemma, the probabilistic renewal representation, and the pathwise representation are four versions of essentially the same mechanism. Two versions suffice.
Section 4 requires more substantial compression. Lemma 4.2 is central. The complete proofs of the general renewal and stable-law inputs are not. Lemma 4.3 can be reduced to the precise cited theorem and the calculation fixing its constant. Lemma 4.5 can be replaced by a citation to the standard domain-of-attraction theorem followed by verification of the tail balance, centering, and normalization. The present characteristic-function proof gives a standard consequence approximately the same visual weight as the new arithmetic tail theorem.
Remark 4.7 should be deleted or rewritten. A numerical sanity check is not objectionable, but a table of truncations and a reference to an unspecified “artifacts directory” are not appropriate in the final journal article. 
Finally, the conclusion should be cut to no more than one page, and all discussion of the companion manuscript should be removed. The article should end with its own theorem rather than with an explanation of its relation to another submission.
The single objection most likely to sink the paper is the unresolved one-paper-versus-two-papers architecture. A referee may conclude that the Brocot theorem is the paper and that the Fibonacci material is an extended application package based only on one scalar corollary. The repeated boundary language makes that conclusion more, not less, salient.
The objection is fixable without new mathematics. The authors must make the Brocot-to-Fibonacci hierarchy visible in the theorem organization, page allocation, title, abstract, and introduction, and must demote the standard stable-law material. Subject to that substantial rewrite, I would be prepared to recommend acceptance.
Recommendation: major revisions.
