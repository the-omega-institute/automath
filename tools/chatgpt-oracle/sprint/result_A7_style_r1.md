PART ONE — THE HOUSE STYLE, LEARNED FROM ACTUAL RECENT PAPERS
1. What recent Fibonacci Quarterly papers actually look like
I based this on a working sample from the 2022–2024 open archive, with checks against the longer papers appearing after the journal’s publishing transition in 2025–2026. The 2024 volume is especially useful because complete contents and PDFs are readily inspectable. By my count from the four 2024 contents pages, the volume contained 27 research articles, with a median length of about 10 pages and a range of roughly 5–18 pages. fq.math+3fq.math+3fq.math+3 There are now genuine long-form exceptions: the 2025 Markoff–Fibonacci classification runs 25 pages, and a 2026 paper on sums of Pell-type sequences runs 33 pages. Thus 33 pages is exceptional but not, by itself, disqualifying. 技术研究所+2Taylor & Francis Online+2
The important point is that even the longer papers read as one paper. Their length is generated by the proof of one central result or one tightly integrated classification, not by several adjacent research programs sharing a cover.
How a typical paper opens
The normal opening is:


a short abstract stating the result;


immediate definition of the object;


one small example or a few sentences of prior context;


the main result on page 1 or 2.


For example, the recent Kaprekar-classification paper has a three-sentence abstract, begins immediately with the definition, gives 6174 as the motivating example, and states the purpose of the paper on the first page. fq.math The Schreier-set paper states its principal identity in the abstract, introduces the preceding counting problem directly, gives a small numerical table, and presents Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 on page 2. fq.math+1 The 13-page arithmetic paper by Luca and Manape spends somewhat longer setting up the class of recurrence sequences, but still presents its main theorem at the start of page 2 and begins preliminaries on page 3. fq.math
There is no rigid rule that the theorem must be literally on the first page. But there is a clear house expectation that the reader should know, by the end of page 2, exactly what has been proved.
A normal introduction is therefore around one to two pages, occasionally three in a technically broader arithmetic paper. As a proportion, approximately 10–20% of the article is normal. A four-page introduction in a 33-page article is not automatically excessive, but it must be doing introduction work. Four pages of theorem previews, novelty auditing, failed-program narration, and literature exclusions feels much longer than four pages.
Expository prose versus statement and proof
The journal is proof-dense. Expository prose is concentrated in three places:


the introduction;


one paragraph at the beginning of a section;


a short sentence immediately before a theorem, lemma, or example explaining why it is needed.


Inside proofs, the prose is directional rather than discursive: “we first show,” “applying Lemma 2,” “the converse follows,” “summing gives.” Remarks after proofs normally explain an exception, an immediate consequence, or a connection with a known identity.
What is not customary is to supply a mini-essay before every formal statement, then another paragraph after it explaining what has and has not been claimed, then repeat the same boundary in a later overview. Recent papers trust the theorem statement and proof to carry much of the emphasis.
How proofs are written
A short proof that invokes a cited theorem is entirely acceptable when the cited result is standard and the new point lies elsewhere. Luca and Manape, for example, state standard estimates as lemmas with direct references, while proving the auxiliary estimate that is specific to their argument. fq.math
The distinction is this:


Imported infrastructure may be cited and summarized.


The paper’s central reduction or classification should be proved in the paper.


Routine algebraic or inductive identities may be compressed or placed in a short appendix.


The Schreier paper is a useful model. Its final theorem is dispatched in a few lines from a previous theorem and an identity; the elementary proof of that identity is then put in an appendix beginning late on page 9 of an 11-page paper. fq.math+1 That is the journal’s usual notion of relegated material: a small supporting verification, not a second article.
Main text versus appendices and supplements
In the representative recent papers I checked, appendices were usually absent. The Kaprekar and Luca–Manape papers have no appendix; the Schreier paper uses approximately a page and a half for elementary identities after the main argument. fq.math+2fq.math+2
My practical estimate of the customary ratio is:


0% is most common;


5–15% is ordinary where an appendix is useful;


around 20% is still unobjectionable if it consists of computations, tables, or routine lemmas;


a supplement equal to or longer than the article is highly atypical.


That is a statement about custom, not permission. The journal may technically accommodate supplementary files. But a 33-page article accompanied by 36 pages of mathematical supplement reads, in this venue, as either two papers or as a paper whose editorial hierarchy has not yet been settled.
Register and signposting
The normal register is active first-person plural:


“We prove…”


“We define…”


“We now apply…”


“The following theorem gives…”


Impersonal constructions occur, but they do not dominate.
Results are referred to by number. Motivation is usually stated once. An organization paragraph, when present, is generally one compact paragraph or sentence. A result is not normally followed everywhere by a disclaimer cataloguing all stronger things it does not prove.
The prose is accessible but mathematically adult. The journal does not demand elementary proofs of every familiar Fibonacci fact. It does value concrete examples, explicit recurrences, numerical layers, and visible connections to Fibonacci or Lucas arithmetic.
Tells that a paper does not fit the register
The common warning signs are:


an abstract that functions as a miniature referee response;


a long priority-and-novelty audit before the paper begins;


several formally independent “applications,” each with its own literature;


a main theorem that appears first as a preview and only much later as the actual theorem;


extensive discussion of a stronger theorem that the paper does not prove;


repeated statements of what is “not claimed”;


reliance on a large supplement for parts of the conceptual story;


imported terminology from several neighboring fields when simpler Fibonacci-language descriptions would suffice;


transitions that describe manuscript architecture rather than advance the mathematical argument.


Your manuscript has several of these tells.

3. The main-text-to-supplement proportion
The present 33 pages plus 36 pages is not right for this journal’s customary presentation.
The answer is not simply to transfer another ten pages from the main article into the supplement. That would make the visible article shorter while leaving the underlying editorial problem untouched. The required movement is in three directions.
Material that should remain in the main article
The main article must be self-contained for:


the definition of witness covers;


the bijection with Mn​;


Fibonacci prime-power lifting and the prime–ladder dichotomy;


the rank-pure construction;


the squarefree exact-fiber identification;


the weighted cover formula;


one principal support-entropy theorem.


No proof essential to those statements should be available only in the supplement.
Material that may properly remain supplemental
Based on the main article’s description, these are appropriate supplementary items:


exhaustive classifications for ω(n)≤4;


finite computations and verification tables;


full exact-support spectra;


long case lists;


secondary refinements of an asymptotic already proved in the article;


routine labelled-cover calculations.


Those are the kinds of things a reader may reasonably consult after understanding the article.
Material that should become separate papers, not supplement
The following should not be hidden in a supplement and should not remain in this article:


the large primitive-divisor/Wall–Sun–Sun theorem and its fibotomic machinery;


the prime inverse-ray application;


any substantial connected-factorization theory that has its own independent theorem sequence;


a large secondary asymptotic theory requiring several sections.


After restructuring, I would expect something like:


main article: 18–22 pages;


technical appendix or supplement: 6–12 pages;


primitive-divisor paper: separate;


inverse-ray note: separate.


A 22-page article with a 10-page supplement would still be on the long side for the journal but entirely defensible. A 33-page article with a 36-page supplement is not customary.

4. Does it read as machine-assisted?
Yes, in substantial passages it does.
The mathematics does not read generic or fabricated. The machine-assisted quality lies in the editorial behavior: the prose appears optimized to answer every conceivable objection, preserve every distinction, and give every result a formally symmetrical place.
The clearest signs are:
Exhaustive abstracting
The abstract refuses to choose. Almost every theorem, limitation, open interface, literature boundary, and auxiliary application appears there.  A human author writing to this journal would suppress most of that information and allow the paper’s hierarchy to emerge through omission.
Throat-clearing before statements
Many sections open by explaining whether their contents are classical, structural, Fibonacci-specific, independent advances, or not independent advances. Section 3 begins this way; the fibotomic subsection does it again; Section 7 explicitly disclaims new input before presenting the application.   
Some of these qualifications are useful once. Their repetition is machine-like.
Symmetrical caveat structures
The manuscript repeatedly uses balanced formulations of the form:


necessary, but not sufficient;


exact for one sector, but not the full fiber;


a parameter for the bound, but not an information-theoretically complete invariant;


one consequence is proved, but no optimality is asserted;


one conjecture would imply this, but no positive evidence is known.


Each distinction has content. The cumulative effect is unnaturally even. Nothing is allowed to stand without an adjacent qualification.
Transitions that announce structure
Phrases such as “The reduction to a weighted arithmetic problem,” “Unconditional bounds and the failed step,” “We now apply,” “The following sharp extremal calculation separates,” and “Exact residual arithmetic interface” continually label the manuscript’s architecture. The labels are more systematic than the mathematical narrative needs.
Flatness of emphasis
The classification theorem, the squarefree identification, the abstract extremal weight theorem, the fibotomic entropy theorem, the Wall–Sun–Sun alternative, and the inverse-ray corollaries all receive named theorem treatment and introductory preview space. The paper does not choose which of these is the reason it exists.
A human mathematician is uneven on purpose. Here the decisive classification should receive thirty pages of consequences and development; the inverse-ray observation should receive either half a page or its own note. Instead, both are fully packaged.
Residue of the review process
“The requested almost-all equivalent,” “the proposed tier-up theorem,” “the theorem attempt,” and the catalog of database searches do not belong to the voice of a finished paper. They are traces of an external evaluation process.   
My blunt assessment is therefore:

The manuscript reads as heavily machine-assisted or heavily audit-assisted, not because its sentences are grammatically unnatural, but because it is over-complete, over-signposted, and unwilling to let any qualification remain implicit. The cure is not cosmetic variation of sentence length. The cure is deletion and hierarchy.


PART TWO — YOU ARE THE REFEREE
Recommendation: Reject and invite resubmission after substantial restructuring.
To the Editor:
This manuscript studies divisibility-minimal elements in the exact fibers of the Fibonacci rank-of-apparition map. Its principal structural result is a bijection between these minimal preimages and irredundant collections of rank-jump prime powers satisfying a coordinate witness condition. It then develops the Fibonacci prime-power lifting needed to distinguish prime jumps from ladder jumps, identifies a canonical rank-pure sector, and proves that on squarefree indices this sector is exactly the squarefree part of the minimal exact fiber. The resulting weighted cover formula is a natural and potentially useful description of the arithmetic object.
In my view, this core material is suitable in subject and level for The Fibonacci Quarterly. The worked examples are effective, and the prime–ladder distinction is presented clearly. I do not base my recommendation on an alleged mathematical error.
I nevertheless do not recommend publication of the submitted manuscript in its present form. The principal difficulty is not length alone. The journal now occasionally publishes long articles. The difficulty is that the submission does not read as one long article. It reads as several papers assembled around a common rank-of-apparition vocabulary, accompanied by a supplement longer than the usual complete article.
The witness-cover classification, Fibonacci lifting theory, squarefree exact-fiber theorem, and weighted cover count form one coherent paper. The fibotomic exact-rank estimates and the large primitive-divisor/Fibonacci–Wieferich alternative form a second. The prime inverse-ray application forms a third, short paper. The manuscript itself acknowledges that the inverse-ray result uses none of the witness-cover or rank-pure machinery.  The large primitive-divisor theorem likewise has its own classical input, proof mechanism, comparison literature, and conditional consequence. It does not function merely as a lemma in the classification paper.
This over-assembly has substantial effects on the exposition. The abstract is nearly two pages and reports not only the principal theorems but also a failed almost-all program, necessary and sufficient interfaces, limitations of cumulative arithmetic data, a Wall–Sun–Sun alternative, applications to rank dynamics, and the contents of the supplement. The introduction continues for four pages and contains two “Preview Theorems,” a lengthy novelty audit, a discussion of a theorem not proved, and even a record of database searches. The actual classification theorem does not appear formally until Theorem 3.7.
The paper also repeatedly explains what it does not claim. Some such qualifications are important, particularly around exact-rank multiplicities and the proposed almost-all asymptotic. In the present version, however, they occur in the abstract, introduction, several theorem discussions, a long residual-interface remark, and the final open-problem section. The resulting prose is defensive and flat in emphasis. It resembles a comprehensive research dossier more than a finished journal article.
The supplement raises a separate editorial concern. According to the main text, it contains connected factorizations, exact support spectra, complete classifications for low support, secondary asymptotics, and finite checks. Some of those topics sound appropriately supplementary, but others sound like independent structural results. A 36-page supplement attached to a 33-page main article is far outside the usual balance of this journal. I have not been given the supplement itself, so I cannot determine whether all main-text dependencies are adequately self-contained.
I believe the manuscript is fixable, but not by ordinary line editing. I would invite a new submission centered solely on the following chain:


witness-cover classification of Mn​;


Fibonacci prime-power realization and prime–ladder dichotomy;


canonical squarefree/rank-pure sector;


exact weighted cover count;


a streamlined support-entropy bound and concise open arithmetic question.


The main theorem should be stated formally in the introduction. The abstract should be reduced to the classification, squarefree identification, and one quantitative consequence. Most of the repeated novelty and nonclaim language should be removed. The large primitive-divisor theorem and the inverse-ray application should be submitted separately. The supplement should be reduced to computations, low-support classifications, and technical proofs not needed for the logical self-containment of the main article.
The single objection most likely to prevent acceptance is therefore the absence of one editorial center. The submission asks the reader and editor to accept a 69-page research package containing several separable theorem blocks. That objection is fixable, and the repair does not appear to require new mathematics. It does, however, require a genuine split and a substantial rewrite, which is why I recommend rejection with an invitation to resubmit rather than major revision of the present submission.
